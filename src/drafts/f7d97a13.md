In 1895, the Lumière brothers pointed a camera at a train. In 2026, we ask a large language model to write a Beatles song. Both machines work, yet both assignments come from an older form.

A large language model, or LLM, learns from huge amounts of data and predicts what should come next. That makes imitation easy while telling us little about what people will create after the copy phase.

I tried to see AI slop as a quality problem. Jack Conte's SXSW talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc), gave me a better frame. I think we are watching a young medium get the wrong assignments.

<div class="article-tldr">
<span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span>
<p>AI slop is what a young medium produces when we use it to copy work people already do better. The useful response is to protect artists through the transition while building experiences that require memory, interaction, and a human choice the model cannot erase.</p>
</div>

## The camera worked before cinema did

Maxim Gorky saw a functioning machine and came away disappointed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Early film looked thin because filmmakers aimed the camera at theater. The creative leap came when people used cuts and camera effects that a stage could never provide.</p></div>

After a Lumière screening in 1896, Gorky described "a world without sound, without colour" where everything was dipped in monotonous gray. I think the review was fair. A camera could record motion, but early filmmakers still treated it like a good seat in a theater.

Georges Méliès changed that in 1902 with *A Trip to the Moon*. Cuts made bodies vanish and carried the story from Earth to the Moon. The machine could shape time as well as record it.

Kubrick took that idea much further in *2001: A Space Odyssey*. A bone rises into the air, then one cut lands us beside a spacecraft. Millions of years pass in a second, an effect that depends on film.

Recorded music had its own rough beginning. In the 1930s, the American Federation of Musicians ran ads against "canned music." By 1930, an estimated 22,000 moviehouse musicians had lost their jobs as sound films replaced live accompaniment. The union began a commercial recording ban in 1942 that lasted more than two years.

Then the studio became part of the instrument. The Beatles built *Revolver* in 1966 with tape loops and reversed guitar. Conte quotes George Martin describing the studio as "a new canvas on which they had to paint." Charlie Chaplin made a similar turn with *The Great Dictator* in 1940, using the human voice for the film's final plea after years of resisting talkies.

The Hammond Novachord adds another piece. Unveiled in 1939, the first commercial polyphonic synthesizer used 163 vacuum tubes and weighed almost 500 pounds. Musicians heard a labor threat. Later artists heard new sounds.

The economic pain remains real. Baumol and Bowen named the cost disease of the performing arts in 1966: a Haydn symphony still takes about 25 minutes while costs rise. A [Stanford GSB analysis](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) found that 46 of the 63 largest US orchestras ran deficits in 2008.

## Slop copies the song and leaves the risk behind

Keep one example in mind: an AI system asked to make a new Beatles track.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A fake Beatles song copies the surface of work that once involved public risk. Better generation can polish the sound, but the model has no reputation tied to the result.</p></div>

Conte calls slop "that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium where we're all just trying to reproduce existing things that are honestly just better if the people do them." The fake Beatles track fits. It may sound convincing, yet every design choice points backward.

Pretraining creates most of that pull. Familiar music has a wide road through the data. Preference tuning then uses human ratings to favor outputs people accept. The first force rewards likelihood. The second narrows the acceptable range. I think the result can surprise us while still orbiting what listeners know.

AlphaGo's Move 37 against Lee Sedol in 2016 is a strong counterexample. A trained system found a move that professional players would not have chosen. Games also created responsive worlds long before LLMs. They have rules with clear feedback. A song released into culture has no score function that returns `1` for the next Beatles and `0` for a polished tribute band.

Risk changes the work too. *Sing Street* gives the older brother a blunt line: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." A model can generate a strange chorus without losing friends or future work. The person who signs the song carries that exposure.

One line Conte quotes ties art to scarcity and to what is hard to make. I read "hard" as the part that costs a person something. Kendrick Lamar's *good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries weight because Kendrick lived the story.

I think this is why slop feels empty even when the craft looks competent. The output copied the song, while the risk stayed with the people whose work trained the machine.

## Build a song that remembers

Our fake Beatles track becomes more interesting when it can develop a history with one listener.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Give the song memory and let listening change what happens next. Keep one composer's rule outside the model's control so adaptation cannot wash away every difficult choice.</p></div>

Imagine a song that changes over several months. It remembers which motif a listener replayed. A human composer sets one rule: every version must contain one full bar of silence.

The shared state can stay small:

```python
from typing import TypedDict

class SongState(TypedDict):
    listener_id: str
    motifs_heard: list[str]
    last_feedback: str
    tension: float
    required_silence_bars: int
```

A `TypedDict` is a Python description of a dictionary with named fields. It lets an editor or type checker catch a missing field before the system runs.

LangGraph is a Python library for LLM workflows. Its `StateGraph` connects steps that share state. One step reads listening history. Another composes. A validator checks the silence rule.

ReAct is a loop where a model chooses an action, observes the result, then chooses again. Suppose the model notices that shorter tracks get more completions. It tries to change `required_silence_bars` from `1` to `0`. The validator rejects that update, and the `StateGraph` sends the original rule back into the compose step.

That rejection separates personalization from surrender. The model can learn from the listener without optimizing away the choice that gives the work tension. This is why [the spec is the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/): a written boundary preserves judgment across model calls.

I think this is a better project for a junior AI engineer than another chat screen. A model API plus a small database is enough for a first version. You can hear whether the system is developing a relationship with the listener, and you can test whether the silence rule survives.

## Use the imitation test on Monday

An AI pilot can save real time even when it copies an existing workflow.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Review your AI work and label the projects that reproduce an old workflow. Keep the useful ones, then fund one experiment whose value depends on the machine remembering what happened before.</p></div>

Take the pilot list from your team. Email summaries and generated slides belong in the imitation phase. They may be worth shipping, with success measured through time saved.

Now look for one project with no useful static version. Our evolving song qualifies because listening changes the next composition while the human rule stays fixed. An enterprise version could remember earlier exceptions while an accountable owner locks the policy fields. If removing memory leaves another generated document, the project is still filming the stage.

Conte describes Pomplamoose's career as an infinite sine wave. YouTube changed its algorithm. Streaming changed the economics. COVID stopped touring. The band rebuilt each time. I don't know whether AI follows the same path as cinema or recorded music. Dead media exist, and history does not promise a happy ending.

I am willing to make a narrower bet. By 2030, the AI work people care about most will remember prior contact and still carry a visible human constraint. If the celebrated result is a cleaner fake Beatles song, I was wrong.

Once someone succeeds, models will train on the result and the [copy cycle will start again](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). I think junior engineers should expect that. The job is to keep exploring anyway, with enough respect for the people paying for the transition.
