# AI is still looking for its first cut

In 1895, the Lumière brothers’ [Cinématographe](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere), a machine that recorded and projected moving pictures, began attracting paying audiences in Paris. The images moved, but film’s artistic language had barely begun. After a programme in 1896, Russian writer Maxim Gorky described [“a world without sound, without colour”](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/91ddc4e0-5576-4f98-a3b6-ba8a97703eda/section/2fe1a387-c815-45d7-9609-2a47615dc9e8), with everything “dipped in monotonous grey.” He was seeing a medium before artists knew how to speak through it.

In 2026, we prompt a large language model, software that predicts and produces text, to write a poem, or ask an image model to imitate a drawing. Then we treat the copy as evidence that AI-generated creative work has reached its final form. I think we are making the 1895 mistake.

Jack Conte, a musician in the duo Pomplamoose and co-founder of Patreon, a membership platform for creators, calls slop [“that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium”](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=844), when people reproduce things humans already make better. My take is that slop is a symptom of immaturity. It gives us a reason to explore, provided we stay honest about what would count as progress and who pays while we search.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI slop resembles a medium’s imitation-heavy phase. History permits hope, but artists still have to discover what depends on AI itself.</p></div>

## New media start with borrowed forms

Conte describes early film as borrowing theatre’s logic. The camera took the audience’s seat while actors performed inside a fixed frame. In his account, Georges Méliès, a French stage magician who became a filmmaker, changed that logic by stopping the camera, altering the scene and restarting it. Joined together, the moments made an impossible event appear continuous.

By 1902, Méliès’s [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) used cuts and visual tricks to bend sequence, place and time. A cut joins separately recorded shots, allowing film to do something a stage performance could not. I think that is the pattern worth carrying forward: new machinery preserves an old form until an artist discovers a move that belongs to the machinery.

Recorded sound offers two variations on that pattern. The Beatles, the British rock group, used tape loops and backwards recordings on their 1966 album [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver), turning studio equipment into part of the composition. Charlie Chaplin, the actor-director identified with silent film, chose a different path: after resisting synchronized dialogue, he made recorded speech central to 1940’s [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/7-The-Great-Dictator). One case discovered a compositional tool; the other shows an established artist deciding when a new tool finally belonged in the work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film found its language through the cut, while recorded sound became expressive through studio manipulation and deliberate voice. The breakthrough came when artists stopped treating the machine as storage.</p></div>

## Slop has a real economic cost

Conte’s definition describes a behaviour. Asking AI for a hand-drawn or camera-shot look keeps the old form and changes the producer. The result may be polished, but that says little about whether the medium has found a creative possibility of its own.

The imitation phase can still hurt people. When synchronized-sound films, known as talkies, replaced live musical accompaniment in cinemas from 1927, the [American Federation of Musicians](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/) says about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs. In 1939, its [rules](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) said that adding a Novachord, an early electronic keyboard able to play several notes at once, or a Hammond organ could not reduce an ensemble’s headcount.

Their fear had a clear mechanism. Economists William Baumol and William Bowen called it performing-arts [“cost disease”](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) in 1966: live performance keeps much of its human time as recorded or synthetic alternatives become cheaper to reproduce. I think hope becomes dishonest when creators absorb that gap. If their work supplies an AI system, they should know the terms, receive promised payment and have a specific publisher or platform employee who investigates disputes and removes output when the right to use its source material cannot be shown.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A medium can produce new art while its economics displace working artists. Exploration deserves support only when the people supplying the source work have clear terms, payment and a practical remedy for misuse.</p></div>

## The artist supplies the risk

Conte asks whether a music model trained only on recordings through 1959 could arrive at the Beatles’ studio experiments, the funk-rock experiments of American singer Prince, or the abrasive grunge of the band Nirvana. I don’t know. The question exposes a missing variable: a model can search patterns, while a person chooses which rule to break and risks ridicule or failure, perhaps staking a career on that choice.

Human connection also changes how work matters. Conte points to [*good kid, m.A.A.d city*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3319), the 2012 album in which American rapper Kendrick Lamar tells a story shaped by his adolescence in Compton. Conte values Kendrick’s life and pain, rather than sound alone. An audience may value the person who chose and answered for what was made.

That leaves two separate standards. Audience response tells us whether AI enables an experience that a fixed human-made work cannot provide. Licences, payment records and an enforceable dispute process tell us whether the transition treats creators fairly. By 2030, widely valued AI work should satisfy both. If removing AI changes only production speed or cost, the 1895 analogy has failed.

Using AI only to fake an illustration is like using the holodeck in *Star Trek*, the fictional room that builds a responsive world around its visitor, to display a framed poster. I think artists should explore that responsive world. AI will come of age when an artist finds its cut, making an experience that depends on the machine and matters because a human being chose to take the risk.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Creative AI needs evidence beyond fluent output: a valued effect that disappears without the system, a human author willing to carry the consequences, and creator terms that can be enforced. By 2030, cheaper imitation alone should count as failure.</p></div>
