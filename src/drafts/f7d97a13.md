I tried to treat AI slop as a quality problem. Better models and sharper prompts seemed like the cure. Then Jack Conte put a year on it: 1895.

In 1895, the Lumière brothers patented the Cinématographe and projected a film of workers leaving a factory. In 2026, we ask a large language model for a lost Beatles song. Both moves point a new machine at a finished human form.

Conte is a musician and [Patreon co-founder](https://www.patreon.com/about). His 58-minute July 2026 SXSW talk is called [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc). He treats the creative pull and the economic damage as parts of the same problem. I think that conflict is a better starting point than either AI hype or disgust.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI slop may be the imitation phase of a young medium. The useful response is to protect artists while testing which creative jobs only generation can perform.</p></div>

## Every new machine borrows an old form

In Conte's examples, early film looked like recorded theater.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film first borrowed the stage, and recordings first tried to preserve live performance. Their grammar appeared through specific craft decisions that the older medium could never hold.</p></div>

The Lumière brothers held public Cinématographe screenings in [1895](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere). A year later, Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière program. [Conte quotes him](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=284) describing a world without sound or color, dipped in monotonous gray. The camera captured motion, while the staging still came from theater.

Georges Méliès bent that frame with camera tricks and editing in the 1902 [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/). Decades later, *2001: A Space Odyssey* cut from a thrown bone to an object in orbit. Millions of years passed inside one edit. That cut shows film doing work no stage change could match.

Recorded music carried a harsher cost. The American Federation of Musicians attacked recordings as ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). Its own history says talkies cost about 22,000 theater musicians their jobs. The union began a recording strike in 1942.

The 1939 Hammond Novachord brought the argument inside the instrument. Union [rules](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) kept an orchestra from cutting players when it added the machine. Solo use in a venue that had employed musicians required local union consent. Baumol and Bowen later named the [cost disease of the performing arts](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease). A quartet still needs four people and the same playing time as productivity rises elsewhere.

Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then made the 1940 [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/009e372a-646d-5b6e-a6b0-59f96722a1fd/the-great-dictator). The Beatles used tape loops and reversed sounds on the 1966 [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver). Speech changed Chaplin's reach. The studio entered the composition itself.

I think the useful pattern sits in the craft decisions. Film found the cut. Recorded music found sounds that could exist only in the studio.

## Slop copies the finished artifact

Our lost Beatles song asks a model to imitate a cultural result from sixty years ago.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Resemblance produces slop because the target already has a human original. Creative risk enters when a person sets a rule, releases the choice under their name, and accepts the response.</p></div>

Conte [defines slop](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=841) as the lazy phase after a new medium appears, when people use it to reproduce things humans already make better. A fake hand drawing and a fake camera shot inherit the limits of the forms they copy.

He offers a thought experiment. Train a music model only on recordings made before 1959, then ask whether it would discover the Beatles, Prince, Beyoncé or Jacob Collier. Conte presents this as a philosophical test without a published benchmark. Someone still has to make the odd choice and take responsibility for it.

Conte quotes *Sing Street*: ["Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed."](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3006) He also credits Karen X Cheng with the line ["Art is scarcity"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3090). The model risks no reputation when the fake Beatles song fails. The person who signs the work does.

Conte says [*good kid, m.A.A.d city* matters because Kendrick Lamar made it](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3287). The sound carries a life behind it.

Holly Herndon's 2019 album [*PROTO*](https://hollyherndon.bandcamp.com/album/proto) used another approach. Her team developed Spawn through live training ceremonies in Berlin, where people taught the system with group singing. Traces of those sessions can be heard on the album.

I think a model judged by Beatles resemblance will keep producing slop, even when the audio is flawless. That is also why [familiar model output keeps returning](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). The song can succeed only by sounding like a past that already belongs to someone else.

## Give generation a necessary job

Keep the song, then change the assignment.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A generative system earns its place when fixed tracks or authored branches cannot produce the same listener experience. State alone is ordinary software; the model has to compose a valid new response under a human rule.</p></div>

Imagine a listener who opts in, hears sixteen bars, and chooses one motif to carry forward. The next version must use that motif. A human composer owns one stubborn rule: every version contains a full silent measure. The model may move the silence, while removal triggers rejection.

Games have saved state for decades. Recommendation systems remember taste. Procedural music existed long before LLMs. Memory by itself proves very little.

Use a medium-dependent test: replace the model with a static track, a database or a rules engine. If the listener gets the same experience, generation has no necessary job.

Start with a JSON score containing notes, rests and bar lengths. In 4/4 time, code checks for one measure with four beats of rest. A score that passes the validator goes to an audio renderer. A failed trace can read `silent_beats: 0 → reject → retry_count: 1`; another zero sends the score to human review.

In Python, a `TypedDict` describes expected state fields such as consent, motif, score and retry count. It helps a type checker catch missing keys, while storage and runtime validation still require code.

[LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/graph-api) is a Python library for workflows that branch or repeat. Its `StateGraph` connects the model, validator and review steps while passing shared state. A [checkpointer and store](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/persistence) can resume one listener thread and retain an approved motif across sessions, with deletion tied to consent.

ReAct would be the wrong label for this score check. [ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) alternates model reasoning with tool actions and feeds observations back into the next decision. This song uses a bounded validation loop.

I think this is enough for a serious two-week experiment: one composer as sponsor, one engineer as owner, twenty opt-in sessions and a €500 inference ceiling. Compare it with an eight-branch authored version. Stop when the branches cover every choice observed in the pilot or the model keeps breaking the silent-measure rule. Continue only when generation produces valid paths the baseline cannot hold. Write those limits before the first model call, the same discipline as [writing the spec before delegation](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/).

## Build before the grammar settles

Conte describes Pomplamoose's career as an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390).

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Artists have rebuilt around repeated platform changes, and AI offers another hard turn without a guaranteed outcome. Junior engineers can make the argument testable through small experiments with clear ownership.</p></div>

YouTube changed how the band was discovered. Streaming weakened its iTunes model. COVID changed how the musicians recorded together. Vertical video forced another production setup. Change kept arriving after the team had learned the previous game.

I don't know whether generative AI will find a lasting creative grammar. History cannot promise that. Dead media disappeared too.

So make the claim falsifiable. By 2030, we should be able to point to a work that collapses when a template or fixed branch replaces the model. Human authorship should remain obvious. If every celebrated example still works without generation, the 1895 frame failed.

I think junior AI engineers are unusually well placed to run that test. You know enough about model limits to build the guardrails, and you are early enough to ignore the habits formed around fixed media.

The camera already works. Our job is to discover the cut.
