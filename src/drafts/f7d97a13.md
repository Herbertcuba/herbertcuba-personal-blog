In 1895, the Lumière brothers filmed workers leaving their factory. The camera stayed still. People crossed the frame as if the lens were a seat in a theatre.

In 2026, we prompt a large language model to write a poem. The model predicts likely next words from patterns in its training data, and the result looks like something a person might have written.

I think we are making the same early mistake. We judge a new medium by how well it copies an object built for an older one.

Jack Conte has skin in this argument as a Pomplamoose musician and Patreon co-founder. In his July 2026 talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=844), he calls slop the lazy phase when a new medium reproduces things humans already make better.

That framing leaves the present damage in view. A transition can still take somebody's income. It also gives us a more useful diagnosis than treating today's feed as AI's finished creative form.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI slop resembles early cinema because both began by copying an older creative form. The phase can be temporary and still cause immediate harm to working creators.</p></div>

## Film had to learn what a cut was

Early film looked like theatre because its makers had no film language to borrow from.

Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière programme in 1896 and saw ["a world without sound, without colour"](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/91ddc4e0-5576-4f98-a3b6-ba8a97703eda/section/2fe1a387-c815-45d7-9609-2a47615dc9e8), with everything "dipped in monotonous grey." Moving images had flattened much of the life he knew. I think his disappointment sounds familiar now: the machine was impressive and the copy felt thin.

Stage magician Georges Méliès filmed an empty box, stopped the camera, placed a person inside, and restarted. Joining the strips made the person appear. By 1902, [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) used that stop-substitution effect to show transformations a theatre could only suggest.

Decades later, *2001: A Space Odyssey* used the same grammar at full power. An early human throws a bone into the air after discovering it can be a tool. Kubrick cuts to a similarly shaped spacecraft. The [BFI calls it cinema's most famous match cut](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/cefccdb2-b558-5623-9c17-72b4be7bd4c1/2001-a-space-odyssey). Millennia pass in one edit.

Most AI creativity still has its camera in the tenth row. When a model writes in another writer's style, the result mainly shows how well it can reproduce familiar features. I want to see generation change the reader's experience in a way fixed text cannot.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film grew when filmmakers made the join between images part of the art. AI remains early while imitation is its main proof of creative value.</p></div>

## The transition sends a bill to working artists

For theatre musicians, the lost paychecks arrived before the new artistic uses.

The American Federation of Musicians called recorded accompaniment ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). Its history says about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs when talking pictures arrived in 1927. The union formed the Music Defense League in 1930.

The Hammond Novachord caused another fight in 1939. AFM [rules published that May](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) said the early synthesizer could not reduce orchestra headcount. Solo use where musicians had worked required local union approval.

William Baumol and William Bowen later called this the [cost disease of the performing arts](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease). A string quartet still needs four players and roughly the same time while the rest of the economy gets faster.

But recorded sound also became a creative material. Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then used speech for the closing address in 1940's [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/1-The-Great-Dictator). The Beatles used tape loops and backwards sound on 1966's [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver), making music that depended on the studio.

Both parts belong in the same history. Exploration produced new art, and working musicians paid before those forms were clear. An AI prototype should begin with licensed material and a named creator who controls release.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Recorded sound removed jobs before artists found some of its strongest creative uses. AI experimentation earns trust only when the people supplying the work agree to the terms and keep release authority.</p></div>

## Prediction carries no reputation

Conte tests the limits of pattern learning with a simple thought experiment.

Train a music model only on recordings made through 1959. Then ask it to produce the break that led to the Beatles or Beyoncé. Conte raises this [47 minutes into the talk](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2828). The model searches learned patterns. The artist puts a name behind an unproven choice.

I don't know how far future models will push beyond their training patterns. Their combinations can surprise me. The model cannot be embarrassed at a show or lose an audience's trust. The person releasing the work carries those consequences.

That person changes how we hear the output. Conte says Kendrick Lamar's *good kid, m.A.A.d city* means more because Kendrick's history and pain enter the listening experience [near the end of the talk](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3317). He [hates the word "content"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3272) because it flattens that bond into one more file.

I think this is why generated culture can feel like what I have called [elevator music](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/), even when each file sounds polished. The listener gets a polished file with no person's history attached.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Models can recombine learned patterns, while an artist puts a name and a livelihood behind a break with convention. That stake shapes both the work and the way an audience hears it.</p></div>

## Give generation a reason to be there

A junior AI engineer can test this argument without pretending to solve the future of art.

Return to the poem. A poet asks each reader for one line about a place they miss. The system replies with a new stanza inside the poet's rules. The line was unknown during production, so fixed text cannot answer it directly. Generation now has a job beyond imitation.

Ask the poet to write responses for common themes, then route each reader line to the nearest authored response. Hide which path produced each reply. If readers value the generated reply's specific connection, generation earns another test. If they prefer the authored response despite its looser fit, the model added cost without improving the experience.

Keep the engineering plain. A `TypedDict` lists the keys a Python type checker expects in shared state, such as the reader's line and release approval. If the flow gains retries, LangGraph's `StateGraph` can connect steps around that state. ReAct alternates model reasoning with external tool calls. This poem has no tool-choice problem, so leave ReAct out.

The first version can be a normal function. It rejects publication when rights verification fails, even if the poet likes the stanza. Record the model version and delete reader submissions on schedule. This is the discipline behind [treating the spec as cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/). The framework comes after the behavior is clear.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Generation deserves a place when it creates an experience a fixed work cannot provide and wins against a serious authored baseline. Start with a small, inspectable flow whose rights and release rules can block the output.</p></div>

## Change still needs someone to absorb it

Conte speaks from a band that had to rebuild its working model more than once.

Pomplamoose grew through YouTube and digital music sales. Conte calls what followed an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390). Growth flattened around YouTube's Cosmic Panda redesign, although the cause was unclear then. Streaming weakened the iTunes model. COVID changed recording. Vertical video required new production systems.

Each wave broke a setup that had worked. Pomplamoose adapted, but adaptation consumed time and money that a platform never had to repay.

I think cheap imitation may dominate feeds while a more valuable form develops somewhere smaller. If generation never beats authored alternatives on fair terms, slop was the product rather than a phase.

For now, 1895 gives junior engineers a useful assignment. Keep criticizing theft and deception. Then build one careful experiment where generation changes the experience under conditions the creator chose. Make the result face a fair comparison.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Platform change can create new forms while forcing artists to rebuild at their own expense. AI's creative promise becomes credible when a creator-approved experiment beats authored work for a reason the audience can feel.</p></div>
