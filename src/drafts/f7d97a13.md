In 1895, the Lumière brothers introduced the Cinématographe. In 1896, their train film became a cinema icon, and Maxim Gorky described the new world on screen as "without sound, without color."

In 2026, we prompt a large language model to write a Beatles song and call the result AI creativity.

I tried to treat the gray flood of AI output as a model-quality problem. I think the diagnosis is too small. We are judging a young medium by how well it can dress up as an old one.

Jack Conte, the Pomplamoose artist and Patreon co-founder, calls this the lazy phase of a new medium in his [SXSW talk on creativity and AI](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc). We use image models to fake drawing. We use video models to fake cameras. Our Beatles prompt asks the machine to hide every sign of itself.

<div class="article-tldr"><span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span><p>AI slop is a copy phase, and history gives us reasons to expect another phase. The opening for builders begins with systems whose memory and human boundaries make older forms impossible to copy.</p></div>

## The camera came before the cut

Early film looked weak when measured against theater.

Many early narrative filmmakers placed a fixed camera in front of theatrical sets. Gorky's review in 1896 was fair. The image moved, but the room had lost sound and human presence.

Georges Méliès changed the assignment. His 1902 film *A Trip to the Moon* used cuts and camera tricks to move a story between places. In *2001: A Space Odyssey*, a bone tossed into the air becomes a spacecraft in one cut. Millions of years pass before your eye can protest. A stage could never do that.

Our AI Beatles song still points a camera at the stage. Better vocals and cleaner mastering improve the disguise while the idea stays trapped inside 1966.

Recorded music escaped the same trap. The Beatles used tape loops and reversed sounds on *Revolver* in 1966, making work that was hard to reproduce live. Conte says producer George Martin called the studio a new canvas. Recording had begun to carry ideas a concert could not hold.

Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years. Then *The Great Dictator* arrived in 1940, and its final humanist speech used recorded sound as the point of the film. The tool he resisted became the tool his argument needed.

I think this pattern hides inside slop. A medium feels gray while it copies the form that came before it. Color arrives when someone discovers its native move.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film found the cut, recorded music found the studio, and Chaplin found a use for speech that silence could never carry. Our Beatles prompt remains in the copy phase because it asks AI to conceal the machine.</p></div>

## The transition sends a real bill

Historical comfort can become cheap if we skip the people who paid for it.

The American Federation of Musicians called recorded sound "canned music" in 1930s campaigns. By 1930, the union estimated that sound film had displaced 22,000 moviehouse musicians. A separate AFM recording ban began in 1942 and ended through agreements in 1944.

The Hammond Novachord made the fear physical. Introduced in 1939, this early commercial polyphonic synthesizer used 163 vacuum tubes and weighed almost 500 pounds. Conte says the union announced a prohibition because it could imitate an orchestra.

That fear had an economic base. Baumol and Bowen named the performing arts' "cost disease" in 1966. A Haydn symphony still takes roughly the same number of musicians and minutes to perform while wages and other costs rise. A later study of the 63 largest US orchestras found persistent financial pressure, which [Stanford GSB explains through cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease).

The pessimists had receipts. New media created art and removed paid work. Calling slop temporary cannot make the damage invisible.

Conte asks whether a music model trained only on recordings made before 1959 could discover the Beatles. I think current models can search a style space far better than they can choose which cultural rule deserves to break. A game gives AlphaGo rules and a score, which is why Move 37 could look strange and still be judged. Culture has no clean reward function.

*Sing Street* puts the human gap in one line: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." The model carries no reputation when our Beatles song fails. A musician does. Conte says he hates the word "content" because it flattens that human connection. *good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries Kendrick Lamar's life with it. The sound alone cannot explain its weight.

Conte also attributes a line about scarcity and difficulty to a creator he names as Keredex Chang. I could not verify it beyond the talk, so the provenance stays open. Ease reduces the signal carried by the act of making.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The copy phase destroys jobs as well as conventions, so optimism has to keep the bill visible. Human art also carries reputation and lived history, two things a generated Beatles song cannot borrow from its training data.</p></div>

## Give the song a memory

The Beatles copy becomes useful when we change the job.

Imagine a song that remembers one listener. It keeps a motif they chose and changes after each session. A human composer requires one full bar of silence in every version. Listener feedback may reshape the arrangement, but the model cannot delete that bar.

The shared state can stay small:

```python
from typing import Literal, TypedDict

class SongState(TypedDict):
    listener_id: str
    favorite_motif: str
    required_silence_bars: int
    proposed_silence_bars: int
    rejection: str | None

def validate(state: SongState, actor: Literal["model", "composer"]):
    changed = state["proposed_silence_bars"] != state["required_silence_bars"]
    if changed and actor == "model":
        return {**state, "rejection": "composer owns the silence rule"}
    return {**state,
            "required_silence_bars": state["proposed_silence_bars"],
            "rejection": None}
```

A `TypedDict` is a Python type hint that names the fields expected in a dictionary. It helps an editor or type checker catch a misspelled field. The `validate` function provides the runtime guard. Python does not enforce a `TypedDict` by itself.

LangGraph is a Python library for building agent workflows as connected steps. Its `StateGraph` class lets those steps read and update shared state such as `SongState`. The developer still has to add the route. In this example, a conditional edge sends a rejected proposal back to the composing step.

ReAct is a pattern where a model alternates reasoning with actions, then uses each result as its next observation. Our model proposes deleting the silent bar. The guard rejects it. The model can move the bar or change the tempo while the composer's decision survives.

The system depends on a relationship and a boundary. Delete the listener history and the experience collapses. Delete the guard and the composer's intent collapses. This is why [the spec is the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/).

I think this is a stronger junior AI project than another chat wrapper. You can test the illegal transition directly. If a model changes `required_silence_bars`, the system failed, regardless of how beautiful the song sounds.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>`TypedDict` describes the living song's memory, the runtime guard protects a human rule, and an explicit `StateGraph` route handles rejection. The model can explore inside the boundary without erasing the person who set it.</p></div>

## Run the imitation audit

A portfolio reveals its assumptions when you remove memory.

Take each AI pilot and erase its state between runs. If the value barely changes, the pilot probably speeds up an existing workflow. Email summaries and drafted slides can still save hours. They tell us little about the native language of the medium.

Then choose one experiment whose value collapses when memory disappears. Our song loses its relationship with the listener when `SongState` is deleted. Inside a company, the same test separates a faster handoff from a capability that exists because the system can remember an earlier decision and preserve human intent.

Games and interactive media have used procedural generation and player state for decades. Our example asks the model to maintain a personal history while a human-authored boundary survives every generation. The creative claim comes from what that relationship makes possible over time.

Conte calls Pomplamoose's career an "infinite sine wave." YouTube's Cosmic Panda change coincided with flat growth. Streaming broke the iTunes economics. COVID changed recording. Vertical video forced another rebuild. Change kept arriving, and the band kept learning the medium again.

AI will compress that cycle. Once a new pattern spreads, future training sets can absorb it and products can turn it into a preset. I wrote about that convergence in [The Elevator Music Already Started Playing](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). Exploration continues after the first breakthrough becomes a button.

I don't know whether a living song is AI's equivalent of the cut. By 2030, I think the defining AI work will carry a history with its audience and preserve a human decision through every model call. If celebrated AI work is still judged mainly by how well it imitates an older form, my prediction failed.

By then, I want our Beatles prompt to feel as primitive as a fixed camera pointed at a stage.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Audit AI work by deleting its memory and checking whether the value survives. The opening begins where persistent history and human boundaries create an experience that older media could never support.</p></div>
