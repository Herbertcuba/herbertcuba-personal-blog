In 1895, Louis Lumière filmed workers leaving his family's factory. In 2026, we point a language model at a blank page and ask for a poem.

A new machine arrives, so we use it to reproduce a form we already know.

Jack Conte makes music with Pomplamoose and [co-founded Patreon](https://www.patreon.com/about). AI reaches him as an artist and through a company built around artists getting paid. In [his talk about creativity and AI](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=844), he calls slop "that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium" when people reproduce work that humans already do better.

I think his frame reduces both panic and hype. Slop becomes evidence that we are early, and history gives us a job: protect people from the damage while exploring what this medium can do on its own terms.

## A camera first learned to imitate a seat

The [Lumière Cinématographe](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere) combined a camera with a projector, so moving images could be recorded and shown to a room.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Early film copied theater because creators had yet to discover editing. Méliès used cuts to create impossible events, and Kubrick later compressed millennia in one edit. AI is still searching for a comparable grammar.</p></div>

Early crews treated the apparatus like a theater seat. The camera held one position while action and broad stage gestures filled the frame.

Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière program in 1896 and described ["a world without sound, without colour"](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/91ddc4e0-5576-4f98-a3b6-ba8a97703eda/section/2fe1a387-c815-45d7-9609-2a47615dc9e8), with everything "dipped in monotonous grey." Film could preserve motion, but its makers were still borrowing the stage's language.

Georges Méliès came from stage magic. To make a person appear inside an empty box, he filmed the box, stopped the camera, placed someone inside, and resumed. Joining the strips hid the pause. The screen showed an instant appearance.

That physical join became part of film's grammar. By 1902, Méliès was using similar effects throughout [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/). The camera had gained a way to shape time.

Science fiction later showed how much meaning that join could carry. Near the beginning of *2001: A Space Odyssey*, an ape discovers that a bone can be used as a tool and throws it into the sky. One frame holds the spinning bone. The next holds a spacecraft with a similar shape. The [match cut crosses millennia](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/cefccdb2-b558-5623-9c17-72b4be7bd4c1/2001-a-space-odyssey) in less than a second.

I think generative AI is still close to the factory gate. The output moves, but the camera remains pointed at an old stage.

## The fear was attached to a paycheck

When synchronized sound spread through cinemas in 1927, the house orchestra became a cost a theater could remove.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Recorded sound created new forms and erased thousands of theater jobs. The Novachord drew union safeguards, while Chaplin and the Beatles found creative uses for sound. AI's medium-building phase has to include creator rights from the beginning.</p></div>

The American Federation of Musicians called recorded tracks "canned music." [Its own history says about 22,000 theater musicians lost their jobs](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/) when talkies arrived, and the union formed the Music Defense League in 1930.

That number should slow down any victory lap. An innovation can open a form while closing a paycheck.

Hammond introduced the Novachord in 1939. The AFM's [rules that May](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) said employers could not add one and reduce orchestra headcount. Some solo uses needed approval from the local union.

Baumol and Bowen later called this the performing arts' [cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease). A string quartet still needs four players and roughly the same time, even as other industries produce more with fewer labor hours. A recording can be replayed without rehiring the quartet each time.

Yet sound also became creative material. Charlie Chaplin built the Little Tramp around silent physical comedy and resisted dialogue for years. In 1940, [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/7-The-Great-Dictator) ended with an address spoken directly to the camera. On 1966's [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver), the Beatles used tape loops and backward sounds until the studio became part of the composition.

I think AI optimism becomes credible when it keeps invention and loss in the same frame. Creator consent, credit, and pay belong inside the medium's development because adding them after deployment turns them into cleanup.

## A prediction engine inherits the past

Conte's sharpest thought experiment ends a music model's training data in 1959.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Prediction makes imitation easy because models learn from past patterns. Human art also carries risk and identity, which higher sampling randomness cannot supply.</p></div>

A large language model, or LLM, turns text into tokens, which are short pieces of words and punctuation. It learns which token tends to follow the tokens before it. During generation, it samples one continuation and repeats the process. That mechanism can produce surprise, but every choice begins inside patterns learned from earlier work.

Ask the model to write a grief poem in the style of a living poet and it may reproduce the poet's meter or favorite images. A close surface still leaves the medium question open, the same pull toward familiar output I explored in [The elevator music already started playing](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/).

Conte asks whether a generative music model trained only on recordings through 1959 could arrive at the Beatles, Prince, Nirvana, Beyoncé, or Jacob Collier. He poses a [thought experiment](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2828) about where prediction reaches its edge. Those artists are remembered for breaking rules their culture had made visible.

Higher sampling temperature can widen the range of possible output. The model still has no reputation on the line. Conte [borrows a sentence from *Sing Street*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3007): "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." He later [attributes another idea](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3090) to creator Karen X Cheng: difficulty and scarcity give art part of its weight.

[Conte also says he hates the word "content"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3272). His example is *good kid, m.A.A.d city*. The record reaches him through Kendrick Lamar's background and pain, along with the sound. Conte's connection includes the person who made it.

I don't know whether a future architecture can create a cultural break we would recognize as its own. I think engineers should treat that uncertainty as a design constraint. The human author supplies stake and identity. The model can supply variation.

## Use generation where the work stays open

The same grief poem changes when a visitor supplies a sentence before the model writes.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Generation earns a creative role when live input changes the work in a way a finite authored path cannot anticipate. The artist keeps the license and decides whether anything is released.</p></div>

Imagine a living poet who licenses a set of their own poems for a small installation. A visitor contributes one sentence about someone they miss. The system creates a reply within boundaries the poet helped write.

The value comes from the encounter between that sentence and a response whose wording could only be chosen after the visitor arrived. This uses generation for live variation instead of asking it to impersonate a finished poem.

I think a serious authored version deserves to compete. The poet could prepare branches for common themes. If they carry the same experience, the generative layer is expensive scenery. Open-ended visitor language gives generation a stronger reason because a finite tree cannot anticipate every sentence.

The rights need to be concrete. The poet defines the source license and is paid. Training reuse requires separate permission. Publication begins blocked, and only the poet can approve an output for release. A rejected output is deleted. The visitor's sentence expires after the session unless they choose storage.

Start with the simplest workflow that can enforce those states. A clear specification still carries the human intent, as I argued in [The spec is the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/), while the open slot belongs to the participant.

My take is that junior engineers should spend more time defining this boundary than shopping for another AI library. The medium earns its place when the experience would collapse without live generation.

## The sine wave keeps moving

Conte learned this pattern through Pomplamoose before he applied it to AI.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Pomplamoose rebuilt after each platform shock. Conte's infinite sine wave offers a better emotional model for AI: a broken method can push creators toward another form.</p></div>

YouTube gave Pomplamoose an audience, and iTunes turned some of that attention into MP3 sales. But the band's growth flattened around YouTube's Cosmic Panda redesign, although Conte says the cause was unclear. Streaming weakened the sales model. COVID changed how the band could record, so they rebuilt their setup. Vertical video moved the frame again.

Conte calls this recurring pattern an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390). A working method rises until a platform shift breaks it.

That history keeps job loss and creator rights in sight. When a platform broke the method, Pomplamoose tested another setup and watched how its audience responded. The next form came from making something and listening to the response.

Generative AI has spent its first years pointing a new machine at old work. The people who define its creative future will move the camera, protect the humans in the frame, and make the first cut.
