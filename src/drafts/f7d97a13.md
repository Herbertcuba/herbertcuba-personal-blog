In 1895, the Lumière brothers pointed a camera at a train. A year later, Maxim Gorky watched their films and saw "a world without sound, without colour," dipped in gray.

In 2026, we ask a large language model to write a new Beatles song. It sounds familiar and strangely dead. The job came from a culture that already knew Beatles songs.

A large language model, or LLM, learns patterns from huge amounts of data and estimates what could come next. Its map begins with previous work.

I tried to treat AI slop as proof that the medium had failed. Jack Conte's SXSW talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc), changed the frame for me. I think we are hearing a young medium imitate before it has found a grammar of its own.

<div class="article-tldr">
<span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span>
<p>AI slop appears when we ask a new medium to imitate, as with a Beatles copy. Early reviews can be accurate before the medium finds its own grammar. Junior engineers can test another path by giving generated work memory and a human-owned limit.</p>

## The first critics were right about the first version

Early film looked thin because filmmakers were still arranging scenes for a stage.

Gorky's review in 1896 called the Lumière program "a world without sound, without colour ... Everything is dipped in monotonous grey." I think he described the experience honestly. Much of the action still unfolded in front of a fixed lens.

Georges Méliès gave the machine stranger work. In *A Trip to the Moon* in 1902, cuts made bodies disappear and moved the story between Earth and the Moon. Kubrick pushed that grammar further in *2001: A Space Odyssey*: one cut joins a falling bone to a spacecraft, crossing millions of years.

Recorded music had a harsher transition. The American Federation of Musicians called it "canned music" in 1930s ads. By 1930, an estimated 22,000 moviehouse musicians had lost their jobs as sound films replaced live accompaniment. A union recording ban began in 1942 and lasted more than two years. Any historical analogy that skips those people becomes sales copy.

The studio developed a language. The Beatles built *Revolver* in 1966 with tape loops and reversed guitar. Conte quotes George Martin calling the studio "a new canvas on which they had to paint." Charlie Chaplin held out through *City Lights* and *Modern Times*, then used his voice for the final speech in *The Great Dictator* in 1940.

The Hammond Novachord put the discomfort in one machine. Unveiled in 1939, the first commercial polyphonic synthesizer used 163 vacuum tubes and weighed almost 500 pounds. Conte says the union prohibited it. Synthesizers later entered entire branches of popular music.

The economic wound never closed cleanly. Baumol and Bowen named the cost disease of the performing arts in 1966: a Haydn symphony still takes about 25 minutes while costs rise. A [Stanford GSB analysis](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) reported deficits at 46 of the 63 largest US orchestras in 2008.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>New media can destroy jobs before artists discover their grammar. Film needed the cut. Recorded music needed artists willing to use the studio as an instrument.</p></div>

## A fake Beatles track is a diagnosis

Our model reaches for the Beatles because the request points straight into patterns in its training data.

Conte defines slop as "that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium where we're all just trying to reproduce existing things that are honestly just better if the people do them." A fake Beatles track is almost a lab sample. Higher fidelity makes it sound closer to the reference.

Pretraining creates much of the pull because familiar musical patterns occupy a lot of space in the data. Sampling can produce an unusual combination. Preference tuning adds pressure toward answers that human raters accept. I think this explains why an output can surprise us locally and still feel culturally safe.

AlphaGo's Move 37 against Lee Sedol in 2016 is the obvious counterexample. A trained system found a move professional players would have avoided. Games have also used generated variation for decades, inside worlds with rules the machine can read. Go has a winner. Culture never returns a clean score for the next Beatles.

Risk adds something the score cannot hold. In *Sing Street*, an older brother tells a kid starting a band, "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." A model can emit a strange chorus without losing status, money, or sleep. The person who signs the work takes that exposure.

Conte credits a creator whose name sounds like Keredex Chang with an idea about scarcity: art gathers value around what is hard to make. I could only trace the attribution to his talk. *good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries Kendrick Lamar's life alongside its sound.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Training data gives familiar forms a wide path, and preference tuning can make that path safer. A game supplies a score for machine exploration. Culture asks a person to choose a direction and live with the response.</p></div>

## Give the song memory and a locked field

The Beatles imitation becomes a different engineering problem once each listen changes the next version.

Suppose the system writes an evolving song for one listener. It remembers which motif they replayed. A composer adds one rule: every version must contain a full bar of silence. The listener may hate that pause. The system preserves it until the composer changes the rule.

The shared state can stay small:

```python
from typing import TypedDict
from langgraph.graph import StateGraph

class SongState(TypedDict):
    listener_id: str
    motifs_heard: list[str]
    last_feedback: str
    required_silence_bars: int
    rejected_changes: list[str]
    version: int

graph = StateGraph(SongState)
graph.add_node("compose", compose)
graph.add_node("validate", validate_composer_rule)
graph.add_edge("compose", "validate")
graph.add_conditional_edges("validate", route_after_check)
```

A `TypedDict` is a Python type that describes a dictionary with named fields. Your editor can catch a missing `version` or a misspelled `listener_id` before the workflow runs.

LangGraph is a Python library for building LLM workflows as graphs. Its `StateGraph` connects steps that read and update shared state. Save that state in a small database, keyed by `listener_id`, and the song can remember what happened last week.

ReAct means reason and act. In a ReAct loop, the model selects an action, reads the tool result, and chooses its next action. For version 12, the model sees that the listener skipped the silent bar. It proposes changing `required_silence_bars` from `1` to `0`. The validator rejects the update, records `silence_rule` in `rejected_changes`, and routes the original state into another compose step.

The model can alter tempo or bring back a motif the listener loved. It cannot edit a field owned by the composer. If the listener keeps skipping the pause, a person has to decide whether the rule still earns its place.

Games already have memory and procedural change. Interactive machine-learning tools let artists steer output during a performance. This project adds a relationship across many listens, with a human limit stored outside the model's permissions. I think that delta is small enough for a junior engineer to build and large enough to test honestly.

This is also why [the spec is the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/). The locked field turns taste into a system boundary instead of another sentence the model may reinterpret.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Persistent state gives the song a history. A write-protected composer rule gives the workflow something it cannot optimize away, and an illegal transition makes that boundary testable in code.</p></div>

## Put the imitation test on the backlog

A portfolio review can separate useful automation from experiments that need a new medium.

Take every AI pilot and ask one practical question: if you remove memory between runs, does the product lose its reason to exist? An email summary probably survives. Our evolving song collapses into another generated track.

Imitation can still save time. A governed enterprise can summarize cases or draft reports, then measure the gain against review cost and error rate. Keep those pilots and reserve a smaller budget for work that depends on prior contact or a boundary owned by an accountable person.

Conte calls Pomplamoose's career an infinite sine wave. A YouTube algorithm change flattened growth. The band rebuilt around iTunes and later Spotify. COVID stopped touring. TikTok changed discovery again.

I don't know whether AI will follow cinema. History contains plenty of media that stayed awkward and disappeared. I think the useful bet is narrower: by 2030, the AI work people care about most will carry a history of contact and a human decision the model could never quietly revise. If the celebrated result is still a cleaner fake Beatles song, my prediction failed.

Models will train on the breakthrough, and [the copy cycle will begin again](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). Every new grammar eventually becomes data.

In 2030, I want to hear the listener's history and the composer's refusal inside the song. Another fake Beatles track would mean we spent four more years filming the stage.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Audit AI pilots by removing their memory and checking whether their value survives. Keep imitation projects that save measured time, then fund at least one experiment whose history and human-owned boundary are essential to the result.</p></div>
