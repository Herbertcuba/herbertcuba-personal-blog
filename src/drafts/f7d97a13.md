# AI is still in its 1895 phase

When [Lumière programmes](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere) began drawing paying audiences in 1895, cameras often stayed still while ordinary motion crossed the frame. One film showed a train entering a station. After a programme in 1896, Maxim Gorky described [“a world without sound, without colour”](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/91ddc4e0-5576-4f98-a3b6-ba8a97703eda/section/2fe1a387-c815-45d7-9609-2a47615dc9e8), with everything “dipped in monotonous grey.” He saw life’s shadow captured by a machine.

In 2026, we ask a large language model for a poem or an image model for a hand-drawn look, then compare the result with human work. I think we are repeating the early mistake: using new machinery to copy an inherited form, then treating the weak copy as the limit of the medium.

Jack Conte, a musician and Patreon co-founder, calls slop [“that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium”](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=844), when people reproduce things humans already make better. My take is that slop diagnoses immaturity. A hopeful reading still needs proof that generation can develop its own creative language and that creators can keep a defensible stake in the transition.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI slop is what an imitation-heavy medium looks like before artists discover expressive moves that depend on its machinery. History makes that possibility credible, while offering no guarantee.</p></div>

## Film escaped the stage through the cut

Early filmmakers borrowed theatre’s mental model. The camera occupied the audience’s seat while actors performed inside one fixed frame. The magician Georges Méliès changed the logic by filming an empty box, stopping the camera, placing a person inside it and recording again. Joining those moments made the person appear from nowhere.

By 1902, [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) used such tricks to build events that belonged to film. The cut, a join between separately recorded moments, let artists reshape sequence and time. I think this is a useful definition of a medium’s native grammar: expressive moves whose effect depends on the medium itself.

Recorded music found a similar freedom in the studio. On 1966’s [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver), the Beatles used tape loops and backwards sound as parts of composition. Charlie Chaplin offers a narrower lesson about adaptation. After resisting synchronised dialogue for years, he used his recorded voice at the centre of 1940’s [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/7-The-Great-Dictator), delivering words and vocal tone to the mass audience at once.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film became more than recorded theatre when the cut changed time and sequence. Recorded sound became more than preservation when the studio and the human voice started shaping the work itself.</p></div>

## Every transition sends a bill

Creative breakthroughs arrive on a different timeline from economic pain. When talkies spread in 1927, about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs. The [American Federation of Musicians](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/) called recorded accompaniment “canned music” and formed the Music Defense League in 1930. That evidence describes displacement, while the Beatles example describes artistic invention. Joining them too quickly hides who paid during the decades between disruption and discovery.

The Novachord made the conflict around substitution explicit. [AFM rules published in May 1939](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) said that adding a Novachord or Hammond organ could not reduce the number of musicians in an ensemble. Decades later, Baumol and Bowen’s idea of performing-arts [cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) explained the pressure: live performance keeps much of its human time while recorded or synthetic substitutes scale cheaply.

AI creates related pressure when creators supply source material and someone else captures the saving from generation. Permission determines whether that material may be used, and payment determines who shares the value. Source records keep the human contribution visible, and a named decision owner must withdraw disputed output and compensate creators when agreed rights are breached. I think exploration earns trust when those protections sit inside the experiment from day one.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Historical transitions produced new art and immediate job loss on separate timelines. AI experiments should use authorised material and name the person responsible for enforcing creator terms.</p></div>

## Generation has to change the experience

Conte asks whether a music model trained only on work available through 1959 could somehow reach the Beatles, Prince or Nirvana. I don’t know. Models can recombine patterns and search large spaces, especially when artists guide them, so surprise alone proves little. The person who selects and releases a strange result still carries the risk of ridicule or a damaged career.

Conte says *good kid, m.A.A.d city* matters to him because it carries Kendrick Lamar’s life and pain. The evidence describes his response alone, yet exposes something output tests miss: audiences may care who chose the work and carried the consequences. Human and model collaboration can qualify, but the whole work still has to show what live generation contributes.

Remove live generation and check whether the intended audience effect remains. That is the substitution test. Imagine an installation that turns a visitor’s spoken memory into a scene. Before opening, the artist defines the intended effect: visitors should recognise a unique detail and let that reply shape their next choice. Some receive live-generated scenes; others, without knowing, receive the closest scene from a fixed, human-authored library.

The artist records in advance how large the difference between the groups must be. If both versions create the same response, generation served as a faster production method. If the live version returns recognised personal details and changes the interaction more often by the chosen margin, it has added an experience the fixed library failed to provide. Using AI only to imitate a drawing is like using *Star Trek*’s holodeck to display a poster. The machinery promises a responsive world, while the chosen form remains fixed.

By 2030, I think the 1895 analogy should face this substitution test. Removing generation should erase the audience effect, and tracing the work backward should lead to people who retain permission and an economic stake.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Generation becomes a creative medium when a defined audience effect disappears in a fixed, human-authored comparison. By 2030, that evidence should exist while the people behind it retain permission and a share of the value.</p></div>
