In 1895, the Lumière brothers pointed a camera at workers leaving their factory. The camera stayed in one place while people crossed the frame. The image moved, but the experience still looked like theatre from the tenth row.

In 2026, we ask a large language model to write a poem and call the result AI-generated content. An LLM predicts likely continuations from patterns in its training data, so enough poems let it produce something shaped like one.

I think we are repeating the mistake of early cinema. We judge a young medium by how well it copies work built for an older one.

Musician and Patreon co-founder Jack Conte gives this phase a useful name in his July 2026 talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=17_HcR95YBc). Slop, in his framing, is the lazy period after a medium arrives, when people reproduce things humans already make better. The phase may pass. A working illustrator can still lose income this month.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Slop appears when a new medium spends its early years imitating older work. The phase may pass, while the cost to working creators arrives immediately.</p></div>

## Film found the cut

The first filmmakers had a machine that could record movement, but they still composed scenes for a stage.

In 1896, Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière programme and described ["a world without sound, without colour"](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/91ddc4e0-5576-4f98-a3b6-ba8a97703eda/section/2fe1a387-c815-45d7-9609-2a47615dc9e8), with everything "dipped in monotonous grey." He was looking at a technical wonder that felt strangely lifeless.

Georges Méliès came from stage magic. He filmed an empty box, stopped the camera, placed a person inside, then restarted it. Joining the strips made the person appear. The break in recording became part of the illusion. By 1902, his [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) built a world a theatre could only suggest.

Stanley Kubrick later showed how far the cut could go. Near the start of [*2001: A Space Odyssey*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/cefccdb2-b558-5623-9c17-72b4be7bd4c1/2001-a-space-odyssey), early humans discover that a bone can be a tool. One throws it into the air. Kubrick cuts to a similarly shaped spacecraft, and thousands of years disappear between two frames.

I think AI creativity still has its camera in the tenth row. A better prompt often gives us a sharper recording of the same stage. Méliès became interesting when he used the break in the film itself.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film grew beyond recorded theatre after filmmakers used stopping, joining and rearranging images as part of the art. AI remains early while imitation is its main proof of quality.</p></div>

## Creators pay during the experiment

A medium can take decades to find its form. People lose work much sooner.

The American Federation of Musicians called recorded accompaniment ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). Its history says about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs when talking pictures arrived in 1927. The union formed the Music Defense League in 1930, then called a recording strike in 1942.

The Hammond Novachord triggered a related fight in 1939. This early polyphonic synthesizer could play several notes at once. Union [rules published that May](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) protected orchestra headcount when venues added it. Solo use where musicians had worked before required local consent.

William Baumol and William Bowen later described this pressure as the [cost disease of the performing arts](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease). A string quartet still needs four players and the same performance time as other industries get faster. Speeding up the quartet changes the music.

Artists eventually found uses that belonged to the tools. Charlie Chaplin built the Tramp through silent comedy and resisted synchronized speech for years. In 1940, he used speech for the closing humanist address in [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/1-The-Great-Dictator). The Beatles used backwards sound and studio experiments on 1966's [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver), making music a live capture could not reproduce.

I think this history gives AI builders a double duty. We have to search for the new form while putting source licences, payment terms and human release authority into the first prototype. A future masterpiece cannot repay a creator whose work was taken today.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Creative technology can remove jobs before artists discover its strongest form. Exploration deserves support only when creators keep rights, payment and authority during the transition.</p></div>

## A model has no reputation to lose

Cultural invention asks someone to choose a break before the audience rewards it.

Conte offers a sharp thought experiment. Train a music model only on recordings made through 1959, then ask it to find the Beatles, Prince, Nirvana, Biggie, Beyonce or Jacob Collier. The model can recombine patterns it has learned. The harder move is choosing a break its data has never rewarded.

Conte later attributes a line to *Sing Street*: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." A model can produce a bad chord and lose a benchmark point. A musician puts years of trust behind a release and may have to perform the choice in front of people.

Recombination can produce novelty, and models already surprise their users. I don't know whether future systems will make cultural breaks without a person choosing the risk.

I think the harder gap is biography. Conte points to Kendrick Lamar's *good kid, m.A.A.d city* and says the album carries Kendrick's history and pain. Listeners meet the sound through a person, while a model has no reputation attached to the chord it generates. The word "content" strips away that relationship and turns the album into one more file in a feed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Models can produce surprising output, while artists carry the social cost of a choice. A person's history changes how the work is heard and who answers for it.</p></div>

## Give generation a test it can fail

Junior AI engineers can turn the 1895 claim into a product test.

Imagine a call-and-response song. A living composer writes the musical rules. A listener plays a short motif, and the system replies inside those rules. The composer has never heard that exact motif during production.

The test begins by removing live generation. A static recording cannot answer the listener, so it only checks whether interaction was needed. Authored branches are the serious baseline: the composer records replies for expected motifs. If those branches satisfy listeners, the model adds cost and uncertainty without enough value.

Call this the removal test. Hold back 30 motifs during development. Let the composer score branch and model replies from 1 to 5 without seeing which system made them. Before coding, require the model to beat branches by half a point, keep 95 percent of replies inside the latency target and stay below the cost cap. If it misses a bar, ship the branches.

An ordinary state machine should run the first version. If the flow grows, LangGraph is a Python library for workflows that branch or loop. Its `StateGraph` moves a shared song record through named steps. A `TypedDict` tells type checkers which fields belong in that record. ReAct, short for reasoning and acting, lets a model use a tool and react to the result. This song needs it only if tools must be selected during the performance.

The release rule can stay plain: `release = rights_verified and composer_approved`. The pilot brief should also name the licence scope, payment, data retention limit and kill switch. This is [the spec doing the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/) before the model starts producing.

My take is that generation should survive comparison with the cheaper version. If removing it changes little for the listener, the project is still filming the stage.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The removal test compares a model with authored branches on inputs hidden during development. Generation earns a role only after a creator-approved test shows a valued gain within rights and performance limits.</p></div>

## Change comes in waves

Conte has lived this cycle as an artist, which makes his optimism carry a personal cost.

Pomplamoose built an audience through YouTube and digital music sales. Conte calls the band's disruptions an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390). Growth flattened around YouTube's Cosmic Panda redesign, although the cause was unclear at the time. Streaming weakened the iTunes model. COVID changed how the band recorded. Vertical video demanded new production systems.

Each wave broke a setup that had worked. Pomplamoose rebuilt and kept making music.

I think that is the useful kind of hope. History offers a pattern without giving AI a guaranteed future. Some media never find a durable form. Slop could remain the dominant business model if cheap imitation keeps paying.

That risk gives engineers a better target than volume. We can document where training data came from, give creators release control and test whether generation creates an experience that authored work cannot cover. [The cultural elevator music already playing](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/) is a reason to raise the standard.

Slop describes our current use of the medium, with its final form still unwritten. We are filming from the tenth row, and the next step begins with a cut only this medium can make.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Creative change arrives through repeated disruption, and history offers a pattern rather than a promise. AI has a future only if engineers move beyond copies while creators retain authority over the work.</p></div>
