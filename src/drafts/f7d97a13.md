In 1895, the Lumière brothers debuted the Cinématographe. In 1896, their train film became an early icon of cinema, and Maxim Gorky described the new medium as "a world without sound, without color."

In 2026, we ask a large language model for a Beatles song and grade the result by how little it sounds like a machine.

I tried to treat the flood of gray AI output as a model-quality problem. A better model seemed like the answer. I think that diagnosis is too small.

A large language model, or LLM, predicts the next token from patterns learned during training. Familiar prompts give those patterns a wide road. Jack Conte, the Pomplamoose artist and Patreon co-founder, argues in his [2026 talk on creativity and AI](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc) that we are watching the copy phase of a new medium.

<div class="article-tldr"><span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span><p>Early film and recorded music make AI slop look less final, though the transition still destroys work. Builders can test another direction with persistent state and a runtime guard that preserves a human decision.</p></div>

## The camera came before the cut

A new machine usually receives an old assignment first.

Many early narrative filmmakers borrowed theatrical staging and fixed-camera composition. Gorky saw the loss clearly in 1896. The image moved, but the sound and physical presence of theater had vanished. I think his review was fair.

Georges Méliès used the camera differently. His 1902 film *A Trip to the Moon* relied on cuts and camera tricks to move a story between places. Decades later, *2001: A Space Odyssey* cut from a falling bone to a spacecraft. Millions of years passed in a fraction of a second. Theater could change scenes. Film could cut time.

Our AI Beatles song is still a camera pointed at a stage. Cleaner audio improves the copy while the assignment stays inside 1966.

Recorded music went through the same early habit. By 1966, *Revolver* used tape loops and reversed sounds that could hardly be reproduced live. Conte quotes producer George Martin calling the studio "a new canvas on which they had to paint." Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then used it in *The Great Dictator* in 1940. Its final speech depended on recorded sound.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film found the cut, and recorded music found the studio. Our Beatles prompt stays in the copy phase because it asks AI to hide the machine rather than use it.</p></div>

## The people who feared it had receipts

Creative breakthroughs left real people carrying the cost of transition.

The American Federation of Musicians attacked recorded sound as "canned music" in 1930s ads. By 1930, the union estimated that sound film had removed 22,000 moviehouse musicians from work. A separate recording ban began in 1942 and ended through agreements in 1944.

The Hammond Novachord made the threat visible in one huge box. Introduced in 1939, the early commercial polyphonic synthesizer used 163 vacuum tubes and weighed almost 500 pounds. Conte says the musicians' union announced a prohibition because the machine could imitate an orchestra.

Baumol and Bowen named the cost disease of the performing arts in 1966. A Haydn symphony takes roughly the same time while costs rise. Robert Flanagan's 2008 report on the 63 largest US orchestras found deficits at 46 of them, a problem [Stanford GSB described through cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease).

I can hold two reactions at once: excitement about the medium and anger at who pays for the transition. Calling slop temporary gives us no excuse to treat the damage as temporary.

Conte calls slop "that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium where we're all just trying to reproduce existing things that are honestly just better if the people do them." Pretraining pulls our Beatles song toward patterns already common in the data. Preference tuning, which uses human ratings to adjust model behavior, can pull it toward answers that feel safe to raters. The two forces work differently, but both reward familiarity.

Games give the strongest counterexample. Procedural systems have generated worlds for decades, and AlphaGo's Move 37 surprised professional players in 2016. A game supplies rules and a way to score an action. Culture supplies neither. Conte asks whether a music model trained only on recordings made before 1959 could reach the Beatles, Prince, Nirvana, or Beyoncé on its own. I think current models search style far better than they choose which cultural rule deserves to break.

*Sing Street* gives the human part one clean sentence: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." The model carries no reputation when our Beatles song fails. A musician releasing a strange track under their own name carries the response. Kendrick Lamar's *good kid, m.A.A.d city* means more because Kendrick lived the story we hear.

Conte attributes a line about art and scarcity to a creator named in the transcript as Keredex Chang. I could not verify the person or wording beyond the talk, so I will not lean on it. The effect itself is familiar: when it becomes easy, the decision to use it carries less information about the artist.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Recorded media created new art while destroying paid work, so optimism has to keep the bill visible. Our AI Beatles song also lacks the reputation and lived history that gave the original work its weight.</p></div>

## Give the song a memory

The same example becomes more interesting when the output depends on a relationship.

Imagine a song that remembers one listener's favorite motif and changes after each session. A human composer requires one full bar of silence in every version. Listener behavior may move that silence, but the model has no permission to delete it.

The state can stay small:

```python
from typing import TypedDict

class SongState(TypedDict):
    listener_id: str
    favorite_motif: str
    last_skip_second: int
    required_silence_bars: int
    version: int

def apply_update(state: SongState, update: dict, actor: str):
    if "required_silence_bars" in update and actor != "composer":
        raise PermissionError("composer owns the silence rule")
    return state | update
```

A `TypedDict` is a Python type hint that names the fields expected in a dictionary. It helps an editor or type checker catch a misspelled field. Runtime enforcement comes from `apply_update`, the guard below the schema.

LangGraph is a Python library for connected LLM steps. Its `StateGraph` class passes `SongState` between them. ReAct is a loop where the model proposes an action, reads the result, then chooses again. In this song, one step reads listener history, another composes, and the guard checks the composer's rule.

In version 12, the listener skips during the silent bar. The model proposes changing `required_silence_bars` from `1` to `0`. The guard rejects the write. `StateGraph` sends the original state back, so the model can move the silence or change the tempo. The composer's decision survives.

That field exists because a human wrote the boundary into the design. The guard turns [the spec into executable cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/). I think this is a stronger junior AI project than another chat wrapper because failure becomes visible: either the forbidden update is rejected or it is accepted.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The living song remembers a listener while one human rule remains protected at runtime. `TypedDict` describes the memory, and the guard decides which updates the system will accept.</p></div>

## Run the imitation test

You can use the 1895 frame on an AI portfolio this week.

Erase the state between runs. If the product feels unchanged, it is probably reproducing an existing workflow. That can still save time. Email summaries and drafted slides are useful copies, but they tell us little about the ceiling of the medium.

Then fund one experiment whose value collapses when memory disappears. Our song should lose its relationship with the listener when `SongState` is deleted. The composer's silence rule should return when the system starts again. Inside a company, the same test separates a faster old handoff from a capability that only exists because the system can remember what happened and preserve human intent.

Conte calls Pomplamoose's career an "infinite sine wave." The band's growth flatlined around YouTube's Cosmic Panda change, though Conte says they did not know the cause at the time. Streaming changed the iTunes economics. COVID changed their recording setup, and TikTok later forced another production workflow. They kept rebuilding as the medium moved.

AI will repeat that cycle faster. Once a new pattern works, models will learn from it. Product teams will package it as a preset. That is the same convergence I examined in [The Elevator Music Already Started Playing](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/): repeated AI loops drift toward similar output. Builders then have to explore again.

I don't know whether the living song is AI's equivalent of the cut. By 2030, I think the defining AI work will carry a history with its audience and preserve a human decision through every model call. If celebrated AI work is still judged mainly by how cleanly it copies an older form, my prediction failed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Delete the living song's listener history and its value should collapse, while the composer's silence rule should remain. That is a tougher test than asking for a cleaner Beatles copy.</p></div>
