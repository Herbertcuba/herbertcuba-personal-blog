In 1895, the Lumière brothers filmed a train arriving at a station and called it cinema. Critics called it lifeless. In 2026, we prompt a large language model to write a poem and call it AI-generated content. We are making the same mistake.

A large language model, or LLM, learns patterns from training data and predicts which piece of text should come next. A familiar prompt can produce a convincing copy while keeping our attention fixed on the old form.

I tried to treat AI slop as a quality problem. In his SXSW 2026 talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc), Pomplamoose artist and Patreon co-founder Jack Conte gave it a more useful history. I think AI is in its 1895 phase.

<div class="article-tldr">
<span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span>
<p>Young media begin by imitating older ones, which is why AI output feels gray. Builders can move the medium forward through work with memory and a human boundary the model cannot rewrite.</p>
</div>

## The Gorky phase

Maxim Gorky saw a working camera and an unfinished medium.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Early cinema borrowed the grammar of theater. Film found its own voice through the cut, camera tricks and movement between scenes.</p></div>

After a Lumière screening in 1896, Gorky described a world without sound or color, submerged in monotonous gray. I think his review was fair because the stage was the only map early filmmakers had.

Georges Méliès changed the assignment. His 1902 film *A Trip to the Moon* used cuts and camera effects beyond the stage. Decades later, Stanley Kubrick compressed millions of years into one cut in *2001: A Space Odyssey*, from a bone in the air to a spacecraft in orbit.

That sci-fi cut is a medium discovering its own verb. The camera had worked for years. The grammar arrived later.

## The old medium always fights back

Recorded sound made the fear measurable in lost paychecks.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Recorded sound opened creative space while closing real jobs. Resistance faded only after artists found uses the old form could never support.</p></div>

The American Federation of Musicians attacked "canned music" in 1930s ads. By 1930, it estimated that talkies had cost 22,000 moviehouse musicians their jobs. A commercial recording ban followed in 1942.

Recorded music developed its own grammar. On *Revolver* in 1966, the Beatles used tape loops and reversed guitar. Conte quotes producer George Martin calling the studio "a new canvas on which they had to paint."

Charlie Chaplin resisted talkies for years. His first full sound film, *The Great Dictator* in 1940, ended with a plea built around the human voice.

The Hammond Novachord appeared at the 1939 New York World's Fair. The first commercial polyphonic synthesizer used 163 vacuum tubes and weighed almost 500 pounds. Conte says the union prohibited it, though independent evidence remains thin. Its descendants shaped the music of Stevie Wonder and Prince.

Economists William Baumol and William Bowen named the cost disease of the performing arts in 1966. A Haydn symphony still takes about 25 minutes while costs rise. A [2008 Stanford GSB analysis](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) found deficits at 46 of the 63 largest US orchestras.

I think honest optimism has to hold both sides. A medium can expand culture while the transition hurts the people who built the culture it learned from.

## Slop is the imitation layer

A fake Beatles song gives us a clean test.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Slop appears when a new machine gets an old medium's assignment. Better imitation may improve the surface while leaving the creative question untouched.</p></div>

Conte calls slop "that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium where we're all just trying to reproduce existing things that are honestly just better if the people do them." A Beatles prompt makes the old form our benchmark.

Two technical forces pull the song toward the familiar. Pretraining teaches likely continuations from a huge body of examples. Preference tuning uses human ratings to narrow the acceptable range.

AI systems can still surprise us. AlphaGo's Move 37 against Lee Sedol in 2016 found an option experts missed. Games have also used procedural generation and player state for decades.

Those examples work inside spaces with rules and a way to score progress. Culture has no win function. A model trained on music up to 1959 has no reward that returns `1` for the Beatles and `0` for another skilled guitar group.

I think cultural leaps still need a kind of rulebreaking that current models have yet to show at scale. Better models may give us a cleaner fake Beatles track. The assignment remains borrowed.

## Art carries a name

The difference becomes clearer when the fake song fails.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A model can generate surprise without carrying the social cost of failure. Human work gains meaning through the person who signs it and accepts the risk.</p></div>

In *Sing Street*, an older brother tells the kid starting a band, "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." The model has no reputation to lose when our fake Beatles song lands badly. The person who releases a strange song under their own name carries the consequence.

Conte attributes an idea to a creator whose name sounds like Keredex Chang: art lives in scarcity, and difficulty gives it weight. I found no independent source for it. Cheap effects push effort toward choices that reveal a person.

Conte dislikes the word "content" for the same reason. Kendrick Lamar's *good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries his history inside the sound, which changes how we hear it.

Today's systems leave the reputation, consent and economic cost with the human beings around them.

## Build the song the old medium cannot hold

Our fake Beatles song becomes useful once we change its job.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Turn the one-off imitation into a composition that remembers one listener and changes through contact with them. Keep one creative rule under human control so the system can adapt without erasing its author.</p></div>

The imitation returns one audio file. An AI-native version could remember a listener across months, including which motifs they replay and how long they stay with silence. A human composer sets one boundary that survives every model call.

The shared state can stay small:

```python
from typing import TypedDict

class SongState(TypedDict):
    listener_id: str
    motifs_heard: list[str]
    last_feedback: str
    tension: float
    required_silence_bars: int
```

A `TypedDict` is a Python type hint for a dictionary with named fields. It helps your editor catch wrong values.

LangGraph is a Python library for connecting steps in an LLM workflow. Its `StateGraph` class lets those steps share state. Our steps read the listener's history, compose and validate the human rule.

A ReAct loop lets a model choose an action, observe the result and choose again. Our loop reads a listening event, composes and retries after a rejected validation.

Suppose shorter tracks get more completions. The model proposes changing `required_silence_bars` from `1` to `0`. The validator rejects the update because the composer owns that field. `StateGraph` routes the work back to the compose step with the original value intact.

The rejection gives the system its shape. The song can learn from a listener while preserving a choice the optimizer would erase. [The spec is the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/) because the boundary carries the authorship.

I think this is a better junior AI project than another chat interface. A model API and a small database are enough to test the idea. Méliès needed the cut more than he needed a sharper recording of a stage play.

## Run the imitation test

A team can use the 1895 frame on Monday morning.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Audit each AI pilot by checking whether it reproduces an existing workflow or creates a capability with no useful human baseline. Keep useful copies, then fund one experiment that depends on the new medium.</p></div>

Put every pilot into one of those two buckets. An old workflow made faster can still produce real value. It simply tells you little about the medium's ceiling.

Our fake Beatles generator sits in the first bucket. The living song sits in the second because its memory changes the work while the composer's rule survives. In an enterprise, the same test separates a faster handoff from a capability beyond static workflows.

Conte describes Pomplamoose's career as an infinite sine wave. A YouTube algorithm change broke their growth, Spotify changed the economics and COVID stopped touring. TikTok rewired discovery. The band kept rebuilding.

The Gorky phase will repeat inside AI. Once someone finds a native pattern, models will train on it and product teams will turn it into a preset. The strange thing becomes the average, the pull I explored in [The Elevator Music Already Started Playing](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). Every successful cut eventually becomes another stage to leave.

My bet is dated so it can fail. By 2030, the defining AI-native work will remember who is present and change through contact with them while obeying a human constraint. If the most celebrated artifact is still a cleaner Beatles imitation, the bet failed.

I don't know who discovers the first native verb. A junior engineer can be the person who builds it.

The prompt already works. Build the thing that could never have existed before it.
