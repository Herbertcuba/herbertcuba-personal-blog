In 1895, the Lumière brothers debuted the Cinématographe. A year later, their train film became one of cinema's early icons. In 2026, we ask a large language model to write a song in a familiar style and judge the whole medium by how well it hides the machine.

We are filming the stage again.

I tried to treat AI slop as a quality problem. The prompts felt lazy and the results felt gray, so a better model seemed like the answer. I think that diagnosis is too small.

A large language model, or LLM, predicts the next token from patterns learned in existing work. Familiar requests lead toward familiar patterns. Jack Conte, Patreon co-founder and Pomplamoose artist, gave this phase a wider historical frame in his [2026 talk on creativity and AI](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc).

<div class="article-tldr"><span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span><p>AI slop is the copy phase of a young medium, and its economic harm is real. Engineers can help the medium grow by building work that remembers a person, changes through use, and keeps human choices inside the system.</p></div>

## The Gorky phase

Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière screening in 1896 and described "a world without sound, without color," where everything was "dipped in monotonous gray."

I think Gorky's review was fair. Many early narrative films borrowed the staging of theater and placed it in front of a fixed camera. Film could preserve a performance, but the result lost the sound and physical presence of the stage.

Georges Méliès found a native move. His 1902 film *A Trip to the Moon* used cuts and camera tricks to jump between places. The cut gave film control over time itself.

Kubrick pushed that grammar further in *2001: A Space Odyssey*. A bone falls through the air. One cut later, a spacecraft floats above Earth, and millions of years have passed. That scene is more than a sci-fi reference. It shows what happens when an artist finds an action the older medium could never perform.

Our example song begins in the earlier phase. Ask an LLM for something that sounds like the Beatles and it becomes a tribute act with infinite stamina. Better audio makes the copy smoother. The assignment stays trapped in 1966.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Young media often begin by copying an older form. Film grew when artists found the cut, an action that belonged to film itself.</p></div>

## The cost was real

Recorded media created new art while removing paid work from old systems.

The American Federation of Musicians attacked recorded sound as "canned music" in 1930s ads. By 1930, the union estimated that sound film had cost 22,000 moviehouse musicians their jobs. A separate AFM recording ban began in 1942 and ended through agreements in 1944. The people who feared the machine had numbers on their side.

Artists still discovered what recording could do. The Beatles made *Revolver* in 1966 with tape loops and reversed sounds. Conte quotes George Martin describing the studio as "a new canvas on which they had to paint." Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then used his voice in *The Great Dictator* in 1940. His final speech needed the reach of recorded sound.

The Hammond Novachord made the conflict physical. Introduced in 1939, this early commercial polyphonic synthesizer used 163 vacuum tubes and weighed almost 500 pounds. Conte says the musicians' union announced a prohibition because one machine could imitate an orchestra.

The union also understood the economics. Baumol and Bowen named the cost disease of the performing arts in 1966. A Haydn symphony still takes roughly the same time to perform while wages and venue costs rise. Robert Flanagan's 2008 Mellon Foundation report, *The Economic Environment of American Symphony Orchestras*, found deficits at 46 of the 63 largest US orchestras. [Stanford GSB summarized the cost problem](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease).

I think any honest anthem about new media has to carry both outcomes. New forms arrived, and some livelihoods disappeared. The history makes me hopeful. It also stops me from waving away the bill.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A medium can expand art and still damage the people working through the transition. Optimism earns trust only when it keeps the economic cost visible.</p></div>

## A model carries no reputation

Conte defines slop as "that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium where we're all just trying to reproduce existing things that are honestly just better if the people do them."

The song in our example copies the surface of a human artifact. Pretraining gives common patterns a wide path because the model learned from work that already exists. Preference tuning, which adjusts a model using human rankings of answers, can narrow the path further toward responses that raters accept.

Machines can still surprise us. AlphaGo's Move 37 against Lee Sedol in 2016 looked wrong to professional players before it became famous. Games have used procedural generation for decades. Reinforcement learning in games adds another machine-native method: a system can search inside explicit rules and rewards. Culture offers no clean win function.

Conte makes this tension concrete with a thought experiment. Train a music model only on work recorded before 1959, then ask whether it would reach the Beatles, Prince, Nirvana, Biggie, Beyoncé, or Jacob Collier by itself. He says current models have yet to show the rulebreaking and invention behind those jumps. I think current models search a style far better than they decide which cultural rule deserves to break.

*Sing Street* puts the human side plainly: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." The model carries no reputation when our song fails. A musician releasing a strange track under their own name carries the response.

Conte hates the word "content" because it flattens different kinds of work into one bucket. A tutorial for a blocked sink can deliver useful information without revealing much about its maker. Kendrick Lamar's *good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries Kendrick with it. The connection depends on the person who lived the story.

Conte also attributes the line "Art is scarcity" to a creator he names as Keredex Chang, followed by the idea that difficulty gives art weight. I could not trace the person or wording beyond the talk, so I only attribute it to Conte. The useful part is the risk test: the work gains weight when someone has something to lose.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Slop copies the surface of work whose meaning came from a person taking a risk. A model can search patterns while leaving human identity and reputation outside the artifact.</p></div>

## Give the song a memory

A native AI song could build a relationship over time instead of producing a fresh file after every prompt.

Imagine that the song remembers one listener's favorite motif and changes after each session. The composer requires one full bar of silence in every version. Listener data may influence where the silence appears, but the model has no permission to delete it.

I think this is a stronger junior AI project than another chat wrapper. The state can stay small:

```python
from typing import TypedDict

class SongState(TypedDict):
    listener_id: str
    favorite_motif: str
    last_skip_second: int
    required_silence_bars: int
    version: int

def apply_patch(state: SongState, patch: dict, actor: str):
    if "required_silence_bars" in patch and actor != "composer":
        raise PermissionError("composer owns the silence rule")
    return state | patch
```

`TypedDict` is a Python type hint that lists the fields expected in a dictionary. It helps an editor or type checker catch a misspelled field, though the program still needs the runtime guard shown in `apply_patch`.

LangGraph is a Python library for building LLM workflows as connected steps. Its `StateGraph` class can pass `SongState` between a composer step and the guard. ReAct means reasoning and acting: the model proposes an action, observes the result, then chooses another action.

Version 12 sees that the listener skipped during the silent bar. The model proposes changing `required_silence_bars` from `1` to `0`. The guard rejects the write. The model can move the silence or change the tempo, while the composer's rule survives. One small field turns [the spec into cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/).

Audit your AI pilots by erasing memory between runs. If the product feels unchanged, it is probably reproducing an existing workflow. That may still save real time. Label it honestly, then fund one experiment whose value disappears when its state is erased. This also gives human intent a way to resist [the elevator music of repeated AI loops](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/).

Conte calls Pomplamoose's career an "infinite sine wave." Around YouTube's Cosmic Panda change, the band's growth flatlined, though Conte says the cause was unclear at the time. Streaming replaced iTunes economics. COVID changed how the band recorded, and TikTok later forced another production workflow. Each shift demanded another build.

I don't know whether an evolving song is AI's equivalent of the cut. It gives us a claim we can test. By 2030, I think the AI-native work people care about most will carry a history with its audience and a human choice the model cannot erase. If celebrated AI work is still judged mainly by how well it imitates an older form, the prediction failed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A native AI work can remember a relationship and preserve a human constraint across versions. The next Méliès will stop prompting for smoother copies and give this medium its first native verb.</p></div>
