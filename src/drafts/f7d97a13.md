AI slop may be the most honest thing this medium has produced so far. It shows us where we are wasting the machine.

In 1895, the [Lumière brothers projected workers leaving their factory](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere). Early film kept borrowing the stage. In 2026, we ask a large language model for a lost Beatles song, then complain that it lacks the Beatles.

A large language model, or LLM, learns patterns from training data and predicts likely text. A familiar form gives it a clear target to imitate.

Musician and [Patreon co-founder Jack Conte](https://www.patreon.com/about) calls slop the lazy early phase of a new medium in [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=17_HcR95YBc), published 16 July 2026. People reproduce work that humans already make better.

I think the insult contains a map. Slop tells us that the assignment is old, even when the machine is new.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A lost Beatles song gives AI a task with a better human original. Slop exposes the borrowed assignment. Change the job before judging the medium.</p></div>

## A medium arrives wearing old clothes

The camera worked before cinema had a language of its own.

In 1896, Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière program. [Conte quotes him](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=284) describing a soundless, colorless world where everything was dipped in monotonous gray. The machine captured motion, but theater still set the standard.

Georges Méliès changed the job. His 1902 [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) used cuts and camera tricks to make bodies vanish. In *2001: A Space Odyssey*, one cut moves from a thrown bone to an object in orbit. Millions of years disappear in a second. A stage cannot do that.

Recorded music followed a rougher path. The American Federation of Musicians attacked recordings as ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). Its own history says talkies cost about 22,000 theater musicians their jobs. The union began a recording strike in 1942.

The 1939 Hammond Novachord brought the conflict inside the instrument. Union [rules published that year](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) prevented an orchestra from cutting players when it added the machine. Solo use in a venue that had employed musicians required local union consent. The fear had an economic base. [Baumol and Bowen's cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) explains why a quartet still needs four players while productivity rises elsewhere.

Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then made the 1940 [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/009e372a-646d-5b6e-a6b0-59f96722a1fd/the-great-dictator). Its closing speech used sound for a direct human appeal. The Beatles released [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver) in 1966 with tape loops and reversed sounds. The studio had entered the composition.

I think this history offers hope with a bill attached. A medium can find new work while people lose old livelihoods. Calling slop temporary cannot excuse unlicensed training, weak attribution or lost income.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film found the cut, and recorded music found the studio. Those discoveries opened new forms while workers absorbed real damage. Creative exploration needs an honest economic transition beside it.</p></div>

## Slop is the imitation phase

Our lost Beatles song begins with the answer and asks the model to copy its surface.

Conte offers a [thought experiment](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2828). Train a music model only on recordings made before 1959, then ask whether it would discover the Beatles, Prince, Nirvana, Biggie, Beyoncé or Jacob Collier. This is a philosophical test rather than a published benchmark. The model searches patterns that survived into its data. An artist can choose the strange move before culture has a label for it.

Risk changes how we hear that move. Conte quotes *Sing Street*: ["Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed."](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3006) He also credits Karen X Cheng with linking art to scarcity and to work that remains hard to make. The model carries no reputation when the fake Beatles track fails. A musician releases the odd choice under their own name.

Conte argues that [*good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries its force because Kendrick Lamar made it](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3287). The sound reaches us with a life attached.

I think a more faithful imitation can make slop more convincing while leaving its central weakness untouched. It deepens [the pull toward familiar output](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/) because the past still defines success.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Imitation rewards resemblance to an existing achievement. Art also carries risk and a person who answers for the choice. Better fidelity cannot supply those stakes by itself.</p></div>

## Give generation a necessary job

Keep the song, then change what the listener can do with it.

A listener opts in, hears sixteen bars and chooses one motif to carry forward. The next session must answer that motif with a fresh passage. A human composer owns one rule: every version contains a full silent measure. The system may propose, but the composer approves what anyone hears.

My take is a simple replacement test. Swap the model for a static track, a database or a rules engine. A rules engine is ordinary code that follows fixed if-then instructions. If the experience survives the swap, generation added little. If the song can respond to an open set of listener choices only through a fresh score, the model has a necessary job.

I think this is where engineering becomes creative work. The system gains shape from the boundary it must respect. Our living song can change with a listener while the silent measure keeps the composer's hand audible.

## Keep the human boundary visible

The living song needs a small contract before it needs a large model.

```python
from typing import TypedDict

class SongState(TypedDict):
    consent: bool
    chosen_motif: str
    source_license: str
    composer_rule_id: str
    silent_beats: int
```

A `TypedDict` is a Python type hint for a dictionary with named fields. It helps a type checker catch a missing key during development. At runtime it is still an ordinary dictionary, so validation and storage need real code.

[LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/graph-api) is a Python library for workflows that branch or repeat. Its `StateGraph` class connects named steps and passes shared state between them. For this song, the steps are consent, compose, validate and composer approval. A failed silent-measure check routes the score back for one bounded retry.

[ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) is a common agent pattern where a model chooses an action, reads the result and uses that observation for its next choice. Our song needs a tighter loop. The graph controls the route, ordinary code checks the silent measure, and the composer owns release. Calling every retry ReAct hides who has authority.

Compare the model with the static track and a written branch library on the same listener choices. Capture the prompt, model version, latency and cost for each run. Stop when fixed software preserves the experience, the model keeps breaking the rule, or source rights become unclear. The boundary belongs in [the spec before delegation](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/).

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>`TypedDict` makes the state legible, `StateGraph` makes the route inspectable, and ordinary validation protects the composer's rule. Licensed inputs, deletion and human approval keep authorship operational.</p></div>

## Build while the answer is open

Conte describes Pomplamoose's career as an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390).

YouTube changed discovery. Streaming weakened the band's iTunes model. COVID changed how the musicians recorded together, and vertical video forced another production setup. Each stable answer eventually moved.

I don't know whether generative AI will find a lasting creative grammar. Some media remain imitations until they disappear. By 2030, though, we should be able to point to a work whose central experience collapses when a template or fixed branch replaces the model. Human authorship should remain obvious. If every celebrated example survives the swap, the 1895 claim has failed.

I think junior engineers have unusual leverage here. The graph can stay small enough to inspect, and its failures remain visible. One rejected score can teach more about the medium than another polished chat wrapper.

The next creative breakthrough will come from someone who stops asking the model to imitate the stage and gives the machine a verb of its own.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The 1895 comparison is a bet that can fail. Replace the model and watch the living song: if the experience survives, generation was decoration. If it collapses while the composer's rule remains visible, the medium may be finding its own grammar.</p></div>
