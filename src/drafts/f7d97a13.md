In 1895, a Lumière film showed workers leaving the Lumière factory. The camera stayed still as people crossed the frame like actors on a stage.

In 2026, we ask a large language model to write a poem about grief. An LLM predicts likely pieces of text from patterns learned during training. The result is smooth, familiar, and easy to forget.

In [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc), Pomplamoose musician and Patreon co-founder Jack Conte calls slop "that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium" when people reproduce work that humans already do better.

I think he has named the phase accurately. We use a new medium as a fast copy machine, then mistake the copy for its limit.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI slop appears when a young medium copies older forms at industrial speed. Early film had the same problem before artists developed a screen language.</p></div>

## Film began as a fixed seat

The [Lumière cinematograph](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere) recorded and projected moving images. Many early films kept the camera in one place, facing action arranged like theatre.

After a Lumière programme in 1896, Maxim Gorky described ["a world without sound, without colour"](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/91ddc4e0-5576-4f98-a3b6-ba8a97703eda/section/2fe1a387-c815-45d7-9609-2a47615dc9e8), with everything "dipped in monotonous grey."

One answer came from a practical trick. Film a scene, stop the camera, change what sits before the lens, then resume. Joining the strips makes the change happen instantly on screen. That splice was an early cut. By 1902, Méliès was using similar effects in [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/).

Decades later, *2001: A Space Odyssey* showed how much meaning an edit could carry. An ape throws a bone into the air. Stanley Kubrick replaces it with a similarly shaped spacecraft, [crossing millennia in one cut](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/cefccdb2-b558-5623-9c17-72b4be7bd4c1/2001-a-space-odyssey). Film makes two distant moments collide in less than a second.

I think that collision is the lesson. Rearranging time became one part of film's grammar.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Early filmmakers placed a new camera in front of old stagecraft. Méliès created an impossible event with a splice; Kubrick later crossed millennia with an edit. Creators gave film its own language.</p></div>

## The transition changed who got paid

When synchronized dialogue reached cinemas in 1927, theatres needed fewer live musicians. The American Federation of Musicians called recordings "canned music"; [its history says about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). It formed the Music Defense League in 1930.

In May 1939, the AFM published [rules for Hammond's Novachord](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf), an electronic keyboard that raised the same fear. The rules prevented employers from adding it and cutting an orchestra's headcount. Some solo uses required local union approval.

Baumol and Bowen later named the performing arts' [cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease): a string quartet still needs four players and roughly the same time while other work gets faster. For live musicians, playback made the wage threat direct.

Charlie Chaplin kept the Little Tramp silent for years after synchronized dialogue reached cinemas. In 1940, he used speech for the closing address in [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/7-The-Great-Dictator). On 1966's [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver), the Beatles used tape loops and backward sounds until the studio became part of the composition.

I think honest AI optimism has to hold the creative opening beside the wage loss. Calling AI a medium supports a narrow claim about form. Rights and livelihoods remain urgent.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Recorded sound displaced thousands of theatre musicians, and the Novachord triggered job protections. Chaplin and the Beatles later found new uses for sound. Present rights and livelihoods remain in view.</p></div>

## Why models imitate so easily

Conte asks us to imagine a music model trained only through 1959. Could it reach Prince or Nirvana?

A large language model breaks text into tokens, small pieces of text, then predicts a likely next token from training patterns. Sampling adds variation, but each choice still comes from learned probabilities. Conte's thought experiment raises a question about invention; it supplies no experiment on current systems.

I don't know whether today's architectures can make that cultural leap. Models can surprise us. A failed line can still cost an artist reputation or work, while the model's weights remain untouched.

Conte says he hates the word "content." He argues that *good kid, m.A.A.d city* is special because Kendrick Lamar's background and pain are part of the work. The sound alone cannot explain the bond.

I think this adds a human mechanism to [The Elevator Music Already Started Playing](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). Probability helps explain why outputs converge. Risk and identity help explain why some human work escapes the average.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>LLMs generate from probable training patterns and can still surprise us. Human experimentation carries social exposure. A song or poem reaches us through its form and its maker.</p></div>

## Give generation a harder test

Return to the grief poem. The easy demo asks an LLM to imitate a famous poet. It may produce a convincing surface while adding little beyond an authored template.

A better prototype begins with a living poet who opts in and approves the source set. A reader contributes one image from their day. The system replies to that image under a poet-approved release rule.

Call this the **non-substitution test**: generation earns a role when live input creates a better experience than a serious authored alternative.

Use two baselines. First, compare model replies blindly with a poet's replies to the same images from consenting readers. Then compare live generation with a branching version made from the poet's fragments. Ask whether each reply connects to the reader's image. Set cost and latency ceilings first.

Generation has to match the poet's quality and beat the authored branch in live use. If it fails either test, ship the authored work. A genuine win earns another test.

Start with plain code. ReAct is a loop where the model reasons, calls a tool, and reads the result. If review branches, LangGraph's `StateGraph` connects steps through shared state; Python's `TypedDict` lists the state fields for a type checker. Frameworks are plumbing. Consent still needs code and tests.

My take is to write the release rule before choosing a framework. The poet can block publication or require output deletion. Reader input disappears after the session unless the reader approves storage. This is the creative version of [When Software Becomes Disposable, Trust Becomes Luxury](/posts/when-software-becomes-disposable/): cheap production increases the value of judgment.

By 2030, I would drop the medium thesis if serious authored systems keep matching generative creative experiences in tests like this. The deadline keeps the claim falsifiable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Compare generation with a responsive authored baseline. Set the measure and limits first, then give creators release control. Continue only when generation earns its cost and risk.</p></div>

## Pomplamoose kept rebuilding

Pomplamoose found an audience on YouTube and sold MP3s through iTunes. Its growth flattened around YouTube's Cosmic Panda redesign, although Conte says the cause was unclear. Streaming weakened the sales model. COVID changed recording, and vertical video demanded another setup.

Conte calls this the ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390). Each dip broke a working method, and Pomplamoose rebuilt.

I think this is the useful emotional register for AI. Slop is the symptom of an unfinished medium, and some media never escape imitation. A failed test should block release.

Run the poem test. If authored branches work equally well, ship them. When live generation creates a connection those branches cannot hold, we have one clue about where AI's first cut may be.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Pomplamoose rebuilt after each platform shock. Slop becomes transitional only if new work survives the non-substitution test. A result that holds up is one sign of AI developing its own creative language.</p></div>
