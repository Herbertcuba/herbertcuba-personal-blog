# AI is still filming the stage

In 1895, Auguste and Louis Lumière, the French inventors behind the Cinématographe camera-projector, helped bring moving pictures to paying audiences. One year later, Russian writer Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière programme and described [“a world without sound, without colour”](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/91ddc4e0-5576-4f98-a3b6-ba8a97703eda/section/2fe1a387-c815-45d7-9609-2a47615dc9e8), with everything “dipped in monotonous grey.” He was judging a real achievement before artists had learned how to use it fully.

In 2026, we ask a large language model, software that predicts text from patterns in training data, for a poem. We ask image-generating software for a hand-drawn look, then mistake the copy for the final form of AI-generated creative work. I think we are making the same mistake.

Jack Conte, a musician in the duo Pomplamoose and co-founder of Patreon, a membership platform for creators, calls slop [“that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium”](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=844), when people reproduce things humans already make better. My take is that slop reveals how little of the medium we have explored. History offers permission to search, though it offers no promise that the search will succeed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI slop shows that imitation is easy. The useful question is what artists can make with AI that older media cannot provide.</p></div>

## Every new medium borrows an old shape

Conte describes early filmmakers placing the camera where a theatre audience would sit. Georges Méliès, a French stage magician who became a filmmaker, broke that arrangement by stopping the camera, changing the scene and starting it again. By 1902, his film [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) used cuts, joins between separately recorded shots, and visual tricks to bend place and time. That sequence could exist only on film.

Recorded music made a similar turn. On their 1966 album [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver), the Beatles, the British rock group, used tape loops, short pieces of magnetic recording joined so they repeat, along with sounds played backwards. The studio became part of the composition instead of merely storing a performance.

Charlie Chaplin, the actor-director famous for silent films, took another route. After resisting synchronized dialogue for years, he made speech central to his 1940 film [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/7-The-Great-Dictator) and its humanist appeal against dictatorship. I think both examples matter: one found a new way to compose; the other found when a new capacity strengthened the artist’s purpose.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Cuts, studio manipulation and deliberate speech gave artists new choices. The tools became media when they stopped serving only as storage for older forms.</p></div>

## The imitation phase still sends a bill

Calling slop transitional does not make its damage imaginary. The [American Federation of Musicians](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/), a United States musicians’ union, says about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs when talkies, films with synchronized sound, began arriving in 1927. They replaced the live accompaniment required by silent films, removing paid work before a new artistic language had settled.

The fear continued with electronic instruments. In 1939, [the union ruled](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) that adding a Novachord, an early electronic keyboard that could play several notes at once, or a Hammond electric organ could not reduce an ensemble’s headcount. The rule addressed a fear that one player could replace sounds previously supplied by several people.

Economists William Baumol and William Bowen named part of this pressure in 1966: performing-arts [“cost disease”](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease). A live symphony still needs roughly the same human time while other industries become more productive, making performed work relatively expensive beside cheap copies. I think optimism about AI becomes empty if creators alone absorb that difference. My standard is that when a company uses creators’ work to build a system, creators should agree to the terms, promised payment should arrive, and a named person should handle disputes and remove material when usage rights cannot be shown.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A medium can open new artistic territory while closing existing jobs. Exploration earns support only when its economic terms let source creators know what is used, receive what was promised, and challenge misuse through a real decision-maker.</p></div>

## The artist carries the consequence

Conte asks whether a music model trained only on recordings available through 1959 could have reached the Beatles’ later experiments. I don’t know, and the question does not prove that a model cannot surprise us. It exposes a harder distinction: a model can produce an unusual pattern, while a person releases a choice under their own name and risks ridicule, failure or a damaged career.

That human connection changes the value of the work. Conte points to *good kid, m.A.A.d city*, American rapper Kendrick Lamar’s 2012 album about adolescence in Compton, and argues that its meaning comes from Kendrick’s life and pain as well as its sound. AI may participate in art, but fluent output does not carry that history by itself.

So the hopeful claim needs a test that can fail. By 2030, widely valued AI art should offer an experience whose defining effect disappears when AI is removed, even when compared with the best fixed, human-authored alternative. If celebrated AI work still consists mainly of cheaper imitations of songs, illustrations and voices, then the 1895 analogy has failed and we should describe the tools as production systems rather than a new creative medium.

Using an adaptive AI system only to fake an illustration is like using the holodeck in *Star Trek*, the long-running science-fiction franchise, merely to hang a poster. The fictional room builds a responsive world around its visitor. I think the real opportunity lies in that responsiveness, guided by an artist who chooses the boundaries and accepts the consequences. AI will find its form when the machine makes a new experience possible and a human being makes it matter.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Creative progress needs an effect that depends on AI, an artist who answers for the choice, and fair terms for source creators. Without all three, slop remains imitation rather than arrival.</p></div>
