In 1895, the Lumière brothers [projected workers leaving a factory](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere). In 2026, we prompt a large language model to write a poem. Both moves use a new machine to copy an older form.

A large language model, or LLM, learns patterns in training data and predicts the next likely piece. I think imitation feels natural at first because it gives us a target we already understand. Ask for a lost Beatles song and everyone knows roughly what success should sound like.

Musician and Patreon co-founder Jack Conte calls this slop in his SXSW talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=17_HcR95YBc). His definition is useful: slop is the lazy phase after a new medium arrives, when people use it to reproduce work humans already make better.

That framing lowers the temperature, although creators can still lose income or control while the rest of us learn. Slop may be temporary. Its costs arrive now.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>LLMs begin with imitation because familiar work gives us an easy target. Slop appears when the copy becomes the whole ambition.</p></div>

## Film needed the cut

Early film had moving images, but many filmmakers still framed actors as if the camera were sitting in row ten of a theatre.

In 1896, Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière programme and described ["a world without sound, without colour"](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/91ddc4e0-5576-4f98-a3b6-ba8a97703eda/section/2fe1a387-c815-45d7-9609-2a47615dc9e8), with everything "dipped in monotonous grey." The machine had arrived before its artistic grammar.

Georges Méliès changed that by stopping the camera, altering the scene, and restarting it. A person could vanish inside the cut. His 1902 [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) made editing part of the work.

In [*2001: A Space Odyssey*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/cefccdb2-b558-5623-9c17-72b4be7bd4c1/2001-a-space-odyssey), Kubrick cuts from a bone in the air to a spacecraft. Millennia disappear between two frames. Theatre can suggest that jump. Film performs it.

I think most AI creativity still has its camera in row ten. Better prompts produce cleaner copies, much like a sharper lens produces a better recording of the same stage. The medium begins to move when the machine changes the form itself.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film found its grammar when artists made editing part of the art. AI remains in an early phase while imitation is the main measure of quality.</p></div>

## New media send bills early

The history of creative technology includes workers who paid for the transition long before audiences understood the new form.

The American Federation of Musicians called recorded accompaniment ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). Its history says about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs after talkies arrived in 1927. The union created the Music Defense League in 1930 and called a recording strike in 1942.

The Hammond Novachord caused another labour fight in 1939. Union [rules published that May](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) protected orchestra headcount when the instrument was added. Solo use in a venue that had employed musicians required local consent. The document is more careful than the popular claim that the union simply banned the synthesizer.

[Baumol and Bowen's cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) explains the pressure. A quartet still needs four players while productivity rises elsewhere. Faster playback changes the music.

Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then used speech for the final address in the 1940 [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/1-The-Great-Dictator). The Beatles released [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver) in 1966 with backwards sound and studio experiments that exceeded a plain live capture.

I think this history gives AI builders two duties. Explore the medium, and carry the labour cost into the design. Licensed source material and a human release decision belong in the first prototype.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Creative tools can displace people before artists find their best use. Exploration earns trust when rights and release authority are built into the work from the start.</p></div>

## Artists risk their name

Conte offers a useful thought experiment. Train a music model only on recordings made through 1959, then ask it to reach the Beatles. He presents this as a boundary test rather than a measured benchmark.

A model can recombine patterns from the past. Cultural invention asks someone to choose a break before the audience rewards it. *Sing Street* puts the cost plainly: ["Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed."](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3006)

I don't know whether future models can cross that line on their own. I think the harder problem is that a model has no reputation at stake when it emits a bad chord. An artist can spend years earning trust and lose some of it with one release.

Conte uses Kendrick Lamar's *good kid, m.A.A.d city* to show why he dislikes the word "content." The record reaches us with Kendrick's history attached. Strip away that relationship and its meaning changes.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Models can produce surprise, while artists put a public identity behind a choice. Human connection changes how creative work is valued.</p></div>

## Give generation a job

A junior AI engineer can test the argument with a call-and-response song by a living composer. A listener plays a short motif, and the system replies inside the composer's rules. Generation has one possible job: respond to input the composer never heard during production.

Run the **Irreducible Generation Test** before choosing the architecture:

| Version | Can it answer a new motif? | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Static recording | No | If listeners still get the intended experience, drop the interactive brief |
| Authored branches | Only motifs the team anticipated | Ship branches when held-out inputs rarely need a new response |
| Generative model | It can attempt an original reply | Keep it only when blinded tests show a clear gain over branches |

The static recording fails the response requirement by definition, which proves only that the piece needs runtime interaction. Authored branches may still handle the useful cases. Generation earns its place when unseen motifs occur often enough and the model answers them well.

The implementation can stay small. A `TypedDict` describes the Python state fields, such as the motif and release approval. LangGraph's `StateGraph` passes that state through named steps. ReAct lets a model choose a tool, read the result, and choose again. This song has known steps, so a fixed graph is easier to test. ReAct helps only when the model faces a real tool choice.

Set the evidence before coding. Hold back motifs from development, then ask the composer to score branch and model replies blind. Record response time and marginal cost. A named product owner stops the pilot when the model misses the threshold.

Release needs a gate. Store the source licence and provider data terms. Keep `rights_clear` and `composer_approved` in runtime state, then block release until both are true. If the terms change, roll back to authored branches. I think this is the practical link to [treating the spec as the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/): human intent stays visible while execution remains bounded.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Compare generation with a static version and authored branches. Keep the model when it wins on held-out inputs under rules a human owner can enforce.</p></div>

## Change comes in waves

Conte calls Pomplamoose's cycle of creative resets an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390).

A YouTube change flattened the band's growth. Streaming weakened its iTunes sales model. COVID changed how the band recorded. Vertical video forced another production rebuild. Each wave removed a working setup and demanded a new one.

I think his story supports hope with conditions. Some technologies never find a durable creative form, so the 1895 analogy remains useful only while we test it.

By 2030, look at the work people value most. If its main achievement is a convincing imitation of a human drawing or recorded song, slop may have been the ceiling. If removing live generation breaks the experience, the medium has started to find its grammar.

Critics should keep tracing whose work entered the training set and who gets paid. Builders should stop funding demos that cannot beat a recording or a branch table. Otherwise we get more of the [cultural elevator music already filling the internet](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/).

Film left the stage when the cut became part of the form. AI leaves 1895 when generation does the same.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Change can lead to a new creative form, though history offers no guarantee. AI earns its future when valued work depends on generation and creators keep authority over release.</p></div>
