In 1895, the Lumière brothers introduced the [Cinématographe](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere). Their train film reached audiences in 1896. Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière screening that year. [Conte quotes him](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=284) describing "a world without sound, without color," with everything dipped in monotonous gray.

In 2026, we ask a large language model to write a lost Beatles song and call the result AI creativity. A large language model, or LLM, learns from huge datasets and predicts what comes next. The machine can sound convincing. The assignment is gray.

I tried to treat the flood of synthetic work as a model-quality problem. I think that diagnosis misses the more interesting failure. In his SXSW talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=841), musician and Patreon co-founder Jack Conte calls slop the lazy phase of a new medium, when people use it to reproduce things humans already make better.

That framing changes the job. Disgust can identify bad work, but it cannot discover a new form.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Our fake Beatles song uses a new machine to copy an old artifact. Slop is what that imitation phase looks like at scale, and better models alone will not teach us the medium's own grammar.</p></div>

## Every medium starts with borrowed grammar

Early filmmakers put a theater stage inside the camera frame because theater was the grammar they knew.

The camera often sat in one place while actors performed toward painted sets. Gorky's criticism was reasonable because early film gave him a thinner version of a play.

Georges Méliès began pulling the frame apart. His 1902 film [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.loc.gov/item/00694283/) used cuts and camera tricks to jump between places. Decades later, *2001: A Space Odyssey* cut from a bone to a spacecraft and crossed millions of years in an instant. That sci-fi cut became an argument about human history. A stage curtain could never do the same job.

Recorded music followed a similar path. Conte shows American Federation of Musicians ads from the 1930s that called recordings ["canned music"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=710). The union [began a commercial recording ban in 1942](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). Movie-house musicians faced real displacement.

Then the studio became part of composition. On [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver) in 1966, the Beatles used tape loops and reversed sounds that were difficult to reproduce on stage. Conte attributes a lovely phrase to George Martin: the studio became "a new canvas on which they had to paint." I could verify Conte saying it, though I could not find Martin's exact wording in a primary source.

Charlie Chaplin took years to accept synchronized dialogue. His first full talkie, [*The Great Dictator*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Dictator), arrived in 1940. The closing speech used recorded dialogue for a humanist appeal that traveled beyond one theater.

The 1939 Hammond Novachord, an early commercial synthesizer, held 163 vacuum tubes and weighed almost 500 pounds. [Conte recounts](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=1830) a union prohibition driven by fear that it could replace an orchestra. Baumol and Bowen later named the [cost disease of the performing arts](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease): a string quartet still needs roughly the same human time to perform while other costs rise.

I think optimism that skips the economic damage is cheap. People lose income during these transitions. The history only tells us that creators sometimes find a form worth keeping after the damage has already begun.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film found editing, while recorded music found the studio. Both discoveries opened creative choices that their predecessor could not offer, but working artists carried real costs during the search.</p></div>

## The fake Beatles song is the wrong test

Our lost Beatles song asks the model to travel toward a destination humans already reached.

Conte proposes a sharper thought experiment. Train a music model only on work recorded before 1959. Could it reach the Beatles, then Prince or Nirvana, without hearing what came between? We cannot run the historical version of that experiment, but the gap exposes a limit in how we discuss model creativity.

A model searches patterns left by earlier work. I think a person brings something the model lacks: exposure. [Conte cites Hasan Minhaj](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2955), who describes comedy as speech outside the normal range of social acceptance. He pairs it with a line from *Sing Street*: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed."

When our fake Beatles song fails, the LLM risks no reputation and the engineer can close the tab. A musician releases the strange choice under their own name and lives with the response.

This is also why Conte hates the word "content." [His Kendrick Lamar example](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3317) lands because *good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries the life of its maker. Its value reaches beyond a kick drum or chord change. Conte attributes the compact version to Karen X Cheng: ["Art is scarcity"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3089). Once an effect becomes an easy camera preset, the difficulty moves somewhere else. I explored that pull in [The Elevator Music Already Started Playing](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/).

My take is that AI can take part in art. It still needs a person willing to choose a strange direction and remain visible beside it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Models can search an enormous space of learned patterns. Cultural jumps still depend on people who attach their name and reputation to a choice that may be ridiculed.</p></div>

## Give the song a job a recording cannot do

The same Beatles prompt becomes more useful once we stop asking for another fixed track.

Imagine a listener hears a short song and chooses one motif to carry forward. The next version composes around that choice. A human composer owns one hard rule: every version must contain a full bar of silence. The model can move that silence within the score, while deletion remains outside its control.

Now the song has a relationship with one listener across time. Each play changes the next one, while the composer's boundary survives. A static recording cannot do that.

Memory by itself proves very little. Games have remembered players for decades, and a database can store the chosen motif. The model earns its place only if fresh generation changes the next listen in a way that a small set of prewritten branches cannot match.

I think the first useful experiment is small. Give twenty opt-in listeners the adaptive song and compare it with a version built from prewritten branches. Stop if listeners cannot hear the difference, the composer's rule breaks, or anyone loses control of their data. That test gives a junior AI engineer something better than a polished demo: a result that can disagree with the idea.

This is where [the spec becomes the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/). The human contribution is the boundary and the decision about which variation deserves to survive.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A generative medium earns its place when generation changes the audience experience. In the living-song test, one listener choice shapes the next score while a human composer's rule remains intact.</p></div>

## Change moves like an infinite sine wave

Conte describes Pomplamoose's career as an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390).

YouTube changed how the band was discovered. Streaming weakened the iTunes model. COVID broke their way of recording in one room. Vertical video changed the camera setup again. Each shift forced a rebuild, and some of those rebuilds hurt.

Change means another turn of the wave. It offers no promise that a career, a company, or a medium will survive. Conte has enough skin in the game to hold anger and curiosity at the same time. Creators deserve consent and compensation. Engineers still need room to explore.

I don't know whether the living song will matter by 2030. I think AI-native work will require generation to alter the experience while a human choice remains legible through every version. If the celebrated work of 2030 is still judged by how closely it copies an older artifact, then we failed to find the medium.

A junior engineer can spend this year polishing the fake Beatles track. I would rather see that engineer test an experience a recording could never become. The camera already works. Our generation has to discover the cut.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>New platforms keep breaking old creative patterns, and every rebuild carries risk. The useful response to AI slop is a bounded search for experiences an older medium cannot produce, with human authorship and creator rights kept visible throughout.</p></div>
