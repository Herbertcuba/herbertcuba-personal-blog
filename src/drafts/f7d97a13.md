In 1895, the Lumière brothers introduced the Cinématographe. Their train film joined the public program in 1896, and Maxim Gorky described the screen as "a world without sound, without color."

In 2026, we ask a large language model to write a Beatles song and call the result AI creativity. The mistake feels familiar.

I tried to treat the gray flood of AI output as a model-quality problem. I think the diagnosis is too small. Jack Conte, the Pomplamoose artist and Patreon co-founder, gives the phase a useful name in his SXSW talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc). Slop is the lazy period when a new medium copies existing things that people already make better.

<div class="article-tldr"><span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span><p>AI slop is a young medium borrowing old forms. History gives builders a better response than praise or panic: look for work that depends on the medium itself.</p></div>

## The camera came first

A young medium usually begins with the grammar its makers already know.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Early film borrowed the stage until filmmakers learned to move through time and space. Our Beatles prompt has the same limit because it asks AI to conceal every sign of the machine.</p></div>

Many early narrative films placed a fixed camera in front of theatrical sets. The actors performed toward the lens as if an audience sat behind it. Gorky's gray world was a fair description of what he saw.

Georges Méliès used splices and camera tricks in *A Trip to the Moon* in 1902. Editing let a story jump between places. Decades later, *2001: A Space Odyssey* threw a bone into the air and replaced it with a spacecraft. Millions of years passed in a fraction of a second. That sci-fi edit does something a stage cannot hold.

Recorded music found its own use later. The Beatles used tape loops and reversed sounds on *Revolver* in 1966, producing music that was hard to recreate live. Conte attributes the phrase "a new canvas on which they had to paint" to producer George Martin. Tape had become part of the composition.

Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years. In 1940, *The Great Dictator* ended with a humanist speech built for recorded sound. He used the part of the medium he had once resisted.

I think slop deserves criticism. It also gives us a clue. When our prompt says "write a Beatles song," better vocals improve the imitation while the assignment stays inside 1966.

## The transition sent a real bill

A hopeful history of new media still has to account for the people who lost work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>New media can create possibilities while destroying jobs. AI builders need to hold the creative opening and the economic damage in view at the same time.</p></div>

The American Federation of Musicians called recorded sound "canned music" in 1930s campaigns. By 1930, the union estimated that sound film had displaced 22,000 moviehouse musicians. The union later began a recording ban in 1942, with label agreements ending it by 1944.

The Hammond Novachord made the fear physical. Introduced in 1939, the early commercial polyphonic synthesizer used 163 vacuum tubes and weighed almost 500 pounds. Conte recounts that the union prohibited members from using it because one machine could imitate an orchestra.

The fear had an economic base. Baumol and Bowen named the performing arts' "cost disease" in 1966. A Haydn symphony still takes about the same time to perform even as wages and other costs rise. [Stanford GSB explains](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) why orchestras remain exposed to that pressure.

Conte starts his talk angry. Artists' work entered training systems without their consent. Many received no payment or credit. He also feels drawn to the new medium because it may open another way to make art. Both reactions are honest.

Our Beatles prompt avoids the harder part of art: risk. Conte asks whether a music model trained only on recordings made before 1959 would discover the Beatles, or whether it could later reach Nirvana. I think current models can search a style space far better than they can decide which cultural rule deserves to break.

*Sing Street* says it plainly: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." The model simply receives another prompt after failure, while a musician's name stays attached to the song. The same human connection is why *good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries Kendrick Lamar's life with it. Its weight exceeds a pattern of drums and chords.

## Give the song a job a recording cannot do

The Beatles imitation becomes more useful when the assignment changes.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Build a living song that remembers one listener while preserving a composer's rule. A small state and a code check make that idea concrete for a junior AI engineer.</p></div>

Imagine a song stored as a text score before audio rendering. It changes for one listener. After each play, the listener keeps one motif. The next version remembers it. A human composer also requires one full bar of silence in every version, and the model can move that bar without deleting it.

The graph can stay small. This snippet assumes `llm` already points to a configured chat model:

```python
from typing import Optional, TypedDict
from langgraph.graph import END, START, StateGraph

class SongState(TypedDict):
    listener_motif: str
    required_silence_bars: int
    draft: str
    rejection: Optional[str]
    attempts: int


def compose(state: SongState) -> dict:
    feedback = state["rejection"] or "Write the next version."
    prompt = (
        f"Keep motif: {state['listener_motif']}. "
        f"Include <silence:{state['required_silence_bars']}bar>. "
        f"Previous draft: {state['draft']}. "
        f"Feedback: {feedback}"
    )
    return {
        "draft": llm.invoke(prompt).content,
        "attempts": state["attempts"] + 1,
    }


def guard(state: SongState) -> dict:
    marker = f"<silence:{state['required_silence_bars']}bar>"
    rejection = None if marker in state["draft"] else f"Add {marker}."
    return {"rejection": rejection}


def route(state: SongState) -> str:
    if state["rejection"] is None:
        return "accept"
    if state["attempts"] >= 3:
        return "stop"
    return "revise"


builder = StateGraph(SongState)
builder.add_node("compose", compose)
builder.add_node("guard", guard)
builder.add_edge(START, "compose")
builder.add_edge("compose", "guard")
builder.add_conditional_edges(
    "guard",
    route,
    {"revise": "compose", "accept": END, "stop": END},
)
graph = builder.compile()
```

`TypedDict` names the fields that belong in `SongState`. Your editor can flag `listener_motif` when you misspell it, while the `guard` function handles the check during execution. Python leaves runtime enforcement to the program.

[LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/graph-api) is a Python library for workflows made of connected steps. `StateGraph` is the part that lets those steps read and update shared state, the small record carried between them. In this graph, the route loops a rejected draft back to `compose` and stops after three attempts.

[ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629), short for reasoning and acting, is a pattern where a model alternates reasoning with actions, then uses the observed result to choose the next action. This song loop borrows that shape. The guard reads the model's draft and returns an observation. The next model call uses that rejection as feedback.

I think this is a stronger junior AI project than another chat wrapper. The boundary starts in [the spec](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/), with one bar of silence written before the first model call. A generated draft either contains the marker or the graph rejects it.

## Run the imitation audit

A simple portfolio check can show which projects depend on the medium.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Delete the memory from an AI pilot and see whether its value survives. Work that depends on persistent state gives you a better place to explore than another faster copy of a familiar workflow.</p></div>

Open your pilot list. Put each project in one of two columns. The first reproduces an existing human workflow, such as drafting slides faster. The second depends on a capability that disappears when you remove the model's memory or its ability to act over time.

Our song belongs in the second column. Delete `SongState` after every play and the listener relationship disappears. The output may still sound polished, but the reason to return has gone.

I think every AI team needs at least one experiment in that column. Production work can keep paying the bills while a smaller project tests what this medium can carry.

Conte calls Pomplamoose's career an "infinite sine wave." YouTube changed and streaming replaced the iTunes model. COVID broke the band's recording setup. Vertical video forced another rebuild. Pomplamoose kept going through each change.

AI may compress that cycle. A new pattern can become a preset as soon as it spreads. I wrote about the pull toward generic output in [The Elevator Music Already Started Playing](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). Exploration has to continue after the first breakthrough becomes easy.

I don't know whether a living song is the form AI will be remembered for. By 2030, I think the defining AI work will carry a history with its audience and preserve a human decision through every model call. If celebrated AI work is still judged mainly by how well it imitates an older form, my prediction failed.

The next cut will come from builders willing to stop filming the stage.
