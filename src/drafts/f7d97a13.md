In 1895, the [Lumière brothers projected workers leaving their factory](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere). In 2026, we ask a large language model for a lost Beatles song. We are making the same mistake: giving a new machine a finished human form to copy.

I tried to treat AI slop as a quality problem. Better models seemed like the cure. Then musician and [Patreon co-founder Jack Conte](https://www.patreon.com/about) gave a [58-minute SXSW talk in July 2026](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc) and put a year on our confusion: 1895.

A large language model, or LLM, learns patterns in training data and predicts likely pieces of text. A familiar poem or song gives it a clear statistical target.

Conte calls slop [the lazy early phase of a new medium](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=841), when people reproduce work that humans already make better. I think that frame gives junior engineers a useful response. Slop reveals where we aim the machine, rather than where the medium can go.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A lost Beatles song gives AI a task with a better human original. The 1895 frame treats that failure as a clue: change the job before you write off the medium.</p></div>

## Machines arrive before their grammar

The first camera operators had a machine for motion and a mental model built by theater.

In 1896, Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière program. [Conte quotes him](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=284) describing a soundless, colorless world dipped in monotonous gray. The camera worked. The result still felt thin.

Georges Méliès pushed against that frame in the 1902 [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/). Cuts and camera tricks let bodies vanish. Decades later, *2001: A Space Odyssey* cut from a thrown bone to an object in orbit. Millions of years passed in a fraction of a second. The cut could carry an idea that no stage change could hold.

Recorded music arrived with a much harsher bill. The American Federation of Musicians attacked recordings as ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). Its history says talkies cost about 22,000 theater musicians their jobs. The union began a recording strike in 1942.

The 1939 Hammond Novachord brought that conflict inside the instrument. Union [rules](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) kept an orchestra from reducing its player count when it added the machine. Solo use in a venue that had employed musicians required local union consent. [Baumol and Bowen's cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) explains the pressure: a quartet still needs four players while productivity rises elsewhere.

Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then made the 1940 [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/009e372a-646d-5b6e-a6b0-59f96722a1fd/the-great-dictator) with its humanist closing speech. The Beatles released [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver) in 1966 with tape loops and reversed sounds that existed because the studio became part of the composition.

I think the history is hopeful and uncomfortable. New media can create work while people lose income during the change. Calling slop temporary cannot excuse exploitation.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film found the cut after years of borrowing the stage. Recorded music found studio techniques while musicians absorbed real economic damage. A young medium and a fair transition have to be built together.</p></div>

## Slop copies the answer

Our lost Beatles song begins with a result and asks the model to reverse-engineer its surface.

Conte offers a [thought experiment](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2828). Train a music model only on recordings made before 1959, then ask whether it would discover the Beatles, Prince, Nirvana, Biggie, Beyoncé or Jacob Collier. This is a philosophical test rather than a published benchmark. Models search patterns that survived into their data. Artists can choose the move that looks foolish before a culture has a name for it.

Conte quotes *Sing Street*: ["Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed."](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3006) He also credits Karen X Cheng with calling art scarce and linking its value to [what is hard to make](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3090). A model loses no standing when our fake Beatles song collapses. A musician has to release the strange choice under their own name.

Human connection changes how we hear the artifact. Conte says [*good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries its force because Kendrick Lamar made it](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3287). Knowing who made it changes what we hear.

I think higher fidelity can make this kind of slop more convincing without making it more alive. It strengthens [the pull toward familiar output](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/) because resemblance to an existing past remains the engineering target.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The lost Beatles prompt rewards resemblance to an existing achievement. A person making art has to choose the odd move under their own name and live with the response.</p></div>

## Give generation a necessary job

Keep the song. Change what the listener can do with it.

A listener opts in, hears sixteen bars and chooses one motif to carry forward. The next session must answer it with a fresh passage. A human composer owns one rule: every version contains a full silent measure. Removing it sends the score back for repair.

Games have saved state for decades, and procedural music predates LLMs. State alone proves little. Replace the model with a static track, database or rules engine. If the experience survives, generation has no necessary job.

A Python `TypedDict` lists expected dictionary keys, such as listener consent and the chosen motif. It helps a type checker catch missing fields. Storage and runtime checks still need code.

[LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/graph-api) is a Python library for workflows that branch or repeat. Its `StateGraph` connects steps and passes shared state. Ours would load the motif, request a JSON score and validate the silent measure. A [checkpointer and store](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/persistence) can retain approved history, with deletion tied to consent.

ReAct alternates model reasoning with tool actions, feeding each observation into the next decision. This song uses a bounded validation loop. Calling every loop ReAct hides the design.

I think a credible first test fits inside two weeks: one composer, one engineer, twenty opt-in sessions and a €500 inference ceiling. Compare the model with eight authored branches. Stop if those branches cover every choice or the model keeps breaking the rule. Scale when generation creates valid responses the baseline cannot hold. Write the boundary before the first call, the same discipline as [writing the spec before delegation](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/).

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The living song earns generation only when a static track or authored branch cannot produce the same response. Typed state, consent and a deterministic score check keep the experiment small enough to test and easy enough to stop.</p></div>

## Build while the answer is open

Conte describes Pomplamoose's career as an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390). YouTube changed discovery. Streaming weakened the band's iTunes model. COVID changed how the musicians recorded together, and vertical video forced another production setup.

Change kept arriving after the band had learned the previous system. That history offers courage without a guarantee.

I don't know whether generative AI will find a lasting creative grammar. Some media never escape imitation. By 2030, though, we should be able to point to a work that loses its central experience when a template or fixed branch replaces the model. Human authorship should remain obvious. If every celebrated example survives the replacement, the 1895 claim has failed.

I think this is a good junior-engineer problem. The graph can stay small enough to inspect, and its failures remain visible. You can learn more from one rejected score than from another polished chat wrapper.

That is a better use of the next model call than asking for another lost Beatles song.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Replace the model with fixed software and watch what happens to the living song. If the experience survives, the 1895 claim fails. If it collapses while the composer's rule remains visible, we may have found a useful direction.</p></div>
