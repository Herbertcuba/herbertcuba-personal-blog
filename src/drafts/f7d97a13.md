# AI slop is a medium learning to speak

In 1895, the Lumière brothers filmed a train arriving at a station and called it cinema. A year later, Maxim Gorky saw one of their programmes and described [“a world without sound, without colour,”](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/91ddc4e0-5576-4f98-a3b6-ba8a97703eda/section/2fe1a387-c815-45d7-9609-2a47615dc9e8) with everything “dipped in monotonous grey.” The machine could reproduce motion, yet life seemed to have drained out of it.

In 2026, we ask a large language model to write a poem, or ask an image model to mimic a hand-drawn frame, then call the result AI content. I think we are repeating the early filmmakers’ mistake. We are judging a young medium by how closely it copies forms built for human hands and cameras.

Jack Conte, a musician and Patreon co-founder, calls slop [“that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium”](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=844), when people reproduce work humans already make better. My take is that slop is useful evidence of immaturity. It becomes evidence of failure if imitation never gives way to a native grammar, the creative possibilities that exist because the medium can generate.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI slop looks like an early medium borrowing familiar forms. The hopeful reading survives only if generation eventually creates experiences with their own creative grammar.</p></div>

## New media begin with borrowed moves

Early film placed a fixed camera in front of staged action because theatre supplied the available mental model. Georges Méliès, a magician, stopped the camera, changed the scene and restarted it. A person could suddenly appear inside an empty box. By 1902, [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) used such cuts to create events a stage could never hold. The cut gave filmmakers control over sequence and time.

Recorded music followed a similar path. When talkies arrived in 1927, about 22,000 theatre musicians lost work, and the [American Federation of Musicians](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/) called recorded accompaniment “canned music.” By 1966, the Beatles were using tape loops and backwards sound on [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver). The studio had become part of composition.

Charlie Chaplin shows the same shift from the artist’s side. He resisted synchronised dialogue for years, then put the human voice at the centre of 1940’s [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/7-The-Great-Dictator). The final speech used sound as the film’s emotional engine.

I think history gives us a pattern whose value depends on what happens next. A medium earns cultural weight when artists discover moves inherited forms could never supply.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film found the cut, recorded music found the studio, and Chaplin found a purpose for speech. Each medium grew when artists discovered an expressive move unique to its machinery.</p></div>

## The transition sends a bill

The Novachord made the economic conflict explicit. In 1939, AFM rules required ensembles to keep their existing size when adding a Novachord or Hammond organ. Baumol and Bowen later called the underlying pressure [cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease): a quartet still needs four players for the same duration while recorded and synthetic alternatives scale cheaply.

That history gives today’s AI experiments a clear responsibility. A team should begin with material it owns or has licensed and record whether its maker consented. Someone must be accountable for the generated result, with a path back when the experiment causes legal or human harm. I think exploration and protection have to mature together.

Conte’s deeper argument concerns risk. He asks whether a music model trained only on recordings available through 1959 could somehow reach the Beatles or Prince. I don’t know. Models can produce unfamiliar combinations, especially when people guide the search, so their capacity for surprise deserves serious attention.

Yet the person who releases a strange work carries consequences the model cannot feel. A career can stall and an audience can walk away. Conte explains that *good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries weight for him through Kendrick Lamar’s life in Compton and the pain behind the record. When output becomes abundant, the identity and risk behind the work grow more visible. I think that human connection will remain part of art’s value.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Creative progress can coexist with displacement. Responsible exploration uses authorised material and names a human who answers for the result. The maker’s risk remains part of the work’s value.</p></div>

## Give the analogy an expiry date

Hope needs a test. By 2030, I think we should be able to name works whose central experience collapses when generation is removed. Call this the substitution test.

For each candidate, identify the exact AI-dependent operation. Compare it with a version built from fixed, human-authored material. Ask a blinded audience to complete the same task or describe the experience, with a success condition chosen in advance. If the fixed version preserves the central experience, generation accelerated production. If the system’s real-time composition or reaction changes the response as predicted, we have evidence of a native form.

The science-fiction film *Arrival* offers the right image. Its circular written language changes how its user experiences time. Treating those symbols as decorative handwriting would erase the mechanism that makes them powerful. An AI-native work needs that kind of dependency: generation changes what the audience can experience.

The strongest technical objection is that artists already use models inside larger human-led systems. A new form may emerge from that partnership, and the test should credit the whole system. It still has to show an observable capability beyond conventional editing.

Conte describes creative careers as an “infinite sine wave,” with each platform shift forcing artists to learn again. The current wave asks creators to learn the medium while defending their stake in it. Endless imitation would weaken the 1895 analogy by 2030. A body of work that depends on generation would strengthen it.

The train arriving at the station taught us that motion could be recorded. AI’s first native artwork will teach us why a world should generate at all.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The 1895 analogy has to earn its survival. By 2030, the substitution test should reveal whether generation creates a distinct audience experience or simply makes familiar production faster.</p></div>
