In 1895, the Lumière brothers filmed a train arriving at a station and called it cinema. Critics called it lifeless. In 2026, we prompt an LLM to write a poem and call it AI-generated content. We are making the same mistake.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every new creative medium starts by imitating the old one. AI is in that imitation phase now. It ends when people stop asking AI to fake what humans already make, and start making what only AI can make.</p></div>

Maxim Gorky sat through a Lumière screening in 1896 and wrote: "A world without sound, without colour. Everything is dipped in monotonous grey." He called it the Kingdom of Shadows. You know the feeling. It is what you get scrolling AI images at 1am. Gray. Competent. Dead.

I spent last week with Jack Conte's SXSW 2026 talk, "The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI." Conte co-founded Patreon and is half of the band Pomplamoose. He gets paid by the machine and hurt by it at the same time. His argument: AI is in its 1895 phase. I think he is right. I also think the phase needs a name. Named things are easier to end. Call it the Gorky phase: the years when a new medium is measured against the old one, and loses.

## The pattern repeats, every time

Four times in a hundred years, a new creative machine arrived, the professionals panicked, and the early output was garbage.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Cinema, recorded music, sound film, and the synthesizer all went through imitation, panic, then a pattern-breaker who invented the medium's own language.</p></div>

Cinema first. Early filmmakers pointed cameras at stages and recorded theater. That was the whole idea. Then, in 1902, Georges Méliès made A Trip to the Moon and invented the cut. Special effects, match cuts, a story that moved between places. Impossible on a stage. That is the point. Cinema stopped being recorded theater and became cinema.

Recorded music next. In the 1930s, the American Federation of Musicians ran ads against "canned music." One showed a robot feeding acoustic instruments into a meat grinder. By 1930, the union estimated 22,000 moviehouse musicians had lost their jobs as talkies replaced live accompaniment. In 1942, union president James Petrillo banned all commercial recording outright. No union musician entered a studio for two years.

My favorite receipt is the union's 1929 manifesto: "Musical authorities know that the soul of the Art is lost in mechanization. The quality of music is dependent on the mood of the artist, upon the human contact, without which the essence of intellectual stimulation and emotional rapture is lost." Swap "mechanization" for "generative AI" and you have a LinkedIn post from last Tuesday. The panic is a hundred years old, word for word.

What ended the canned music era? In 1966 the Beatles recorded Revolver, an album they never meant to play live, built from tape loops, backwards guitar, and orchestral collage. Producer George Martin called the studio "a new canvas on which they had to paint." The recording stopped being a photograph of a performance and became the artwork itself.

Sound film got the same treatment from its loudest critic. Charlie Chaplin held out against talkies for thirteen years. City Lights in 1931. Modern Times in 1936. Silent, while the industry converted around him. Then in 1940 he released The Great Dictator, and the final speech, a human at a microphone pleading for decency, is one of the most famous monologues in film history. The man who resisted sound longest used it best.

And the synthesizer. In 1939, Hammond unveiled the Novachord at the New York World's Fair, the first commercial polyphonic synthesizer. One hundred sixty-three vacuum tubes, nearly 500 pounds. As Conte tells it, the musicians' union banned it. Eighty years later, the synthesizer is the sound of Stevie Wonder, Prince, and Daft Punk.

## Slop is the medium's infancy

Conte's definition of slop is the sharpest I have heard: "That lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium where we're all just trying to reproduce existing things that are honestly just better if the people do them."

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>We judge every new medium through the old one's reference points. Asking AI for a song that sounds like the Beatles is 1895 filmmakers framing a stage play. Slop is a symptom of youth.</p></div>

Look at what we ask AI to do. Make something that looks hand-drawn. Make something that looks filmed. Write a song that sounds like the Beatles. Every one of those requests defines success as a convincing imitation of the previous medium. A 2026 prompt that says "make it look like a photograph" is a 1895 director saying "frame it like a stage play."

I think this is why so much AI output feels gray even when it is technically impressive. The Gorky feeling. The output is measured against a thing humans already do better, it loses, and we conclude the machine is hollow. Gorky concluded the same about cinema. Cinema was seven years old.

I keep returning to HAL 9000 in 2001: A Space Odyssey. HAL plays chess, monitors the ship, reads lips. Flawless inside the rules of every game it was given. What HAL never does is invent a new game. Kubrick got something right in 1968 that we are rediscovering now: a system trained to master existing patterns is, by construction, the wrong tool for breaking them.

## The honest part

I tried to write this as a clean anthem for the new medium, and I couldn't get past the economics.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The 22,000 musicians never got their jobs back. Orchestras really did decline. The pattern promises the art form survives. It makes no promises to any individual artist.</p></div>

Those 22,000 moviehouse musicians did not transition into studio careers. The jobs ended. And the fear did not stop in 1942. Economists William Baumol and William Bowen named the problem in 1966: the cost disease of the performing arts. A Haydn symphony takes 25 minutes to perform. It took 25 minutes in 1790 and it takes 25 minutes now. Productivity cannot improve without destroying the thing itself. A Stanford GSB study from 2008 found that 46 of the 63 largest U.S. orchestras ran deficits. Sometimes the pessimists are right about the numbers.

Conte does not skip this, and it is why his talk works. "I'm so upset about how this tech has rolled out," he says. "Our work has been used without our consent, without paying us, and without giving us credit." Then, in the same breath: "I feel like I get to be alive in 1895 for the invention of the cinematograph." Both feelings at once. Anger about the terms, gratitude about the timing. I recognize that tension. I build AI systems and I write about what they cost. I do not know how to hold only one of those.

## What the machine cannot do

Conte runs a thought experiment: train a model on every piece of music recorded up to 1959. Could it produce the Beatles? Nirvana without Kurt Cobain? Biggie, Beyoncé, Jacob Collier? His answer: "Leaps like this require a spirit of rulebreaking and invention that I haven't yet seen demonstrated by these models." He hedges honestly. "I'm not saying it's impossible. But that tech does not exist today."

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Trained systems can find novelty inside a fixed game. Culture is an open game. The gap in the machine is the job description for the human.</p></div>

Conte gestures at this as an artist. As an engineer, I can state the mechanism. When you train a language model, you teach it to predict the most likely next token given everything before it. Then comes RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback, where human raters score outputs and the model is tuned to produce more of what raters prefer. Think about what that optimization does. The tuning pulls the model toward the responses raters approve. Both forces shrink the output toward the rater-approved mode. Ask the model for a song that sounds like the Beatles and you get the median of ten thousand tribute bands.

The counterexample is real. In 2016, AlphaGo played Move 37 against Lee Sedol, a move no professional would have played in 2,500 years of Go. So trained systems can produce genuine novelty. But look at where that novelty lives: inside a fixed game, with fixed rules and a win condition the system can verify by playing itself. Protein folding search works the same way. A defined space, explorable by simulation, with a score function. Culture is the opposite shape. There is no verifier. The game is the thing being invented, and the audience decides after the fact whether it was worth inventing. Move 37 expanded a game. The Beatles invented one.

Conte circles the same idea through comedy. Hasan Minhaj explains that great comedy involves risk: comedians operate outside the normal range of what is socially acceptable to say. And there is a line in the film Sing Street, an older brother talking to the kid starting a band: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." Conte's question lands hard: "If an LLM says some crazy shit, it's not interesting to me. What's it risking? Who cares?"

Nothing. It risks nothing. No reputation to lose, no rent to pay, no audience to alienate, no thirteen years of pride on the line like Chaplin had.

## Scarcity is the price signal

Conte quotes a creator whose name sounds like Keredex Chang, and I cannot verify this person exists beyond the talk, but the sentence earns its place: "Art is scarcity. Art is not what's easy to make, it's what's hard."

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI makes easy output infinite. Infinite supply collapses price, so scarce human work, the kind with real stakes behind it, gets repriced upward. The slop flood is the best argument for taking a risk on something hard.</p></div>

Conte's example is the invisible selfie stick, a camera on a pole that software erases so the footage looks like a drone following you. Hard trick once. The first videos were stunning, millions of views. Then everyone could do it, and now nobody watches them. Easy became infinite, and infinite became boring.

Now flip it. If art is scarcity, then a technology that makes easy output infinite reprices scarce human work upward. Every infinite scroll of gray slop raises the value of the thing somebody risked something on. This is why Kendrick Lamar is Conte's closing exhibit: good kid, m.A.A.d city is special because of Kendrick. Who he is, what he went through, the connection you feel listening.

I see the same repricing in my own feed, and it has a money side. The newsletters I actually pay for are the ones where a named human takes a position I can argue with. The infinite free stuff, I scroll past. Conte sees it from the other side of the ledger: Patreon's whole business is fans paying a specific person for work that person could not get paid for anywhere else. When generated output is free and infinite, the market for the named, the risky, and the scarce becomes the only market left that clears.

## Your job is the leap

Conte ends with his own band's story, and it is the right ending. Pomplamoose built an audience on YouTube, then the algorithm changed and growth flatlined. They rebuilt around iTunes, then Spotify ate iTunes. They rebuilt again, hit a million monthly listeners, then COVID killed touring. Then TikTok rewired discovery again. He calls it the infinite sine wave, and his conclusion is the anthem: "Change does not mean death. You can get back up and you can fucking go again."

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>If you build with these models, the unwritten vocabulary of the medium is your opening. There is a concrete build target: work with memory, real feedback loops, human-set constraints, and a surface no previous medium had. Invent the cut.</p></div>

Since I write about AI for a living, I will put my own bet on the record, dated and falsifiable. By 2030, the defining AI-native work will be something no human could have made alone and no model would have suggested on its own: stateful, responsive, shaped by the person experiencing it. Let me give that bet enough shape to build against, because a prediction without an architecture is a horoscope. I think the artifact needs four things. Persistent per-user state: the work remembers you across months, and that memory changes what it does. A feedback loop the model cannot shortcut: your actions alter the work in ways a one-shot prompt never reaches. An author-defined constraint space: a human sets the boundaries, the way a game designer sets rules, and the model operates inside them. And a surface no previous medium had: it is experienced, simultaneously, as artifact and as relationship. If in 2030 the most celebrated AI artifact is still a convincing imitation of something humans already make, a better fake Beatles, then I was wrong and the medium stalled in 1895. Check back in four years.

If you are early in your career and wiring models into products right now, this bet is also a job description. The four requirements above are not science fiction. They are a spec. You already have every primitive you need: a model, a database for state, a loop for feedback, and a human willing to set the rules. The teams that win the next four years will be the ones who stop asking the model to imitate and start asking it to do something a human never could. Point it at reproducing what humans already do and you get slop, and slop deserves its reviews. Point it at something that could not exist without it, and you might be the person who invents the cut.

Conte's last line is the one I would put on the wall: "Everything that makes anything matter exists because something alive once did. Humans will do that for a long, long time."

It is 1895. The camera works. Go make the cut.
