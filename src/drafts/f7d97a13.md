In 1895, the Lumière brothers patented the Cinématographe. In 2026, we ask a large language model to write a lost Beatles song and call the result AI creativity. I think we are repeating the same early mistake.

A large language model, or LLM, learns statistical patterns from training data and predicts likely next pieces of text. When we ask it for a Beatles song, we point a new machine toward a finished human form.

I tried to treat AI slop as a quality problem. Better models and better prompts seemed like the cure. Jack Conte gave me a more useful frame in a talk [uploaded in July 2026](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=803), *The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*. Conte, a musician and [Patreon co-founder](https://www.patreon.com/about), calls slop the lazy phase of a new medium, when people reproduce things humans already make better.

The economic harm remains real. Contempt still makes a poor research method. Slop may be the mess we produce while learning where the machine belongs.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A fake Beatles song uses a new machine to copy a finished artifact. Slop may tell us more about the poverty of the assignment than the limits of the medium.</p></div>

## The machine arrives before the form

Conte's history starts with artists using a new machine as a smaller stage.

The Lumière brothers held their first public Cinématographe screenings in [1895](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere). A year later, Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière program. [Conte quotes him](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=284) describing "a world without sound, without color," with everything dipped in monotonous gray. In Conte's account, the camera preserved motion while the staging kept borrowing the frame of a theater.

Georges Méliès pushed against that frame. His 1902 [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) used cuts and camera tricks to move through places no stage could hold. Decades later, *2001: A Space Odyssey* cut from a bone to a spacecraft. Millions of years passed in a fraction of a second. That scene works because film learned to think in cuts.

Recorded music had its own ugly beginning. The American Federation of Musicians attacked recordings as ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). The union says about 22,000 theater musicians lost their jobs as talkies spread, and it launched a commercial recording ban in 1942. The cost was carried by working musicians.

The studio later became part of the instrument. The Beatles' [1966 *Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver) used [tape loops and reversed sounds](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=756) that were difficult to reproduce onstage. Charlie Chaplin also resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then made the 1940 [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/009e372a-646d-5b6e-a6b0-59f96722a1fd/the-great-dictator), ending with a human voice that traveled far beyond one theater.

Conte adds the 1939 Hammond Novachord, an early commercial synthesizer. He [recounts](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=1824) its 163 vacuum tubes and near 500-pound weight. The American Federation of Musicians answered with [staffing rules](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf): an orchestra had to keep its existing players when it added the machine, and a member could not play it alone in a venue that had employed musicians. Baumol and Bowen later named the [cost disease of the performing arts](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease): a quartet still needs four people and the same playing time, even when productivity rises elsewhere.

I think this history offers a direction rather than a promise. Some media never find a durable form. The survivors suggest that invention begins when creators stop judging the machine by the standards of its predecessor.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film discovered the cut, while recorded music turned the studio into part of the instrument. Both transitions created new possibilities and imposed real costs on working artists.</p></div>

## Slop is the copy, scaled

Our lost Beatles song aims the model toward a destination humans reached sixty years ago.

Conte [proposes a useful thought experiment](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2828). Train a music model only on recordings made before 1959. Then ask whether it would discover the Beatles, Prince, Beyoncé, or Jacob Collier without hearing the human breakthroughs between them. The experiment has never been run in the form he describes, but the point is sharp: prediction searches learned possibility. Those artists changed the boundary by breaking local rules.

A model risks no reputation when a strange choice fails. The engineer can close the tab. An artist releases the choice under their own name and lives with the response. Conte [quotes *Sing Street*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2955): "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed."

Conte [also says](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3272) *good kid, m.A.A.d city* matters because Kendrick Lamar made it. The kick drum carries part of the record. Kendrick carries the life behind it. Later, he credits [Karen X Cheng](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3090) with a line I think is even tighter: "Art is scarcity." Her point is that difficulty gives the work value.

The fake Beatles song is like using the replicator in *Star Trek* to grade dinner by how closely it copies yesterday's sandwich. The device may be extraordinary. The assignment keeps it trapped in the past.

This is why [familiar model output keeps winning](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). The average is easy to generate and easy to recognize. Human taste chooses where to leave it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Slop is a predictable result when resemblance becomes the goal. Models can search learned patterns at huge scale; people still supply the risky choice that moves culture away from the average.</p></div>

## Give the song a job a recording cannot hold

Turn the fake Beatles song into a composition that changes through one listener's choices.

The listener opts in, hears a short score, and chooses a motif to carry forward. The model writes the next version around that motif. A human composer owns one stubborn rule: every version must contain a full silent measure. The model can move the silence, though it cannot remove it.

Memory alone adds little. The 1986 [*Legend of Zelda* cartridge](https://www.museumofplay.org/games/the-legend-of-zelda/) could save player progress. Persistent state belongs to ordinary software too. Generation earns a serious role only when the next score must be composed under the listener's choice and the artist's rule, with more possible responses than a fixed branching playlist can reasonably hold.

Start with the score as JSON. In Python, a `TypedDict` can name the fields this one run carries, including the chosen motif, consent status, generated score, and retry count. Feed that shape into [LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/graph-api), a library for building agent workflows. Its `StateGraph` object connects the model step to a normal validator and passes the typed state along. When the validator finds zero silent measures, the graph sends the score back once. That write-check-revise loop is a small [ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) pattern. ReAct alternates model decisions with tool actions, then brings the result into the next step. A `TypedDict` describes the shape and saves nothing by itself. Use a checkpointer to resume one thread, and a separate store for listener history across sessions.

I think the first experiment should be boringly governed. Name an owner and a spending limit. Write the stop condition before the first model call. Consent must be reversible, and the composer must control the source material. Compare the generated experience with a carefully authored branching version. Record which listener choices the branches cannot answer and whether the composer still recognizes their own rule. If generation adds only variety, use the simpler system.

The creative work starts with a human rule precise enough to survive the model. That is the same engineering discipline behind [writing the spec before delegation](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/).

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A changing song gives generation a necessary job only when fixed branches cannot provide the same experience. Typed state, deterministic checks, reversible consent, and human approval keep the experiment understandable.</p></div>

## Change keeps moving

Conte describes Pomplamoose's career as an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390).

YouTube changed how the band was discovered. Streaming weakened the iTunes model. COVID changed how they recorded together. Vertical video forced another production setup. Each turn made some skill less useful and another skill urgent.

I don't know whether today's models will produce a lasting creative form. History cannot answer that for us because we remember the media that survived. It can remove one bad excuse: awkward beginnings are weak evidence of a dead end.

I think junior AI engineers have a better assignment than polishing the next imitation. Build the strange experiment where the human rule is visible, the model can surprise you, and failure stays cheap enough to study.

Some of that work will sound bad, and some will be embarrassing. I would still rather see a hundred bounded experiments fail than another perfect copy of 1966.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Change has repeatedly forced artists to rebuild their practice, and AI offers no guarantee of survival. The medium begins to move when builders give generation a new job while keeping human risk and authorship visible.</p></div>
