In 1895, the Lumière brothers [projected workers leaving a factory](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere) with a fixed camera. The image moved, but the grammar came from the stage. In 2026, we ask a large language model for a lost Beatles song and repeat the mistake.

A large language model, or LLM, learns patterns from training data and predicts likely next pieces. A polished imitation can arrive in seconds, which makes slop feel like a final form.

Musician and Patreon co-founder Jack Conte offers another reading in his SXSW talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=17_HcR95YBc). He calls slop the lazy phase after a medium arrives, when people use it to reproduce work that humans already make better.

I think that frame changes the job for a junior AI engineer. Imagine one project for the rest of this article: an adaptive song by a living composer. A listener plays a short motif, and the system writes a reply in real time under rules the composer approved. The goal is an exchange that a fixed recording cannot provide.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>LLMs begin with imitation because familiar forms give them a clear target. Slop appears when we treat that borrowed target as the destination instead of searching for work that needs the new medium.</p></div>

## We put the camera in row ten

Early film often placed a camera in front of actors arranged like a stage production. The machine recorded motion through a theatre director's mental model.

In 1896, Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière programme. [Conte quotes him](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=284) describing "a world without sound, without color" where everything was "dipped in monotonous gray." The experience felt thin.

Georges Méliès found a choice that belonged to film. His 1902 [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) used cuts and camera tricks to make bodies vanish and places change. The camera moved beyond recording a performance and began shaping time.

The match cut in [*2001: A Space Odyssey*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/cefccdb2-b558-5623-9c17-72b4be7bd4c1/2001-a-space-odyssey) is the sci-fi version of that leap. A bone rises, the image cuts to an object in orbit, and film crosses millions of years in a fraction of a second.

I think the lost Beatles prompt leaves our camera in row ten. Better output gives us a sharper copy of an old form. The adaptive song asks for a move that recording alone cannot make: hear this listener now, then answer inside the composer's rules.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film developed its own grammar when cuts and camera tricks let artists shape time and space. Generative AI stays in its early phase while fidelity to an existing human work remains the main test.</p></div>

## Every new medium sends someone the bill

The history becomes less comforting when we follow the money.

The American Federation of Musicians called recorded accompaniment ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). Its history says about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs when talkies arrived in 1927.

The Hammond Novachord caused a related fight. Union [rules published in May 1939](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) protected orchestra headcount when the instrument was added. Solo use in a venue that had employed musicians required local union consent.

[Baumol and Bowen's cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) explains part of the pressure. A string quartet still needs four players and the same time, even while other industries become more productive. Speeding it up would change the work.

New grammar still emerged. Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then used it for the final humanist address in the 1940 [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/009e372a-646d-5b6e-a6b0-59f96722a1fd/the-great-dictator). The Beatles released [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver) in 1966 with backwards sounds and deeper studio experiments. Recording became part of composition.

I think optimism about AI has to carry the economic cost inside it. Conte opens his talk angry that creators' work was used without consent, payment, or credit. Our adaptive song therefore needs licensed source material, a named composer, and a clear release decision owned by that person. A new medium does not get an ethical holiday while it searches for a grammar.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Talkies displaced thousands of theatre musicians, and the Novachord triggered rules to protect live work. Creative breakthroughs arrived beside real labor loss, so an AI-native project needs rights, consent, and visible human ownership from the start.</p></div>

## Prediction has a hard time risking a reputation

Conte proposes a useful thought experiment. Train a music model only on recordings made through 1959, then ask it to reach the Beatles. He offers no benchmark or score. The point is to test our belief that pattern prediction can produce a cultural break before an example of that break enters the data.

Art also carries risk. Conte quotes *Sing Street*: ["Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed."](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3006) A model can emit a strange chord and continue with the next request. A composer can lose an audience, a job, or confidence in a direction that took years to build.

This is why Conte hates the word "content." It packs a life, an argument, and a disposable clip into the same output bucket. His example is Kendrick Lamar's *good kid, m.A.A.d city*. The record reaches us with Kendrick's life attached. The sound and the person arrive together.

Our adaptive song keeps that link visible. The model proposes a reply, while the composer defines the musical world and accepts the result under a human name. I think this gives generation a narrower and more honest role.

I don't know whether a model can make a genuine cultural break. I think it can surprise us. Surprise becomes art through the person who chooses the risk and lives with the response.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Pattern learning can produce novelty, while cultural invention asks someone to risk a name and choose a direction. The adaptive song keeps the model inside a human-authored musical world instead of treating output as anonymous content.</p></div>

## Give generation a job that can fail

The adaptive song becomes useful when we can remove the model and watch the experience change.

Interactive music already uses written branches and procedural systems, so compare those first. If prepared replies can handle the listener's motifs, use the simpler system.

Call this the replacement test:

| Replacement | Test | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Static recording | Can the listener's motif change the reply? | If yes, generation adds little. |
| Written branches | Can 20 unseen allowed motifs receive fitting replies? | If yes, keep the branches. |
| Generative model | Does it answer unseen motifs within the composer's rules? | Keep it only if human review shows a clear gain. |

The implementation can stay small. A [`TypedDict`](https://docs.python.org/3/library/typing.html#typing.TypedDict) is a Python type hint that lists the expected fields in a dictionary. Static type-checking tools can catch a missing field before the program runs. Normal code still has to enforce the rules at runtime.

```python
from typing import Literal, TypedDict

class SongState(TypedDict):
    motif: list[int]
    rights_status: Literal["licensed", "blocked"]
    reply_path: str | None
    composer_approved: bool

def can_release(state: SongState) -> bool:
    return (
        state["rights_status"] == "licensed"
        and state["composer_approved"] is True
    )
```

[LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/graph-api) is a library for workflows that branch or loop. Its `StateGraph` connects steps called nodes and passes shared state between them. For our song, the nodes could receive the motif, check rights, generate a reply, request composer review, then release or delete the file. A blocked rights check routes straight to deletion. A rejected composer review does the same.

[ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) means reasoning and acting. The model chooses an action, reads the result, and uses that observation for its next choice. It earns a place here only if the model can call a real tool, such as a tempo analyzer. A fixed `StateGraph` is easier to test when every step is already known.

Run the test with 20 unseen motifs and compare the model with written branches. Have the composer score musical fit and rule violations. Record latency and cost. Keep failed replies out of release. This is the same discipline as [treating the spec as the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/): human intent stays explicit while the machine handles bounded execution.

I think this is a better engineering brief than "make a lost Beatles song." It can reject the model. It also gives a junior engineer a state contract, a baseline, and a release gate they can test.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Replace the model with a recording or written branches and compare the experience. Use generation only when unseen listener input needs a new reply, then enforce rights and composer approval in normal code.</p></div>

## Change is an infinite sine wave

Conte describes Pomplamoose's career as an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390). YouTube changed discovery. Spotify weakened the band's iTunes model. COVID changed recording, and vertical video forced another rebuild.

The pattern offers hope without promising that every medium survives. Some disappear. The 1895 analogy remains useful only while it produces work we can test against simpler tools.

Criticism can expose stolen labor and show when [polished output turns into cultural elevator music](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). Building has a different job. It has to find the move that depends on the medium while keeping the human relationship intact.

My take is simple. Slop is evidence of a lazy assignment. Our adaptive song earns its model when an unseen listener motif arrives, the system answers under licensed rules, and a human composer is willing to sign the result.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Platform change keeps forcing creators to rebuild, and generative AI may do the same. The medium earns confidence through testable work that needs generation and leaves a human author visible.</p></div>
