In 1895, the Lumière brothers patented the Cinématographe. One of their train films reached audiences the next year. In 2026, we prompt a large language model to write a lost Beatles song and call it AI creativity. I think we are making the same mistake.

A large language model, or LLM, learns patterns from huge datasets and predicts the next likely piece of text. The fake Beatles song uses that new machine to copy an old artifact.

I tried to see the flood of synthetic work as a model-quality problem. Jack Conte gave me a more useful frame. In [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=803), the musician and [Patreon co-founder](https://www.patreon.com/about) calls slop the lazy phase of a new medium, when we reproduce things people already make better.

Slop may be an early symptom. History offers no guarantee that the medium grows past it, though it gives us a better assignment than disgust.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The fake Beatles song uses a new machine to copy an old artifact. Better output may improve the copy, while the medium's own creative grammar remains undiscovered.</p></div>

## New media begin with borrowed grammar

The first version of a medium often looks like a weaker version of the one before it.

Conte describes early film as theater placed inside a camera frame. Maxim Gorky saw a Lumière screening in 1896. [Conte quotes him](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=284) describing "a world without sound, without color," with everything dipped in monotonous gray. Film had gained a machine and lost the room.

Then filmmakers learned to cut. Georges Méliès used editing and camera tricks in the 1902 [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/). Decades later, *2001: A Space Odyssey* cut from a bone to a spacecraft and crossed millions of years in one instant. That sci-fi cut carries an argument about human progress inside the edit itself.

Recorded music went through the same awkward youth. The American Federation of Musicians called recordings ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). The union says about 22,000 theater musicians lost their jobs when talkies arrived, and it began a commercial recording ban in 1942.

The recording studio eventually became part of the instrument. On the Beatles' 1966 [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver), tape loops and reversed sounds created choices that were hard to reproduce onstage. After resisting synchronized dialogue for years, Charlie Chaplin used his 1940 [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/009e372a-646d-5b6e-a6b0-59f96722a1fd/the-great-dictator) to deliver a recorded humanist address.

The 1939 Novachord adds another warning. Conte [describes the machine](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=1824) as an early commercial synthesizer with 163 vacuum tubes and a weight near 500 pounds. He also recounts a musicians' union prohibition driven by fear of orchestral job loss. Baumol and Bowen later named the [cost disease of the performing arts](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease): a quartet still needs human time to play a quartet, even as productivity rises elsewhere.

I think optimism that skips those displaced musicians is too cheap to trust. History can produce a new grammar while workers carry the bill.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film found editing, and recorded music found the studio. Those discoveries expanded the work, while artists paid a real economic price during the transition.</p></div>

## Slop is a bad assignment at scale

Our fake Beatles song aims the model toward a destination humans reached in 1966.

Conte offers a sharp thought experiment. Train a music model only on recordings made before 1959. Could it discover the Beatles, Prince, or Nirvana without hearing the rule-breaking work between them? I think the test exposes the gap between pattern search and cultural risk.

A model risks no reputation when a strange choice fails. The engineer can close the tab. An artist releases that choice under their own name and lives with the response. Conte pairs this with a line from *Sing Street*: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed."

Conte says *good kid, m.A.A.d city* matters because Kendrick Lamar made it. A kick drum can carry only part of that life. He credits [Karen X Cheng](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3090) with an even tighter idea: art is scarce, and its value lives in what is hard to make.

There are already counterexamples to a simple 1895 story. British artist Anna Ridler's 2018 [*Mosaic Virus*](https://ars.electronica.art/outofthebox/en/mosaic-virus/) trained on 10,000 tulip photographs, and the generated flowers changed alongside Bitcoin's price. The artist's rule remains visible. I think this is closer to a native grammar than another synthetic painting in an old style.

For our fake Beatles song, use a harder test. Replace the model with a database, a rules engine, then a set of fixed templates. If listeners get the same experience, generation added decoration. If the experience breaks, the model may have earned a role.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Slop grows when AI is judged by how well it resembles an older artifact. A medium-dependent experiment must lose something essential when the model is replaced by templates or ordinary software.</p></div>

## Give the fake song a job a recording cannot do

Turn the lost Beatles track into a song that changes through a relationship with one listener.

The listener hears a short version and chooses one motif to carry forward. The next version composes around that motif. A human composer owns one fixed rule: every score contains a full measure of silence. The model may move it while the composer's rule keeps it in every score.

A junior engineer could build the first experiment with a small graph. ReAct is an agent pattern that alternates reasoning with actions, such as generating a score and checking it. LangGraph is a library for drawing that loop as explicit steps. In Python, a TypedDict declares the fields carried between those steps, perhaps the listener's chosen motif, the new score, consent status, and retry count. A StateGraph connects the steps and passes that typed state through the workflow.

I would keep the first version conceptual and small. Use a JSON music score so a normal validator can confirm that one measure is silent. Store the listener's motif only after opt-in. Let the graph retry once when the rule fails, then send the score to a human composer.

The experiment needs an owner and a stop rule before it needs more code. Give one product sponsor and one composer two weeks, twenty listeners, and a cost ceiling. Stop if listeners cannot tell the adaptive version from fixed branches, the silent measure disappears, or consent data resists deletion. Scale only when generation changes the experience and the human boundary survives.

That is the same discipline I described in [The Spec Is the Cognitive Work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/). The creative contribution begins with a rule sharp enough to survive the model.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The living-song experiment gives generation a necessary role: one listener choice changes the next score. Typed state, deterministic validation, consent, and a human approval gate keep the experiment bounded.</p></div>

## Change keeps moving

Conte describes Pomplamoose's career as an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390).

YouTube changed how the band was discovered. Streaming weakened the iTunes model. COVID changed how they recorded together. Vertical video changed the cameras again. Each turn asked the artists to rebuild some part of the work.

This history has survivorship bias because I chose media that endured. I don't know which future AI creativity gets.

I think slop deserves less prophecy and more engineering. Protect consent, pay creators, and run small tests that can fail. Then look for the rule that lets this medium make an experience its predecessors could never hold.

The fake Beatles song tells us the camera works. The work ahead is learning what kind of cut only this medium can make.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Platforms keep changing, and survival is never guaranteed. The useful response to slop is a bounded search for AI's own grammar, with creator rights and human authorship visible throughout.</p></div>
