In 1895, the Lumière brothers [projected workers leaving a factory](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere). A year later, Maxim Gorky saw a soundless, colourless world. In 2026, we prompt a large language model to write a poem and call the result AI content. We are making the same early mistake.

A large language model, or LLM, learns patterns from training data and predicts likely next pieces. That makes imitation the easy first move. Ask for the Beatles and you get Beatles-shaped statistics.

Musician and Patreon co-founder Jack Conte gives this phase a useful name in his SXSW talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=17_HcR95YBc). Slop, he says, is the lazy phase after a new medium arrives, when people use it to reproduce work humans already make better.

I think that definition takes some heat out of the debate. A transition symptom can still hurt people. It can also point builders toward the work the medium has yet to discover.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>LLMs begin with imitation because familiar forms give them a clear target. Slop appears when the copy becomes the goal.</p></div>

## We put the camera in row ten

Early film had motion, but its frame still obeyed the stage.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film found its own grammar when artists used cuts to shape time. Generative AI remains in its early phase while we judge it by fidelity to older human work.</p></div>

Many early filmmakers placed a fixed camera in front of actors arranged like a theatre cast. The machine was new. The director's mental model came from the previous medium.

In 1896, [Conte quotes Gorky](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=284) describing "a world without sound, without color" where everything was "dipped in monotonous gray." Then Georges Méliès, a magician, discovered that stopping and restarting the camera could make a person vanish. His 1902 [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) used the cut as part of the art.

The match cut in [*2001: A Space Odyssey*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/cefccdb2-b558-5623-9c17-72b4be7bd4c1/2001-a-space-odyssey) shows how far that grammar travelled. Kubrick cuts from a thrown bone to a spacecraft. Millennia disappear between two frames. A theatre stage cannot make time behave that way.

I think our lost Beatles prompt keeps the camera in row ten. Better output gives us a cleaner imitation. It says little about the form that becomes possible when generation happens during the experience.

## The bill arrives before the grammar

Media history becomes harder to romanticize when the people losing work enter the frame.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>New creative tools have displaced real workers before artists found their best use. Optimism about AI has to include rights and release authority from the first prototype.</p></div>

The American Federation of Musicians called recorded accompaniment ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). Its own history says about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs when talkies arrived in 1927. The union founded the Music Defense League in 1930 and later struck record companies in 1942.

The Hammond Novachord triggered another fight. Union [rules published in May 1939](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) protected orchestra headcount when the instrument was added. Solo use in a venue that had employed musicians required local consent.

[Baumol and Bowen's cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) explains the pressure. A string quartet still needs four players and the same amount of time, even when productivity rises elsewhere. Speeding up the performance changes the work itself.

Yet artists eventually found uses that belonged to the new tools. Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then used it for the final humanist address in the 1940 [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/009e372a-646d-5b6e-a6b0-59f96722a1fd/the-great-dictator). The Beatles released [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver) in 1966 with backwards sounds and deeper studio experiments. Recording became part of composition.

Conte opens his talk angry that creators' work entered AI systems without consent or payment. I think engineers should carry that cost into the design. An AI music project needs licensed source material and a person who can block release. If either is missing, the brief is incomplete.

## Prediction cannot risk a name

Cultural breaks ask a person to choose what the data does not reward yet.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Pattern learning can surprise us, while cultural invention puts a person's reputation on the result. The artist still owns the choice that turns an odd output into a public work.</p></div>

Conte proposes a thought experiment. Train a music model only on recordings made through 1959, then ask it to reach the Beatles. He presents this as a boundary test rather than a measured benchmark. A model can recombine the past. The harder claim is whether it can break a rule before the break appears in its data.

I don't know whether a model could cross that line. Conte points to the human cost of trying. In *Sing Street*, a character says, ["Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed."](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3006) A model that emits a bad chord loses no audience. A musician can lose years of trust in one release.

This is also why Conte hates the word "content." His example is Kendrick Lamar's *good kid, m.A.A.d city*. The record reaches us with Kendrick's life attached. Its meaning cannot be reduced to chord changes or production quality.

I think AI can make strange and useful things. The named artist still faces the audience and decides whether the work goes out.

## Give generation a job the old medium cannot do

A model earns its place when removing it changes the experience in a useful way.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Compare a generative idea with a recording and authored branches before adding a model. Keep generation only when it handles unseen input better, under rules a human owner can enforce.</p></div>

Return to the AI music brief. Replace "make a lost Beatles song" with a call-and-response piece by a living composer. A listener plays a short motif. The system replies inside rules the composer wrote. A fixed recording cannot hear the listener and answer, so responsiveness gives generation a concrete job.

Use a replacement test before choosing the architecture:

| Baseline | What happens | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Static recording | Every listener hears the same reply | If the experience still works, skip generation |
| Authored branches | Expected motifs route to prepared replies | If coverage is good enough, ship the branches |
| Generative model | An unseen motif receives a new reply | Keep the model only if tests show a clear gain |

The plumbing can stay boring. A [`TypedDict`](https://docs.python.org/3/library/typing.html#typing.TypedDict) lists the state fields a Python type checker expects, perhaps the listener motif and rights status. Normal code must still reject bad values while the program runs. In [LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/graph-api), a `StateGraph` passes that state through named steps and routes each result. [ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) is a loop where the model picks a tool, reads the result, then chooses its next action. Use it only if the song needs a real tool choice. A fixed graph is easier to test when every step is known.

Run unseen motifs through both authored branches and the model. Let the composer score musical fit. Measure response time and cost. Release only when the rights check passes and the composer approves the file. This follows the same discipline as [treating the spec as the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/): human intent stays explicit while the machine handles bounded execution.

I think this is a better brief for a junior engineer because the model can fail the test. If written branches handle the motifs well, ship branches. Generation has to earn the extra complexity.

## Change is an infinite sine wave

Conte's own career makes the historical optimism less abstract.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Pomplamoose survived repeated platform changes by rebuilding how it reached listeners. AI gets the same chance only if builders move beyond imitation and produce work that depends on generation.</p></div>

Conte calls Pomplamoose's cycle an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390). A YouTube change flattened the band's growth. Streaming weakened its iTunes sales model. COVID changed recording, while vertical video forced another production rebuild. Each change forced the band to learn again while the old setup disappeared.

The 1895 analogy remains a bet. If generation never produces work that depends on its capabilities, slop may be the ceiling. History gives us permission to explore, without promising that every new tool becomes an art form.

Critics should keep tracing whose work entered the training set and who gets paid. Engineers should run the replacement test before adding a model. Otherwise we get more of the [cultural elevator music already filling the internet](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/).

AI leaves 1895 when removing generation breaks the work.
