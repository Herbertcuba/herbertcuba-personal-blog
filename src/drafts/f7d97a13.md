In 1895, the Lumière brothers [projected workers leaving a factory](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere). The camera watched from one place as people crossed the frame. The image moved, but the shape of the experience still belonged to theatre: a fixed viewpoint, action on a stage.

In 2026, we ask a large language model to write a poem. An LLM [predicts likely continuations](https://cdn.openai.com/better-language-models/language_models_are_unsupervised_multitask_learners.pdf) from patterns in its training data, which lets it imitate an older form.

I think these two moments share the same mistake. We judge a young medium by how well it copies work built for another one.

Musician and Patreon co-founder Jack Conte gives this phase a useful name in his July 2026 talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=17_HcR95YBc). Slop, in his framing, is the lazy period after a medium arrives, when people use it to reproduce things humans already make better.

That framing lowers the temperature without erasing the damage. Creators can lose income and control while engineers search for the new form.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Slop appears when a new medium spends its early years imitating older work. The phase may pass, while the costs to working creators arrive immediately.</p></div>

## Film had to leave the stage

Early cinema became its own form when filmmakers stopped treating the camera as a seat in the audience.

In 1896, Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière programme and described ["a world without sound, without colour"](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/91ddc4e0-5576-4f98-a3b6-ba8a97703eda/section/2fe1a387-c815-45d7-9609-2a47615dc9e8), with everything "dipped in monotonous grey." Many early films recorded an event from a fixed position, using familiar staging inside a new machine.

Georges Méliès came from stage magic. For one trick, he filmed an empty box, stopped the camera, placed a person inside, then started it again. Joining those strips of film made the person appear from nowhere. The interruption became part of the illusion. His 1902 [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) pushed that technique into a world a theatre could only suggest.

Decades later, Stanley Kubrick opened [*2001: A Space Odyssey*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/cefccdb2-b558-5623-9c17-72b4be7bd4c1/2001-a-space-odyssey) with early humans discovering a bone as a tool. One throws it into the air. Kubrick cuts to a spacecraft with a similar shape, and thousands of years disappear between two frames.

I think AI creativity still has its camera in the tenth row. Better prompts often give us a sharper recording of the same stage. The medium moves forward when generation changes the possible form of the work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Film grew beyond recorded theatre when stopping, joining, and rearranging images became part of the art. AI remains early while imitation is its main proof of quality.</p></div>

## The bill arrives before the masterpiece

Working musicians felt the economics of a new medium long before audiences heard its best work.

The American Federation of Musicians called recorded accompaniment ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). Its history says about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs when talking pictures arrived in 1927. The union formed the Music Defense League in 1930, then called a recording strike in 1942.

The Hammond Novachord brought a related fight in 1939. It was an early polyphonic synthesizer, an electronic keyboard that could play several notes at once. Union [rules published that May](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) protected orchestra headcount when venues added it. Solo use where musicians had worked before required local consent.

Those rules came from a real pressure that economists William Baumol and William Bowen later described as the [cost disease of the performing arts](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease). A string quartet still needs four players and the same amount of performance time even as productivity rises elsewhere. Speeding up the quartet changes the music.

Charlie Chaplin had built the Tramp through silent physical comedy, so he resisted synchronized speech for years. In 1940, he used it for the closing humanist address in [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/1-The-Great-Dictator). The Beatles used backwards sound and studio experiments on 1966's [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver), making work a plain live capture could not reproduce.

I think this history gives builders a double duty. We should explore what AI can become while placing source licences and human release authority inside the first prototype. Future creative value cannot repay a creator whose work was taken today.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Creative technology can remove jobs before artists discover its strongest form. Exploration deserves support when creators keep rights and release authority during the transition.</p></div>

## A model has no reputation to lose

Cultural invention asks someone to choose a break before the audience rewards it.

Conte offers a thought experiment. Train a music model only on recordings made through 1959, then ask it to discover the Beatles. The model can recombine the patterns it has learned. The exercise asks whether it can choose a rule-breaking move that its data does not already reward.

Conte later quotes *Sing Street*: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." A model can produce a bad chord and lose a benchmark point. A musician puts years of trust behind the release.

I don't know whether future models will cross that line by themselves. I think the harder engineering problem is that a model has no biography attached to its choice. Conte points to Kendrick Lamar's *good kid, m.A.A.d city* and says the record carries Kendrick's history and pain. The sound reaches us through a person.

This is why the word "content" feels too small. It treats a Kendrick album and a generated sound file as interchangeable units, even though listeners meet them through different relationships.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Models can produce surprising output, while artists carry the social risk of a choice. A person's history changes the meaning and value of the work.</p></div>

## Give generation a job

AI earns a place in creative work when removing live generation breaks the experience.

Consider one concrete prototype. A living composer writes rules for a call-and-response song. A listener plays a short motif, and the system replies inside those rules. The composer has never heard that exact motif during production.

Test three versions against the same brief:

| Version | Response to a new listener motif | What the result tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Static recording | Cannot respond | If listeners still value the piece, the interactive brief was unnecessary |
| Authored branches | Covers motifs the team anticipated | Ship branches when they handle the useful range |
| Generative model | Attempts a new reply at runtime | Generation earns its place when blind tests show a valued gain over branches |

A static recording fails the response requirement by definition, proving only that runtime interaction is needed. Authored branches may still cover the experience. A gain on unseen motifs supports generation because the work now depends on a reply the team could not record in advance.

LangGraph can keep this prototype legible: its `StateGraph` moves a shared song record between steps, while a Python `TypedDict` names fields such as the motif and composer approval. ReAct is a pattern where the model takes an action, reads the result, then chooses again. It adds little here unless the song must choose among tools during the performance.

Hold motifs back during development and let the composer score branch and model replies without knowing which made them. Set response-time and cost limits before coding. Block release until source rights are clear and the composer approves. This is [the spec doing the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/) before the model starts producing.

My take is simple: generation should survive comparison with the cheaper version. Otherwise we are funding a technical costume for an experience that already worked.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Compare a generative experience with a recording and authored branches. Keep the model when unseen inputs reveal a gain that a human creator can evaluate and approve.</p></div>

## Change comes in waves

Conte has lived this cycle as an artist, which makes his optimism more useful than a prediction from the sidelines.

Pomplamoose built an audience through YouTube and digital music sales. Conte calls the band's disruptions an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390). Its growth flattened around YouTube's Cosmic Panda redesign, although he says the band did not know the cause. Streaming weakened the iTunes model. COVID changed how the band recorded, and vertical video demanded new systems.

Each wave broke a working setup. Pomplamoose adapted, then met the next wave.

I think that is the useful kind of hope. History gives us patterns rather than guarantees. Critics should trace whose work enters training sets and who gets paid. Engineers should test experiences that could not exist before generation.

[The flood of cultural elevator music](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/) is a reason to raise the ambition, rather than a verdict on the medium. The Lumières proved that images could move. Méliès discovered that stopping the camera could change what an image meant.

AI leaves 1895 when generation becomes inseparable from the work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Creative change arrives as repeated disruption, and survival requires rebuilding. AI has a future when engineers move beyond copies and creators retain authority over the work.</p></div>
