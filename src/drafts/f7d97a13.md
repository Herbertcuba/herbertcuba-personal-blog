In 1895, the Lumière brothers [projected workers leaving a factory](https://www.institut-lumiere.org/le-cinematographe-lumiere) with a fixed camera. The image moved, but its grammar still came from the stage. In 2026, we ask a large language model for a lost Beatles song and repeat the mistake.

A large language model, or LLM, learns patterns in training data and predicts likely next pieces. It can make the copy feel finished. That polish is why slop can look like the destination.

Musician and Patreon co-founder Jack Conte gives us a better frame in his SXSW talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=17_HcR95YBc). He calls slop the lazy phase after a medium arrives, when people use it to reproduce work that humans already make better.

I think his 1895 comparison gives junior AI engineers a better assignment. Treat generative AI as a medium that is still searching for its own move.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>LLMs begin with imitation because familiar forms provide a clear target. Slop is what that borrowed target looks like before a medium develops uses of its own.</p></div>

## We put the camera in row ten

Early films often placed a fixed camera in front of actors arranged like a stage production. The camera could record movement, but the mental model still came from theatre.

In 1896, Maxim Gorky watched a Lumière programme. [Conte quotes him](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=284) describing "a world without sound, without color" where everything was "dipped in monotonous gray."

Georges Méliès began finding another use. His 1902 [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/) used cuts and camera tricks to make bodies vanish and locations change. A stage could never do that in the same way.

The match cut in [*2001: A Space Odyssey*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/cefccdb2-b558-5623-9c17-72b4be7bd4c1/2001-a-space-odyssey) carries the idea further. A bone rises into the air, the image cuts to an object in orbit, and cinema crosses millions of years in one edit. The scene works because film can join time through images.

I think the lost Beatles prompt still leaves the camera in row ten. Better models may copy the texture with greater skill. The form already belongs to a human record.

## Recorded music changed the labor deal

When talkies reached theatres, orchestras began losing their nightly work.

The American Federation of Musicians called recorded accompaniment ["canned music"](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). Its own history says about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs when talkies arrived in 1927. The same history records a recording strike against record companies in 1942.

The Hammond Novachord raised a related fear. Union [rules published in May 1939](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/30s/International-Musician-1939-05.pdf) protected orchestra headcount when the instrument was added. Solo use in a venue that had employed musicians required local union consent.

[Baumol and Bowen's cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease) explains some of the pressure. A quartet still needs four players even when factories and offices become more productive. The cost gap grows because the performance cannot be sped up without changing the work.

Calling slop transitional can become a cheap way to ignore the people carrying the loss. Conte opens his talk angry that creators' work has been used without consent or payment. I think any optimism about the medium needs to keep that sentence inside it.

Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then used it for the final humanist address in the 1940 [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/009e372a-646d-5b6e-a6b0-59f96722a1fd/the-great-dictator). The Beatles released [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver) in 1966 with backwards sounds and deeper studio experimentation. The recording process became part of composition.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Talkies cost theatre musicians real jobs, and the Novachord triggered rules designed to protect live work. Later artistic breakthroughs grew alongside that economic damage.</p></div>

## Human invention breaks the distribution

Conte proposes a useful thought experiment. Train a music model only on recordings made through 1959, then ask it to reach the Beatles. He offers no dataset or score. The exercise tests our confidence that pattern learning can produce a cultural break before an example of that break exists in the data.

Art also carries risk. Conte quotes *Sing Street*: ["Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed."](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3006) A model can emit a strange chord without putting a name or career on the line. A musician cannot.

The talk later connects art with scarcity and difficulty, though the available captions leave the credited creator's name unclear. That uncertainty is worth preserving instead of polishing the attribution into a false fact.

Conte hates the word "content" because it compresses very different human acts into one unit of output. His example is Kendrick Lamar's [*good kid, m.A.A.d city*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3287). The record reaches a listener with Kendrick's life attached. Its meaning exceeds the sound of the kick drum.

I think a model may still surprise us. The human carries the reason the surprise was worth making.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Conte's pre-1959 exercise asks whether a model can make a cultural break before that break appears in its training history. Human authors add risk and lived meaning to the result, which sound quality alone cannot supply.</p></div>

## Give generation a job that can fail

Keep the song experiment, but change the assignment. Ask a living composer to design an original adaptive piece. A listener chooses a short motif, and the system writes a reply in real time under the composer's rules.

A static track cannot answer a new motif. A library of written branches works only for motifs the composer prepared. Generation adds behavior the replacements lack when the listener offers an allowed motif outside that library.

The implementation can stay small. A [`TypedDict`](https://docs.python.org/3/library/typing.html#typing.TypedDict) is a Python type hint that lists the expected fields in the song's shared state, such as the chosen motif and composer approval. It helps a type checker during development, while runtime validation still needs ordinary code.

[LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/graph-api) is a library for workflows that branch or loop. Its `StateGraph` routes shared state from listener input to composer approval. [ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) is a pattern where a model chooses an action, reads the observation, and uses it for the next choice. It belongs here only if the model can call a tool such as a tempo analyzer. Plain code should enforce the composer's rules.

Use licensed source material and delete listener data after the session. The composer approves release. A compact state contract keeps those decisions visible, which is the same discipline behind [treating the spec as the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/).

I think this brief is stronger than "make a lost Beatles song" because the replacement test can reject the model.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The medium-dependent test replaces the model with a static track or written branches. Generation earns a role only when that swap breaks the intended experience.</p></div>

## Change can mean rebuilding

Conte describes Pomplamoose's career as an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390). YouTube changed discovery. Spotify weakened the band's iTunes model. COVID changed recording, and vertical video forced another rebuild.

Pomplamoose survived those shifts by rebuilding how it reached listeners. The 1895 analogy still gives us a hypothesis to test.

I don't know whether generative AI will find a native creative grammar. I will believe the analogy when we can name a serious work whose central experience collapses when a fixed system replaces the model and whose human author remains easy to name.

Criticism remains useful when [polished output converges toward cultural elevator music](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). The adaptive song gives builders a concrete test for anything beyond imitation.

Stop asking AI to impersonate the Beatles. Build the first work that could only exist after the camera left row ten.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The 1895 frame is a testable bet. A serious AI work should fail the replacement test while keeping its human author visible.</p></div>
