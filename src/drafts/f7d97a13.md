In 1895, the Lumière brothers filmed a train arriving at a station and called it cinema. In 2026, we prompt an LLM to write a poem and call the result AI creativity. I think we are making the same early mistake.

Jack Conte, musician and Patreon co-founder, names that mistake in [his talk on creativity and AI](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc). Slop is "that lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium" when people reproduce work humans already do better.

The flood is ugly. It may also be temporary. I have written about [the elevator music already playing](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). Conte adds a second possibility: sameness can be the sound of an immature medium.

## Cinema found its language in the cut

The camera first behaved like a seat in the audience.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Early film copied theatre and looked diminished beside it. Méliès used the cut to create an experience the stage could never reproduce.</p></div>

Many early films placed a fixed camera in front of theatrical action. Filmmakers were still borrowing the grammar of the stage. After seeing a Lumière programme in 1896, Maxim Gorky described ["a world without sound, without colour"](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/read/91ddc4e0-5576-4f98-a3b6-ba8a97703eda/section/2fe1a387-c815-45d7-9609-2a47615dc9e8), with everything "dipped in monotonous grey."

His reaction makes sense. Film had flattened theatre onto a screen and removed its voices.

Georges Méliès approached the machine as a stage magician. He filmed an empty box, stopped the camera, placed a person inside, then resumed. The joined footage made the person appear instantly. That join became part of cinema's grammar.

By 1902, Méliès was using comparable effects in [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/603--a-trip-to-the-moon-le-voyage-dans-la-lune/). A cannon fires. The film cuts to a rocket moving through space and eventually striking the Moon's eye. The audience suddenly travels somewhere no theatre seat could take them.

When I compare that moment with generative media now, I think we are still pointing the camera at the stage.

## New media can wound before they mature

Creative possibility can arrive with real economic harm.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Recorded sound and electronic instruments threatened real livelihoods. Workers absorbed the cost of the transition long before the artistic value was clear.</p></div>

When talkies reached cinemas in 1927, about 22,000 theatre musicians lost their jobs, according to the [American Federation of Musicians](https://www.afm.org/125-years-afm-history/). The union called recorded accompaniment "canned music" and formed the Music Defense League in 1930. Those musicians were defending their livelihoods.

The Hammond Novachord met a similar response in 1939. AFM rules kept the electronic instrument from reducing an orchestra's headcount. Baumol and Bowen later named the pressure facing live performance "cost disease": a musical work still demands human time while other industries get faster.

Artists eventually found another language. The Beatles used tape loops and backward sound on 1966's [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver). Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years, then built the closing address of [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/7-The-Great-Dictator) around the human voice in 1940.

I think optimism becomes dishonest when it edits out the livelihoods that disappeared. We can keep those people in the frame while noticing that the art kept moving.

## Slop is the fixed camera

AI looks weakest when it impersonates a mature human craft.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A generated poem or hand-drawn imitation tests AI against forms humans already understand. Exploration begins when creators search for a move that belongs to the machine.</p></div>

Ask an image model for something that looks hand-drawn, or an LLM for a poem that sounds like a poet. Both prompts keep the old stage and change the performer.

Conte poses a harder thought experiment. Imagine a music model trained only on work released through 1959. Would it reach the Beatles or Prince on its own? We cannot know. Prediction can recombine the past, but cultural invention often begins with a person willing to look ridiculous.

Conte credits creator Karen X Cheng with a sharp version of this: difficulty and scarcity give art part of its weight. A model cannot be ridiculed after a failed experiment. That social risk lands on the person who releases the work.

Méliès' rocket in the Moon's eye is the sci-fi image I use for AI. Film became interesting when an impossible cut did work the stage could not. I think the native form of generative media will arrive through a similarly strange move, rather than a cleaner imitation.

## Exploration is the work

Conte has lived through several supposed endings as one half of Pomplamoose.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Pomplamoose survived repeated changes to YouTube and music distribution by rebuilding its method. AI deserves the same willingness to explore, with humans still carrying authorship and risk.</p></div>

Pomplamoose's growth flattened around YouTube's Cosmic Panda redesign. Streaming changed the economics that had worked on iTunes. Later, short-form video changed distribution again. Conte calls the pattern an infinite sine wave. Each successful method eventually breaks, forcing another attempt.

His example also keeps the human in view. *good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries weight because Kendrick Lamar made it from a life he lived. A generated imitation cannot inherit that life. This is why [human reality matters more as production gets cheap](/posts/2026-04-11-when-ai-makes-building-cheap-human-reality-starts-to-matter-more/).

I don't know what AI's equivalent of the cut will be. It may be a work that changes with every participant while still bearing a creator's choices. It may be something stranger.

Slop deserves criticism when it exploits artists or floods attention. I think criticism becomes useful when it pushes us beyond imitation. For now, I would rather see one awkward experiment with the machine's own grammar than another thousand polished copies of the stage.
