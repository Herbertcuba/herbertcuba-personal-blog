In 1895, the Lumière brothers filmed a train arriving at a station and called it cinema. Critics called it lifeless. In 2026, we prompt an LLM to write a poem and call it AI-generated content. We are making the same mistake.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every new creative medium starts by imitating the medium before it. That imitation phase looks like slop, because it is slop. AI is in that phase now, and the phase ends when people start doing things the previous medium never could.</p></div>

Maxim Gorky sat in a Lumière screening in 1896 and wrote about what he saw. "A world without sound, without colour. Everything there, the earth, the trees, the people, the water, the air, is dipped in monotonous grey." He called it the Kingdom of Shadows. He was describing the exact same feeling you get scrolling AI-generated images at 1am. Gray. Competent. Dead.

I spent last week with a talk by Jack Conte at SXSW 2026, "The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI." Conte is the co-founder and CEO of Patreon, and half of the band Pomplamoose. He is a platform executive and a working artist, which means he gets paid by the machine and hurt by it at the same time. His argument: AI is in its 1895 phase. I think he is right, and I think the engineering view underneath his argument is even stronger than he makes it.

## The pattern repeats, every time

Four times in a hundred years, a new creative machine arrived, the professionals panicked, and the early output was garbage. Then someone broke the pattern and defined what the machine was actually for.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Cinema, recorded music, sound film, and the synthesizer all went through the same arc: imitation, panic, then a pattern-breaker who invented the medium's native language. The receipts include 22,000 lost musician jobs and a union manifesto from 1929 that reads like a 2026 AI take.</p></div>

Cinema first. The early filmmakers pointed their cameras at stages and recorded theater. That was the whole idea of film for years. Then in 1902, Georges Méliès invented the cut. A Trip to the Moon had special effects, match cuts, a story that moved between places. It was impossible to perform on a stage, which is exactly the point. Cinema stopped being recorded theater and became cinema.

Recorded music next. In the 1920s and 30s, the American Federation of Musicians ran newspaper ads against "canned music," one of them showing a robot feeding live musicians into a meat grinder. By 1930, the union estimated that 22,000 moviehouse musicians had lost their jobs as talking pictures replaced live accompaniment. In 1942 the union's president, James Petrillo, went further and banned all commercial recording. No union musician entered a studio for two years. It remains the longest strike in entertainment history.

My favorite receipt is from the union's 1929 manifesto: "Musical authorities know that the soul of the Art is lost in mechanization. The quality of music is dependent on the mood of the artist, upon the human contact, without which the essence of intellectual stimulation and emotional rapture is lost." Read that again. Swap "mechanization" for "generative AI" and you have a LinkedIn post from last Tuesday. The panic is a hundred years old, word for word.

What ended the canned music era? Sgt. Pepper's lonely studio band. In 1966 the Beatles recorded Revolver, an album they never intended to perform live, built from tape loops, backwards guitar, and orchestral collage. George Martin, their producer, described the studio as "a new canvas on which to paint." The recording stopped being a photograph of a performance and became the artwork itself.

Sound film got the same treatment from its loudest critic. Charlie Chaplin held out against talkies for thirteen years, releasing City Lights in 1931 and Modern Times in 1936 as silent films while the whole industry converted. He said he loathed talkies. Then in 1940 he released The Great Dictator, and the final speech, a human standing at a microphone pleading for decency, is one of the most famous monologues in film history. The man who resisted sound longest used it best.

And the synthesizer. In 1939, Hammond unveiled the Novachord at the New York World's Fair, the first commercial polyphonic synthesizer. One hundred and sixty-three vacuum tubes, over a thousand hand-wired capacitors, nearly 500 pounds. As Conte tells it, the musicians' union announced a prohibition of the instrument. Eighty years later, the synthesizer is the sound of Stevie Wonder, Prince, and Daft Punk.

## Slop is the medium's infancy

Conte's definition of slop is the sharpest one I have heard, so I will give it to you in full: "That lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium where we're all just trying to reproduce existing things that are honestly just better if the people do them."

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>We judge every new medium through the reference points of the old one. AI making images that look hand-drawn, or video that looks shot on a camera, is 1895 filmmakers pointing a camera at a stage. Slop is a symptom of youth.</p></div>

Notice what we ask AI to do. Make something that looks hand-drawn. Make something that looks filmed. Write something that sounds like a person wrote it. Every one of those requests defines success as a convincing imitation of the previous medium. A 2026 prompt that says "make it look like a photograph" is a 1895 director saying "frame it like a stage play." Same instinct, same limitation.

I think this is why so much AI output feels gray even when it is technically impressive. The Gorky feeling. The output is measured against a thing humans already do better, and it loses, and we conclude the machine is hollow. Gorky concluded the same about cinema. Cinema was not hollow. It was seven years old.

There is a moment in 2001: A Space Odyssey that I keep returning to. HAL 9000 can play chess, monitor the ship, read lips. Flawless competence inside the rules of every game it was given. What HAL never does is invent a new game. Kubrick got something right in 1968 that we are rediscovering now: a system trained to master existing patterns is, by construction, the wrong tool for breaking them. More on that in a minute, because it turns out to be the whole engineering story.

## The honest part

Here is where most AI optimism pieces lie to you, and I do not want to. The historical pattern vindicates the medium. It does not vindicate the economics, and it does not comfort the people inside the transition.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The 22,000 musicians never got their jobs back. Orchestras really did decline. Training on artists' work without consent is a real injury, and Conte says so on stage. The pattern promises the art form survives. It promises nothing to any individual artist.</p></div>

Those 22,000 moviehouse musicians did not transition into studio careers. The jobs ended. And the fear did not stop in 1942. Economists William Baumol and William Bowen named the underlying problem in 1966: the cost disease of the performing arts. A Haydn symphony takes 25 minutes to perform. It took 25 minutes in 1790 and it takes 25 minutes now. Productivity cannot improve without destroying the thing itself. A Stanford GSB study from 2008 found that 46 of the 63 largest orchestras in the United States ran deficits. Sometimes the pessimists are right about the numbers.

Conte does not skip this, and it is why his talk works. "I'm so upset about how this tech has rolled out," he says. "Our work has been used without our consent, without paying us, and without giving us credit." Then, in the same breath: "I feel like I get to be alive in 1895 for the invention of the cinematograph." Both feelings at once. Anger about the terms, gratitude about the timing. I recognize that tension from my own work. I build AI systems and I write about what they cost. I do not know how to hold only one of those.

## What the machine cannot do

Now the argument that matters most for people like us, the ones building with these models every day. Conte runs a thought experiment: train a model on every piece of music recorded up to 1959. Could it produce the Beatles? Nirvana without Kurt Cobain? Biggie, Beyoncé, Frank Ocean, Jacob Collier? His answer: "Leaps like this require a spirit of rulebreaking and invention that I haven't yet seen demonstrated by these models." He hedges honestly. "I'm not saying it's impossible. But that tech does not exist today."

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An LLM is a pattern-completion engine by architecture, and the training process actively optimizes against deviation. Cultural leaps require deviation. The gap in the machine is the job description for the human.</p></div>

Here is the part Conte, the artist, gestures at, and that I, as an engineer, can state more bluntly. When you train a language model, you teach it to predict the most likely next token given everything before it. Then comes a stage called RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback, where human raters score the model's outputs and the model is tuned to produce more of what raters prefer. Think about what that does. It compresses the output toward the center of the distribution. Toward the answer most people would rate as good. RLHF is, mechanically, an anti-weirdness machine.

I think about this every time a model gives me a perfectly reasonable, perfectly median answer. The median is the product. Breaking the normal distribution is precisely what the system is optimized to refuse.

Conte circles the same idea from the artist's side, through comedy. Hasan Minhaj has an interview where he explains that great comedy involves risk: comedians operate outside the normal range of what is socially acceptable to say. They defy the collective wisdom. And there is a line in the film Sing Street, the older brother talking to the kid starting a band: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed." Conte's question lands hard: "If an LLM says some crazy shit, it's not interesting to me. What's it risking? Who cares?"

Nothing. It risks nothing. It has no reputation to lose, no rent to pay, no audience to alienate, no thirteen years of pride on the line like Chaplin had. Art is made by the stakes, and the stakes are ours.

A creator named Keredex Chang, quoted by Conte, put the mechanism in one sentence: "Art is scarcity. Art is not what's easy to make, it's what's hard." Conte's example is the invisible selfie stick. It was a genuinely hard trick once, a camera on a pole that software erases, so the footage looks like a drone following you. The first videos were stunning. Then everyone could do it, and now nobody watches them. Easy became infinite, and infinite became boring.

This is why Kendrick Lamar is Conte's closing exhibit. He picks good kid, m.A.A.d city and says the record is special because of Kendrick. Who he is, what he went through, the connection you feel listening. The sound is the delivery mechanism. The person is the content. Conte hates the word "content" for exactly this reason: it puts a beautiful album and a video about plunging your toilet in the same category.

## Your job is the leap

Conte ends with his own band's story, and it is the right ending. Pomplamoose built an audience on YouTube, and then the algorithm changed and their growth flatlined. They rebuilt around iTunes, and then Spotify ate iTunes. They rebuilt again, hit a million monthly listeners, and then COVID killed touring. Then TikTok rewired music discovery again. Four times the ground moved. He calls it the infinite sine wave, and his conclusion is the anthem: "Change does not mean death. You can get back up and you can fucking go again."

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI makes easy output infinite, which makes hard, risky, human work more scarce and more valuable. If you build with these models, the unwritten vocabulary of the medium is your opening. Be Méliès, not the camera crew filming a stage play.</p></div>

Notice the flip hiding inside the scarcity argument. If art is scarcity, then a technology that makes easy output infinite is not the end of art. It is a repricing of it. Every infinite scroll of gray slop raises the value of the thing that was hard to make, the thing somebody risked something on, the thing that could only have come from one specific person. AI does not devalue the human part of the work. It isolates it.

For the engineers reading this, the ones wiring models into products right now: you are not the audience for this transition, you are the film crew. You decide what the machine gets pointed at. Point it at reproducing what humans already do, and you get slop, and slop deserves its reviews. Point it at something that could not exist without it, and you might be the person who invents the cut.

The artists of 2076 will trace their vocabulary back to someone working right now, in the slop era, who refused to just reproduce. Conte's last line is the one I would put on the wall: "Everything that makes anything matter exists because something alive once did. Humans will do that for a long, long time."

It is 1895. The camera works. Go make the cut.
