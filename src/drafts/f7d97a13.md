In 1895, the Lumière brothers introduced the Cinématographe. Their train film reached a public program in 1896. Maxim Gorky watched one of their screenings and [described](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=290) "a world without sound, without color," dipped in monotonous gray.

In 2026, we ask a large language model to write a Beatles song and call the result AI creativity. A large language model, or LLM, learns patterns from huge amounts of data and predicts the next piece of an output. The machine works, but the assignment is gray.

I tried to read the flood of synthetic poems, images and songs as a model-quality problem. I think that diagnosis is too small. In his SXSW talk, [*The future of creativity on the internet in a world with AI*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=841), Jack Conte gives slop a more useful frame: the lazy period when a new medium copies work people already make better.

Slop may be a symptom of transition, which makes exploration more useful than disgust.

## Every medium begins by copying

The first filmmakers carried the theater stage into a camera frame.

Early narrative films often fixed a camera before a painted set. Gorky's review was fair.

Georges Méliès changed the job. His 1902 film [*A Trip to the Moon*](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=370) used cuts and camera tricks to jump between places. Decades later, *2001: A Space Odyssey* cut from a bone to a spacecraft and crossed millions of years in a fraction of a second. That sci-fi cut uses time in a way a stage cannot.

Conte shows American Federation of Musicians [campaigns against "canned music"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=712) and cites the union's [1930 estimate](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=1312) that sound film had displaced 22,000 moviehouse musicians. A commercial recording ban followed in 1942. Working artists paid the bill.

The Beatles used tape loops and reversed sounds on [*Revolver*](https://www.thebeatles.com/revolver) in 1966. Conte attributes a good phrase to producer George Martin: the studio was "a new canvas on which they had to paint." I could not verify Martin's exact wording in a primary source.

Charlie Chaplin resisted synchronized dialogue for years. In 1940, [*The Great Dictator*](https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/films/7-The-Great-Dictator) ended with a humanist speech that traveled beyond one theater.

Introduced in 1939, the Hammond Novachord used 163 vacuum tubes and weighed nearly 500 pounds. [Conte shows an AFM prohibition](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=1830) driven by fear that one machine could replace an orchestra. Baumol and Bowen later named the performing arts' [cost disease](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/no-easy-remedy-symphony-orchestra-cost-disease): a symphony takes roughly the same time to perform while other costs rise.

I think optimism that skips those losses is cheap. History offers a pattern without a guarantee. Plenty of media vanish. Survivors find a native move before imitation exhausts the audience.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Young media borrow an older form because creators have no other grammar yet. Film found editing, recorded music found the studio, and each discovery opened work the older medium could not hold.</p></div>

## A model can search. A person can risk.

Conte's strongest argument begins with a model trained only on music recorded before 1959.

[Would that model discover the Beatles](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2835), then reach Prince or Nirvana? We cannot run that experiment. A model can explore combinations inside its training distribution. It has no reputation to put at stake when it breaks a rule.

Hasan Minhaj places comedy outside the normal range of socially acceptable speech. *Sing Street* says it with less theory: "Rock and roll is a risk. You risk being ridiculed."

The failed Beatles prompt costs the model nothing. A musician who releases an odd song under their own name carries the response. I think that human exposure is part of the work rather than extra context around it.

Karen X Cheng [describes art as scarcity](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=3089). An effect loses cultural weight once a camera turns it into a preset. Conte reaches the same conclusion through Kendrick Lamar. *good kid, m.A.A.d city* carries the experience of the person who made it, beyond its sound.

Berlin-based artists Holly Herndon and Mat Dryhurst offer a responsible example in [*The Call*](https://www.serpentinegalleries.org/whats-on/holly-herndon-mat-dryhurst-the-call/). They recorded fifteen UK choirs for choral AI models and included contributors in a Data Trust experiment. Consent belongs to the artwork, keeping people visible inside the machine.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Models search learned patterns with extraordinary range. Culture also depends on people who attach a name, a life and the risk of ridicule to a choice the data says is strange.</p></div>

## Make the song depend on the model

Return to our fake Beatles song and give it a job a recording cannot perform.

Imagine an opt-in listener chooses one motif after each play. The next version remembers that choice. A human composer owns one rule: every version contains a full bar of silence. The model may move the silence and compose around the chosen motif. It cannot delete either constraint.

Memory alone fails the test. Games have remembered players for decades. Recommendation systems personalize queues. A database can store a motif, and a rules engine can select a prewritten branch.

Replace the generative model with those older tools. If the listener receives the same experience, the model was decorative. In our living song, the model has to write a fresh score around the retained motif. A finite template library eventually repeats itself.

`TypedDict` describes fields in a Python dictionary, such as `listener_motif` and `composer_rule`. It helps a type checker catch misspelled fields. Runtime safety still needs a validator.

[LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/graph-api) is a Python framework for workflows that branch or repeat. Its `StateGraph` connects steps through shared state. Here, a rejected score loops back for revision.

The loop borrows the shape of [ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629), which means reasoning and acting. A model acts, reads the result and chooses its next action. Here, the validator's rejection is the result. Durable listener memory still belongs in a database or LangGraph checkpointer, since graph state may last for only one run.

This stays at concept level. A runnable build needs a music model, a structured score, an audio renderer and tests. The validator has to inspect an actual silent measure instead of a text marker.

I think the composer's rule is where authorship becomes legible. [The spec is the cognitive work](/posts/the-spec-is-the-cognitive-work/) because the boundary survives even when the output changes.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A generative medium earns its place when a static template or rules engine cannot deliver the same experience. A living song can adapt to one listener while a human composer's hard rule survives every model call.</p></div>

## Give exploration a small budget

A two-week experiment can test the living song without turning it into an endless innovation program.

<div class="spec-doc">
<span class="spec-doc__label">experiment card · living song</span>
<ul>
<li><strong>Owner:</strong> one junior AI engineer builds it; the composer owns the silence rule; a product sponsor makes the stop or scale call.</li>
<li><strong>Scope:</strong> two weeks, twenty opt-in listeners, score generation before polished audio.</li>
<li><strong>Data:</strong> store the chosen motif and accepted score; give each listener a delete control; exclude listener data from training.</li>
<li><strong>Cost ceiling:</strong> €500 in model and rendering spend.</li>
<li><strong>Success:</strong> at least twelve listeners identify the retained motif and report that the adaptation changed the second listen.</li>
<li><strong>Stop:</strong> end the pilot after any composer-rule breach, or when a rules-engine baseline performs equally well.</li>
</ul>
</div>

Those numbers are a proposed decision rule, set before the first model call. The result can fail cleanly. That beats a praised demo with no owner.

Conte calls Pomplamoose's career an ["infinite sine wave"](https://youtu.be/17_HcR95YBc?t=2390). YouTube changed. Streaming replaced the iTunes model. COVID broke the band's recording setup, then vertical video changed discovery again. The band kept rebuilding, while each rebuild cost time and income.

AI will compress that wave. A native pattern can become a preset as soon as it spreads, which is the pull I explored in [The Elevator Music Already Started Playing](/posts/the-elevator-music-already-started-playing/). Exploration has to continue after the first useful form becomes easy.

I don't know whether a living song will matter by 2030. I think the defining AI work will require generation to change the experience while a human decision remains intact through every change. If celebrated AI work is still judged mainly by how well it imitates an older artifact, this prediction failed.

A junior engineer can spend 2026 polishing a fake Beatles track. I would rather see one build a song that can exist only because the listener, the composer and the model each hold a different part of it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Give one junior engineer a bounded experiment with consent, a cost ceiling and a stop rule. Scale only if generative adaptation changes the listener's experience and the composer's boundary holds.</p></div>
