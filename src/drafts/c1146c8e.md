OpenClaw is an open-source AI agent: software that can take multi-step actions instead of only producing answers. It is self-hosted, which means users can run it on their own machines and let it work through messaging apps, a browser, and the computer’s command line.

That project still looks dominant if you watch its public counter. On 17 August 2026, [OpenClaw’s repository](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw) on GitHub, the code-hosting platform, showed 386,457 stars, cumulative bookmarks that users leave to signal interest. Yet the ability it packaged now appears across a widening field of agents. The contrast is real, although it does not prove that OpenClaw lost its users.

My take is that OpenClaw’s most important result was never category ownership. It made large-scale demand for agents that act impossible to dismiss. Once that demand was visible, competitors had both a product brief and a reason to remove the friction around it.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>OpenClaw’s public count kept growing as action-taking agents became a broader market. That is evidence of demand validation, not proof of retained use or lasting category control.</p>
</div>

## The demand signal was action

Peter Steinberger, the Austrian software developer who created OpenClaw, first released it under other names. Its [official history](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw/blob/main/docs/start/lore.md) records the late-January 2026 sequence as ClawdBot, Moltbot, and finally OpenClaw on 30 January.

Action-taking AI already existed; Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI models, had introduced computer use earlier. OpenClaw’s breakthrough was the package: local control through messaging apps such as WhatsApp and Telegram, combined with browser operation, background work, and shell access. A shell is the text interface that runs commands on a computer, so the agent had meaningful authority over the user’s environment.

That authority also explains the pressure that followed. By 17 August, the project’s [official security page](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw/security/advisories) listed 143 advisories, public reports of weaknesses rather than attacks. One, [CVE-2026-25253](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw/security/advisories/GHSA-g55j-c2v4-pjcg), used the standard vulnerability identifier for a one-click remote-code-execution chain that could run attacker-controlled code; version 2026.1.29 fixed it. For enterprises, a local flaw becomes a governance issue because the agent may hold credentials and access internal systems.

Model access added a second pressure. Anthropic’s [consumer terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/consumer-terms) prohibit automated access unless a tool uses an approved application programming interface, or API, or has explicit permission. Here, the API is the metered interface software uses to call a model. Under [token-based pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing), billed by units of model input and output, cost varies by workload. Provider policy therefore shapes the economics even without a universal price increase.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>OpenClaw made action convenient by giving an agent meaningful access to a user’s machine. The same permissions created security consequences, while model-provider rules made operating cost depend on access policy and workload.</p>
</div>

## GitHub stars preserve attention, not use

Those stars normally remain after someone stops testing a project, so a rising total can show historical reach but not active users, retention, or where people went next.

The total still represents extraordinary attention. Yet neither the count nor quieter online conversation establishes a lasting user habit, abandonment, or migration to one rival. The useful conclusion is narrower: OpenClaw validated interest at scale, while its current user base remains unknown.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Stars accumulate attention rather than measure continuing use. OpenClaw’s count supports the demand thesis, but it cannot support claims about retention, decline, or user migration.</p>
</div>

## The capability spread faster than the advantage

Once the demand signal became visible, action itself stopped being a rare distinction. Model vendors continued adding computer control, while open-source teams built other agent harnesses, the software around an AI model that supplies tools, memory, and a task loop.

Hermes Agent is one example. Developed by the AI research company Nous Research, the [open-source project](https://github.com/NousResearch/hermes-agent) showed 231,792 GitHub stars on 17 August. Its [documentation](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/user-guide/features/skills) describes saved context across sessions, reusable skills, and post-task skill refinement. Those facts establish a different learning design and substantial attention. They do not establish migration from OpenClaw or lower security and pricing risk.

Managed agents change the trade-off by running more of the system in a vendor’s cloud. That can reduce local installation and maintenance, but it relocates trust and the attack surface, the possible points an attacker could exploit, to the provider. When several systems can act, buyers start comparing setup, permission control, reliability, and cost.

OpenClaw’s early visibility created a lead, but the visible capability alone could not make it durable. What compounds over time has to come from the surrounding system; early attention by itself fades.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Competitors reproduced the action capability and offered different ways to manage memory, infrastructure, and trust. As it spread, defensibility moved into the surrounding product and operating model.</p>
</div>

## Validating demand is only the first job

Explosive adoption can outrun the capacity needed to review code safely. Every integration adds failure paths, and every pull request, meaning a proposed code change, needs review. On 17 August, OpenClaw had 1,243 open issues and 1,338 open pull requests, yet its 100 most recently merged changes occurred within roughly 15.5 hours. Together, those figures describe a project carrying heavy demand while still merging changes quickly. They leave the cause of any specific vulnerability unresolved.

The leadership story also needs care. On 14 February 2026, [Steinberger announced](https://steipete.me/posts/2026/openclaw) that he would join OpenAI, the company behind the ChatGPT assistant, and that OpenClaw would move to a foundation intended to keep it open and independent. This was a governance plan rather than an OpenAI acquisition; the foundation’s final legal status remains unverified. Current merge activity shows that the project is still active.

I think the strategic lesson is stronger without a collapse story. OpenClaw showed what a personal AI agent could look like at scale: locally controlled, reachable through familiar messaging apps, and able to carry work across several steps. That demand proof matters even if the project never owns the whole category.

A clear demand signal also gives competitors a map. They can copy the capability, study where users encounter friction, and choose a different balance of local control and managed operation. A durable edge comes from what keeps improving afterward: reliability learned through use, trusted distribution, and enough capacity to govern change as fast as it arrives.

OpenClaw proved that people wanted agents that act. Which project ultimately serves that market will depend on who can sustain the consequences of action after the novelty has passed.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>OpenClaw remains active and its scale creates real review pressure, but neither fact determines the category winner. Demand validation opened the market; lasting leadership depends on systems that keep AI action reliable, governable, and economical.</p>
</div>
