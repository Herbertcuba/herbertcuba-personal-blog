On August 17, 2026, [OpenClaw's public GitHub repository](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw) showed 386,514 stars. OpenClaw is open-source software that runs on a user's computer or server and lets an AI act through messages, a browser, and other tools. GitHub is where developers publish and collaborate on software.

A [GitHub star](https://docs.github.com/en/get-started/exploring-projects-on-github/saving-repositories-with-stars) saves a project or signals interest in it. It does not record an installation, a renewal, a payment, or a useful result. Yet that huge total sat beside a very different public signal: the BetterWay video [*What Happened to OpenClaw?*](https://youtu.be/moRJqv1z6ww). The title suggests decline. The repository suggests momentum. Neither tells us how many people kept using the product.

My take is that OpenClaw achieved category validation without yet proving category capture. Category validation means showing enough interest that other teams see an opportunity. Category capture means becoming the product people keep choosing.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>OpenClaw proved exceptional developer interest in AI that acts, while its star count says nothing about repeat use.</p></div>

## Why the product shape mattered

Peter Steinberger, the software developer who created OpenClaw, opened the repository on November 24, 2025. The project's [official history](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw/blob/main/docs/start/lore.md) records its later names: ClawdBot became Moltbot on January 27, 2026, then OpenClaw on January 30.

OpenClaw works as a self-hosted gateway: software that connects AI models, messaging services, and tools while running on hardware controlled by the user. An AI agent can then take several steps toward a goal instead of only writing a reply. OpenClaw supports WhatsApp and Telegram, [browser control](https://docs.openclaw.ai/tools/browser), [shell commands](https://docs.openclaw.ai/tools/exec)—instructions that operate files and programs—and [background tasks](https://docs.openclaw.ai/automation/tasks).

OpenClaw did not invent action-taking AI. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, introduced [computer use](https://www.anthropic.com/news/3-5-models-and-computer-use) in October 2024. OpenClaw's achievement was packaging local control, familiar messaging, and practical action in a form that attracted extraordinary attention.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>OpenClaw made a powerful combination easy to understand: message an AI, and let it do the work on your own system.</p></div>

## When action becomes the risk

A wrong text answer can be ignored. A wrong command can change a file, expose a password, or send information to the wrong person. The access that makes an agent useful also makes mistakes more costly.

By August 17, OpenClaw's repository listed [143 public security advisories](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw/security/advisories), including 22 rated critical. A security advisory is a public notice about a software flaw, not evidence that an attack succeeded. One [critical advisory](https://github.com/advisories/GHSA-g55j-c2v4-pjcg) described a route to remote code execution, meaning an attacker could run commands on another person's machine. Maintainers fixed it in version 2026.1.29.

Local software can still depend on outside suppliers. [Anthropic's consumer terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/consumer-terms) require automated access to Claude to use an API key unless Anthropic permits another route. An API key is a software credential for its developer service, which [charges by usage](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing). A provider can therefore change both access rules and costs for a locally hosted agent.

Popularity also creates maintenance work. The same repository snapshot recorded 1,243 open issues and 1,338 pull requests. Issues are reported problems or requests; pull requests are proposed code changes. OpenClaw had also published 25 releases since July 19. Those numbers show a heavy review burden and an active project at the same time—not abandonment or proof that the backlog caused a specific flaw.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>For an AI that can act, security, supplier rules, cost, and maintenance are part of the product itself.</p></div>

## Stars keep attention, not customers

GitHub stars are cumulative. Unless someone removes a star, it remains in the total. The number can keep rising after a product's risks, costs, or competitive position have changed.

No reproducible public series supports the proposed story that search interest collapsed while stars continued climbing. Repository data also leaves user migration unknown. We should not turn missing usage data into a confident decline narrative.

OpenClaw's stars support the first claim. They do not prove the second. They show that a remarkable number of developers cared enough to notice or save the project. They do not show who stayed, paid, or gained lasting value.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Stars preserve evidence of attention, but they do not measure loyalty, retention, or useful work.</p></div>

## Action became the starting line

Once a product shape is easy to understand, competitors do not need to copy its code. They can offer a similar result with different tradeoffs. OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, had already announced [ChatGPT agent](https://openai.com/index/introducing-chatgpt-agent/) in July 2025. It is a managed agent, meaning the provider runs its working environment. That can remove local setup and maintenance, although it moves more trust and data handling to the provider.

Open-source alternatives expanded too. [Hermes Agent](https://github.com/NousResearch/hermes-agent), built by the AI research company Nous Research, listed its first formal GitHub release on March 12, 2026. Its [documentation](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/) describes persistent memory, reusable skills, and review after a task. On August 17, its repository had recorded 231,792 stars.

That number does not prove that OpenClaw users moved to Hermes. It also does not prove that Hermes is safer or cheaper. What it does show is that buyers and developers had more choices for software that could act.

Once action is available in several forms, action itself stops being a durable edge. Products must compete on setup, reliability, control, recovery, cost, and how well they improve through repeated work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>As action became available through managed and self-hosted products, OpenClaw's defining capability became less unique.</p></div>

## The proof and the prize are different

OpenClaw's achievement does not depend on an obituary. The project remains active. Its deeper contribution was making a large pool of developer interest in locally controlled, action-taking AI impossible to ignore. That is meaningful evidence of appetite, even though it is not proof of retained or paid demand.

The durable edge comes from what compounds around the action: reliable results, clear limits, easy recovery, predictable cost, and useful history that customers can take with them.

Two simple building blocks help. A permission layer is software outside the AI model that allows or blocks important actions. A portable work record is a customer-controlled export of tasks, rules, results, corrections, and costs. One makes control testable. The other keeps valuable learning from being trapped with one supplier.

For buyers, the test is straightforward. Does the agent complete the same useful work more reliably over time? Can people see, limit, and undo important actions? Can the work record move if the supplier changes? Weak answers mean the demonstration may be impressive without being defensible.

OpenClaw showed others where to look. It did not automatically secure the final prize. The product that validates a category and the product that eventually captures it can be different products. That is not failure. It is how a new market learns what really matters.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>OpenClaw made the opportunity visible. Lasting value depends on repeat results, enforceable control, and customer-owned learning.</p></div>
