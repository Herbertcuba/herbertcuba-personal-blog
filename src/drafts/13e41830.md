Calling *AI 2040* a validation of *The Three Crucibles* would be convenient, and wrong. [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), which appeared publicly in July 2026, is a policy recommendation written as a conditional scenario. Its authors say on page two that it is primarily a recommendation, not their best guess about what will happen.

The AI Futures Project is the forecasting group behind [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), a 2025 scenario in which automated AI research triggers a rapid intelligence explosion. Four of that report's five authors are among the six authors of *AI 2040*. A teaser and downloadable PDF for [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) were public on cubagarcia.com by March 23, 2026, and the current PDF edition is dated June 24. That public order does not prove influence or predictive success.

It does make the structural overlap worth examining. Both documents map a break between machine production and wage-based income, both arrive at a Citizen's Dividend, and both end at a choice between broad prosperity and concentrated power. I think their disagreement is even more useful: *AI 2040* puts its weight on verification, while my book puts more weight on ownership.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p><em>AI 2040</em> is not observed evidence confirming <em>The Three Crucibles</em>. It is a later, conditional policy scenario that reaches the same economic fault line before proposing a different safeguard.</p></div>

## The overlap is economic, not chronological

The three crucibles are a sequence. The Correction, set in 2026, argues that AI can remove labor from the old productivity equation. The Transformation, spanning 2027 to 2032, asks how demand and public revenue survive when fewer people earn wages. The Alignment, set from 2035 onward, asks whether society becomes a steward of abundance or lets the owners of productive systems become a new ruling class.

*AI 2040* starts with a different clock. Its scenario has the United States and China reach a deal in 2029, avoiding fully automated AI research in 2030. The world then scales AI systems within the range of human ability, pauses in 2035 when they match or exceed top human experts across fields, and proceeds to superintelligence in 2040. Superintelligence here means systems broadly more capable than the best humans and able to work faster and more cheaply.

Yet the economic mechanism converges. On pages 17 to 19, the scenario has the share of labor performed by AI systems and robots, weighted by economic value, rise from about 20% in 2032 to about 85% in 2035. Wage-linked taxes shrink while companies deduct heavy investment in datacenters, factories, and robots, so public revenue no longer rises with production. *The Three Crucibles* describes the same break more starkly as “Productivity = Capital,” with labor solving to zero.

Both documents then use the same phrase, Citizen's Dividend, and both point to the Alaska Permanent Fund, a state fund created in 1976 that has paid residents annual oil-revenue dividends since 1982. I am not claiming to have coined the phrase. I think the meaningful convergence is the sequence: machine output weakens wage income, the old fiscal link breaks, and a direct citizen claim becomes necessary.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The timelines differ, but the economic chain is unusually similar: automation reduces the role of paid labor, wage-linked public revenue weakens, and a citizen dividend reconnects productive capacity to household income.</p></div>

## The funding mechanism changes the bargain

In Plan A, governments cap the amount of advanced computing capacity and robots that can be built, then sell tradable capacity permits. Compute is the processing capacity used to train and run AI systems. Because the permits become the scarce input, the scenario projects $50 trillion in United States permit revenue in fiscal year 2032 and $180 trillion in 2034, both measured in inflation-adjusted 2025 dollars. These are scenario outputs, not forecasts we should budget against.

Most of the American permit revenue eventually supports the Citizen's Dividend. A fixed fraction of the permit fees is legally assigned to a Compute Dividend Corporation; every citizen owns one share, and profits are distributed equally. This is where the Alaska comparison becomes more than a rhetorical flourish: Plan A turns public control of a scarce productive input into a legal payment right.

*The Three Crucibles* proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax instead. A company would pay according to the human-equivalent work performed by its AI systems and the public revenue that comparable human employment once produced. The permit model taxes scarce capacity; the labor-equivalence model taxes substitution. My take is that neither choice is administrative detail. Capacity permits depend on measuring hardware and preventing hidden projects, while a labor tax depends on defining comparable work as AI systems change jobs rather than merely replace them.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both maps redirect machine productivity to citizens, but through different tax bases. Plan A charges for scarce compute and robot capacity; <em>The Three Crucibles</em> charges for AI work that displaces the fiscal contribution of human employment.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership protect against different failures

Plan A's international deal relies on public AI research, monitored datacenters, tracked chips, and devices that can verify whether prohibited training runs are taking place. It also requires safety cases, meaning evidence-based arguments that a particular AI system can operate within stated risk limits. The 2035 pause is supposed to hold until systems that perform at top-expert human level can satisfy auditors and remain under human control.

The report devotes an entire appendix to the possibility of a hidden Chinese project because undisclosed compute could undermine the agreement. That is an honest stress test, and I think it reveals the plan's deepest bet: power can be constrained if the infrastructure is visible enough to inspect and the participants trust the inspection.

*The Three Crucibles* protects against another failure. Its distributed-ownership proposal spreads control of models and infrastructure across individuals, communities, and public institutions instead of leaving the intelligence layer with a few providers. Visibility alone does not give citizens a claim on the returns. At the same time, wider ownership does not by itself prove that powerful systems are safe.

I don't know whether a verification regime at this scale could survive strategic pressure, and *AI 2040* does not present its scenario as the most likely future. The important point is that transparent concentration can still be concentration, while poorly governed distribution can spread technical risk. Each map covers a danger the other treats too lightly.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification is designed to prevent secret races and loss of human control; distributed ownership is designed to prevent the gains from remaining with a small group. Neither protection can substitute for the other.</p></div>

## A stronger map needs both layers

The useful test is not whether either document guessed the correct year. It is whether its institutions still work when progress arrives earlier, later, or unevenly. A strategist can reduce that test to three questions: what technical capacity can be measured and paused, which source of public revenue survives as wages shrink, and what enforceable claim gives citizens a share of machine-created value?

Plan A gives the stronger account of international coordination, inspection, and technical control. *The Three Crucibles* gives the stronger account of ownership and the fiscal contract after labor loses economic weight. I think preparation becomes credible only when those layers meet, because safety without distribution can preserve a stable concentration of power, while distribution without safety can share the proceeds of a system nobody can reliably govern.

The dates will move. The bargain still decides whether machine abundance becomes public prosperity or private rule.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The combined map asks institutions to do two jobs at once: keep advanced AI visible and under human control, then give citizens an enforceable economic claim on what those systems produce. Machine abundance becomes broadly prosperous only when technical safety and public economic rights are designed together.</p></div>
