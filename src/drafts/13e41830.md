[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is not evidence that [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), Herbert Cuba Garcia's book-length framework for the economic stages of advanced AI, is right. Plan A is itself a conditional policy scenario. Its value lies elsewhere: an independent forecasting team reaches almost the same economic structure, then exposes where that structure is incomplete.

The AI Futures Project is the research nonprofit behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), an April 2025 scenario about rapid AI development. Four of that work's five authors also contributed to AI 2040, which describes a preferred path through competition between the United States and China, automated AI research, and eventual superintelligence. The document is explicit about its status: “Plan A is primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.”

Chronology still matters, but only within its limits. A March 23, 2026 commit in the cubagarcia.com repository added *The Three Crucibles* PDF and a homepage download link. AI 2040's official changelog records revisions in July 2026. That establishes an observable sequence of documents, not proof of public deployment, influence, or predictive accuracy.

I think the stronger result is the disagreement. Both frameworks arrive at a Citizen's Dividend and the same Alaska precedent. AI 2040 then puts its weight on verifying and limiting advanced computing capacity, while *The Three Crucibles* puts more weight on who owns productive AI and who retains a claim on its output. Those protections solve different failures.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI 2040 does not validate The Three Crucibles as data. It independently reproduces much of its economic structure, and the useful comparison begins where the two frameworks separate: technical control versus economic ownership.</p>

## The convergence is economic

Both documents follow the same causal chain. As AI systems perform more paid work, wage income and labor-tax receipts weaken. Production can keep rising while household purchasing power falls, so some of the value created by machines has to return to citizens if demand and political legitimacy are to hold.

*The Three Crucibles* describes that return as a Citizen's Dividend: a direct transfer from the productivity of AI infrastructure. Its wider proposal combines AI labor taxation, a levy on work performed by AI systems, with distributed AI ownership, meaning that productive systems should not be held only by a small group of companies or states.

Plan A reaches the same destination through a different mechanism. In its scenario, a 2029 agreement between the United States and China prevents fully automated AI research and development, where AI systems improve new AI systems with little human work, from triggering an uncontrolled capability jump in 2030. Development stays within the human range through 2035, pauses at roughly top-expert ability, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. The report defines superintelligence as systems that outperform the best humans across tasks while operating faster and more cheaply.

The economic intervention starts earlier. Plan A would cap advanced computing capacity, the chips and machines used to train and run AI, then charge for permits to use it. This cap-and-trade system limits the total amount while capturing some of its economic value. A Compute Dividend Corporation, with one share for each citizen, would distribute the permit revenue as a Citizen's Dividend.

Both works point to the Alaska Permanent Fund. Alaska voters created the fund through a 1976 constitutional amendment so oil wealth could become a lasting public asset rather than disappear into annual spending; residents received the first dividend payments in 1982. The phrase Citizen's Dividend is older than either document, but the shared label, precedent, and causal chain make this convergence more specific than a generic call for universal basic income.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The frameworks agree that AI can weaken labor income, the tax base, and consumer demand even as output rises. Both answer with a Citizen's Dividend modeled on Alaska, but one funds it through AI labor and ownership claims while the other relies on permits for scarce computing capacity.</p>
</div>

## The disagreement is about failure

Plan A's core protection is verification. Datacenters declare advanced chips, inspectors check the declarations, and operators submit safety cases: structured arguments, backed by tests, that a system can remain within stated risk limits. The design assumes that dangerous capacity can be found, measured, and stopped.

Its own covert-project appendix shows the weak point. The scenario imagines computing power equal to 500,000 Nvidia H100 AI accelerators hidden in tunnels near China's Medog Hydropower Station, with only about 200 people aware of it. This is not a report of a real facility. It is a stress test demonstrating that an invisible datacenter can evade the permits, inspections, and safety cases on which the wider plan depends.

*The Three Crucibles* has the inverse weakness. Distributed ownership can preserve a broad claim on output, but it cannot by itself prove that a powerful system is safe or stop hidden capacity. Plan A, meanwhile, gives citizens a share in its dividend corporation, yet their claim is primarily on centrally collected permit revenue rather than direct ownership of the productive AI infrastructure.

The two failures can therefore occur separately. A dividend may keep paying while a covert project crosses the technical limit. Every declared datacenter may also pass inspection while control of the productive assets remains highly concentrated. One outcome preserves distribution and loses control; the other preserves control and loses broad economic power.

[George Hotz](https://geohot.github.io//blog/jekyll/update/2026/07/11/ai-2040.html), founder of autonomous-driving company comma.ai and the tinygrad machine-learning project, attacks Plan A's rapid capability assumptions because chip fabrication and physical supply chains move more slowly. His July 2026 critique proposes “Plan L, for local,” with AI aligned to its individual owner. Rejecting Plan A's timeline does not remove the ownership question. It makes that question less dependent on a particular rapid-development forecast.

I do not know whether verification or distributed ownership will prove harder in practice. The evidence here does support a narrower conclusion: neither protection can safely stand in for the other.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI 2040 is strongest where The Three Crucibles is thin: detecting, testing, and stopping dangerous capacity. The Three Crucibles is strongest where Plan A remains centralized: preserving a durable public claim on productive output. Their failure modes are independent, so both protections are necessary.</p>
</div>

<p>HBO's science-fiction television series places lifelike artificial workers inside a park watched from a sophisticated operations center. Staff can observe, pause, and rewrite the machines, while the company owns the park and captures its value.</p>

The mapping is limited but useful. Plan A is strongest on what an operations center must see and stop; *The Three Crucibles* asks who owns the productive system and holds a claim on its output. The first answer cannot settle the second.

## A serious strategy needs two records

The comparison becomes practical when every AI strategy has to produce two separate records. A control record states what capacity and model version are covered, how performance is measured, when evidence is reviewed, which threshold stops operation, and who may authorize a restart. An economic record identifies the legal owner, the funding formula, the beneficiaries, the audit right, and the change that must be rejected if value is diverted.

For a Nordic company, regulation supplies part of the first record. Obligations for general-purpose AI models, models able to perform a broad range of tasks, have applied under the EU AI Act, the European Union's risk-based AI regulation, since August 2, 2025; the bulk of the Act began applying on August 2, 2026. Those risk duties do not create a Citizen's Dividend or decide how productivity gains should be shared.

The insurer thought experiment in *The Three Crucibles* makes the boundary concrete. It compares an incumbent with 5,000 claims adjusters against a rival using claims-handling AI agents, software that can complete multistep work with tools and limited supervision. The imagined rival cuts headcount by 40 percent and premiums by 20 percent. This is an unvalidated stress test, not an observed company case, so its job is to force an approval question rather than predict an outcome.

Before such a deployment, the control record should include the baseline error rate, the system version, the sample size and time window, a maximum approved cost, and the error threshold that triggers a pause. If a check can miss a prohibited automated decision, the allowed miss rate must be explicit and justified. The supplier contract then needs audit access, evidence delivery on a stated schedule, notice and reapproval before a version change, a pause or termination right, and proof required for restart. Product, risk, procurement, and worker representatives need named handoffs and review dates rather than permanent approval.

The economic record asks a different question: how much of the gain lowers customer prices, supports affected workers, reaches owners, or contributes to a public claim? A board can negotiate those terms before signing. It cannot create a national citizen entitlement through procurement; that requires law and a durable funding source.

This is the strategic consequence of the comparison. Document chronology does not establish truth, and scenario agreement does not establish inevitability. It does give us a sharper standard: technical permission should depend on visible, stoppable capacity, while the right to scale should also depend on an enforceable account of who receives the value. Capability may follow many timelines. Distribution remains a choice.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Require a control record that makes unsafe operation rejectable and an economic record that makes value diversion visible and contestable. Regulation and contracts can enforce the first at company level; only an explicit ownership or legal funding structure can secure the second for citizens.</p>
</div>
