Alaska settled an ownership question before its oil income became routine. In 1976, as Prudhoe Bay oil promised a flood of public money, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a public fund that reserves part of the US state's mineral revenue. The first resident dividend followed in 1982. Alaska did not predict the oil price; it secured a public claim before the money became ordinary.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a policy recommendation told through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that studies advanced AI and how governments might navigate it. It follows the group's April 2025 [*AI 2027* scenario](https://ai-2027.com/), and four of that work's five authors also wrote Plan A.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my 2026 speculative book about AI's economic transition, has a retail publication date of March 22. Its PDF was public on my site by March 23, and the current edition followed on June 24. A public link to Plan A appeared on July 9. That order establishes chronology, not influence or predictive accuracy.

I think the narrower comparison is more useful. The works reach a similar economic break and public dividend, then separate over how power should be controlled. Their disagreement exposes three questions that strategy conversations often blur together:

| Test | Failure it addresses | Evidence required | Decision owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Infrastructure verification | Undeclared large-scale computing capacity | Records plus physical inspection | States or treaty bodies |
| Economic ownership | AI-generated gains accrue to a narrow group | A legally enforceable claim on output or revenue | Legislatures and the citizens named in law |
| Supplier exit | A vital process cannot move to another supplier | A live handover against buyer-owned cases | Accountable business and technology owners |

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A is a conditional policy scenario, while The Three Crucibles is my speculative book. Their overlap confirms neither chronology. It reveals that inspecting AI infrastructure, allocating its gains, and retaining the ability to leave a supplier are different tests of control.</p></div>

## The economic break is the real convergence

The public page for my book is authoritative for its taxonomy: the First Crucible is The Correction, the Second Crucible is The Transformation, and the Third Crucible is The Alignment. The Correction describes inflated expectations meeting uneven returns, followed by company failures and stronger pressure to automate. Plan A has no close equivalent because it begins with geopolitical competition over advanced AI. The maps start in different places.

They meet in The Transformation. My book imagines output recovering while many jobs do not and states the demand problem plainly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” In Plan A's scenario, AI performs [one third of all cognitive labor by mid-2031](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=13), meaning work based mainly on information, analysis, communication, and judgment. It then projects about 50 percent real economic growth in 2032 while automation erodes the tax base.

The mechanism matters because production and purchasing power can move in opposite directions. When wages disappear, governments collect less personal-income and payroll tax. Heavy investment in data centers, factories, and robots can also reduce taxable company profit through expensing or depreciation. Output may surge while household demand and public revenue weaken.

Both works answer with a Citizen's Dividend, a public payment tied to AI-driven productivity, and both point to Alaska. [My book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37), based on the taxes and social payments a person doing equivalent work would have contributed. [Plan A instead proposes tradable permits](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17): government-issued permissions to build new robot and computing capacity that companies can buy and sell.

Part of the permit revenue goes to a corporation owned equally by US citizens. In the scenario, its dividend rises from $45,000 per American adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035, measured in 2025 dollars. The Alignment carries this income problem into politics: will citizens become stewards of abundance, or will a narrow group control the productive system?

I think the convergence is unusually specific because it includes the broken income loop, the Alaska precedent, and the same policy name. Yet “Citizen's Dividend” predates both works, and Plan A's figures remain scenario projections. A later scenario can stress-test my framework; it cannot turn speculation into observed evidence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The canonical sequence in my book is The Correction, The Transformation, and The Alignment. Plan A has no matching market correction, but both works reach the same income-and-tax problem and propose a Citizen's Dividend. They agree on the economic break while choosing different funding mechanisms.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership govern different failures

Plan A is much stronger on a technical danger that my book largely brackets. Its preferred scenario has the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029, pause in 2035 at AI matching top human experts, and move toward superintelligence in 2040. Without the deal, the authors model a rapid route to fully automated AI research and superintelligence by the end of 2030. *Takeoff* means AI automates enough AI research to speed up its own improvement; *superintelligence* means systems that outperform the best humans across fields while working faster and more cheaply. These are conditional dates, not promised events.

The proposed restraint depends on seeing the physical build-out. Plan A projects growth from roughly 20 million H100-equivalents in 2026 to 60 billion in 2034. An H100-equivalent measures AI computing performance against Nvidia's H100 processor, so it describes capacity rather than a literal chip count. Major data-center owners and chip suppliers would report large transactions, rival powers would inspect infrastructure, and AI research would become almost entirely transparent.

Plan A tests this design with [a fictional covert-project branch](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45). China diverts about 500,000 H100-equivalents into tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station, a remote Chinese hydroelectric project whose power and underground setting make concealment plausible. Roughly 200 people know. The authors put the chance of the hidden project reaching top-expert AI undetected before 2043 below ten percent, but the branch still identifies what verification must defeat: false records, official collusion, and delayed physical evidence.

The Alignment asks another question: who owns AI infrastructure and receives its output? Plan A gives citizens shares in the corporation receiving permit revenue, creating an economic claim without distributing chips, AI models, or AI companies. My book proposes broader ownership among individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, including models that can run on local hardware.

I don't know which balance could survive geopolitical pressure. I think the disagreement is more informative than a forced match. Plan A tackles secret capacity and loss of control, where my book offers no comparable plan for containing superintelligence. My book goes further on distributed ownership, which an inspection regime cannot create. Detecting a breach cannot decide who holds economic power afterward, while broad ownership cannot contain a system more capable than its owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses reporting, inspection, and transparent research to constrain a rush toward superintelligence. The Alignment asks who owns AI infrastructure and its output. Each addresses a gap left by the other because observing concentrated capacity and distributing economic power are different jobs.</p></div>

## A usable exit starts at signing

The first two tests sit mainly with governments and citizens. A third appears whenever an organization makes a critical process dependent on an AI supplier. My book illustrates the pressure with [two fictional insurers](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21). One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. The other uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that carry out parts of the claims process, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The numbers are illustrative, but the mechanism is clear: adoption can outrun a buyer's ability to reverse it.

My take is that exit proof should be mandatory at renewal for a business-critical AI workflow handling regulated decisions or an operationally essential process. Exit proof is a live handover test showing that a replacement team can run the process to an agreed standard. Low-risk tools may need only a tested data export and documented settings. High-impact workflows need a full handover with named business and technology owners. The requirement belongs in the contract at signing, while the buyer still has leverage.

The operating sequence is short: classify the workflow, contract the test, run it before renewal, and escalate only failed critical workflows. A first pilot can cover one workflow, one replacement team, and a 30-day window. I would start by requiring the supplier to reimburse test costs up to five percent of the annual contract value, with the buyer approving and funding any excess. That figure is a proposal to make the obligation concrete, not an industry standard.

The business owner schedules the test and judges its outcome. The technology owner sets the technical baseline and owns restoration if the exercise fails. Disputes go to a cross-functional AI portfolio forum, the group responsible for AI investment and risk decisions, with procurement, legal, technology, and the affected business represented. That group decides whether a failed critical workflow can be renewed.

The acceptance record can stay compact:

| Field | Required record |
| --- | --- |
| Inputs | Frozen, buyer-owned test cases |
| Baseline and grader | Current results plus the named person or system evaluating outcomes |
| Limits | Agreed error, completion-time, and cost thresholds |
| Evidence | Source trail, decision log, and retention period |
| Authority | Permitted tools, data, and actions |
| Recovery | Owner and deadline for restoring the original service |

The handover package should include versioned workflow settings, model instructions, system connections, delegated permissions, and data lineage, the record of where inputs came from and how they changed. Acceptance then has three gates. Evidence must reconstruct the source trail, authority must remain within agreed tools and permissions, and recovery must restore the original service safely if the transfer fails. A replacement that produces the right decision but loses its source trail is rejected. One failure shows that a workable exit remains unproven, not that migration will always be impossible; it should trigger funded repair and a retest.

This asks more than data portability, which moves files, source-code escrow, which releases code under agreed conditions, or ordinary workflow migration, which moves known components. Exit proof tests whether another team can operate the whole process to the accepted business standard without hidden dependence on the current supplier. It sits beside, rather than replaces, documentation required by the [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law. Most of that law began applying on August 2, 2026; its rules for general-purpose AI models, models able to perform many tasks and support other applications, had already applied since August 2, 2025. Disclosure still does not prove a working transfer.

I think exit proof must remain falsifiable. If a reproducible handover succeeds but exclusive data, licensing terms, or scarce computing capacity still let the original supplier dictate the price, the buyer remains economically dependent. That result would disprove the stronger claim that technical exit preserves bargaining power and lead the argument back to ownership.

Alaska secured a public claim before oil income became routine. The question now is which controls we can still secure before AI infrastructure becomes equally ordinary. The control postponed until after the system hardens is the one we are least likely to recover.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof is a live handover test for critical AI workflows, agreed at signing and run before renewal against buyer-owned cases, measurable limits, traceable evidence, bounded authority, and safe restoration. Even a successful handover may leave the buyer dependent on exclusive inputs or scarce capacity, which is why inspection, ownership, and exit must remain separate tests. Once AI infrastructure hardens, missing control will be difficult to recover.</p></div>
