Two forecasts can agree and still be wrong. They may have found the same mechanism or inherited the same assumptions. What matters is what each work had to believe before reaching its answer.

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), made public on July 9, 2026, is the AI Futures Project's policy recommendation told through a conditional scenario. The group also produced [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), an April 2025 scenario in which AI accelerates research on better AI; four of its five front-page authors return here. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction” and says it is not the authors' best guess.

[The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) reached the public earlier. A homepage PDF link appeared on March 23, 2026, the dedicated book page followed on June 1, and the current PDF is dated June 24. Those dates show order, not influence, predictive success, or empirical confirmation.

I think the resemblance matters for a narrower reason. AI 2040 begins with catastrophic risk and international control, while The Three Crucibles begins with competition, purchasing power, and ownership. They still meet at the same economic break. The useful question is what must be controlled, and who must hold a claim on what the machines produce.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI 2040 is a conditional policy scenario, not confirming data. Its value here is that a safety-focused forecast reaches part of the same economic structure as The Three Crucibles from a different starting point.</p>

## The agreement is narrower than it looks

Plan A starts with a race between the United States and China. In its scenario, a 2029 deal prevents fully automated AI research in 2030; systems reach roughly the level of the best human researchers by 2035 and pause, then scale to superintelligence in 2040 ([AI 2040, pp. 4–5](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). Fully automated AI research means AI systems doing the work needed to design better AI. Superintelligence means systems significantly better than the best humans at everything, while also faster and cheaper.

The public page for The Three Crucibles names The Correction in 2026, The Transformation from 2027 to 2032, and The Alignment from 2035 onward. Its PDF opening map calls the middle crucible “The Void,” then describes transformation within that period. The argument moves from labor losing its old economic role, through a rebuilt income system, to a choice between distributed ownership and concentrated control.

The clocks do not line up. The book places economic disruption early, while Plan A delays the most capable systems and models the shock inside that controlled period. I think the mismatch is useful because it separates the economic thesis from any one theory of rapid AI progress.

There is also a common-cause explanation. A citizen dividend and Alaska's oil fund were already part of public policy debate, so matching language does not prove independent discovery. The agreement matters where the causal routes differ: Plan A asks how governments can slow a dangerous technical race, while The Three Crucibles asks how an economy keeps demand and public revenue when paid human work shrinks. Their meeting point supports the diagnosis of a shared structural problem, not either timeline.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The works converge on a sequence from labor disruption to redistribution and then to a concentration fork, but they disagree on timing and begin from different risks. That makes the shared economic mechanism worth examining without turning agreement into proof.</p>
</div>

## The dividend hides two different machines

The strongest convergence is fiscal. In Plan A's scenario, AI systems and robots perform more work previously done by paid humans, which erodes personal-income and payroll-tax receipts. Corporate taxes also struggle to capture the modeled growth because companies can expense or depreciate machines ([AI 2040, p. 17](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). In The Three Crucibles, the same break appears from the demand side: output can rise while households lose the wages needed to buy it.

Both works use the label “Citizen's Dividend” and cite the Alaska Permanent Fund, created through a 1976 constitutional amendment to preserve oil wealth and first paying a dividend in 1982. Applied to AI, the principle gives people a direct payment backed by a claim on AI-related revenue or productive assets.

The funding machinery is different. Plan A caps robot and compute capacity, the hardware processing power used to train and run AI systems, then sells tradable permits to build more. In the scenario, every US citizen receives one share in a Compute Dividend Corporation. Its portion of compute-permit fees rises from 25 percent in 2032 to 75 percent in 2035, while the modeled adult payment rises from $45,000 to about $1 million ([AI 2040, pp. 18–19](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). The authors explicitly call their economics model uncertain.

The Three Crucibles instead proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax, a charge based on the public revenue a human doing equivalent work would have generated. It pairs that tax with universal basic income, a recurring payment without a work requirement, and distributed AI ownership. The latter means AI infrastructure and models should be held beyond a small group of private firms, including by individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities.

The two mechanisms fail differently. A capacity permit works before production but can miss hidden hardware or activity moved across borders. A labor-equivalence tax works after deployment but can be evaded by relabeling tasks or obscuring the human comparison. Plan A includes ownership through its citizen shares; The Three Crucibles does not offer a system for verifying research on the most capable AI systems. My take is that each proposal is strongest where the other is least complete.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The shared dividend conceals a real policy choice. Plan A raises and distributes revenue by controlling scarce capacity before production; The Three Crucibles taxes displaced human work and spreads ownership after deployment. Each base has a different route for evasion.</p>
</div>

## The two-key test

Call this the Two-Key Test. The capacity key asks whether material AI computing, research, and deployment can be declared, inspected, authorized, and stopped. The claim key asks whether citizens, workers, or public institutions have an enforceable share of machine-created value. Passing requires evidence that neither key can be bypassed while the other appears healthy.

Plan A's capacity key includes research transparency, chip declarations, datacenter inspection, and safety cases, which are detailed arguments that new AIs will not cause an irreversible catastrophe. Its fictional covert-project branch tests the bypass with a hidden datacenter beside the Medog Hydropower Station project in China and 500,000 diverted H100-equivalents ([AI 2040, Appendix D, pp. 45–50](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). An H100-equivalent is a standard measure of AI computing capacity, not a count of identical chips. The branch asks whether undeclared capacity can defeat the controls.

The claim key needs the same discipline. A dividend is weak unless its revenue base, legal owner, payment rule, and bypass route are visible. If automation grows while the public claim shrinks, the mechanism has failed even when every AI system is documented. I do not know which combination of permits, labor taxation, and citizen shares will survive evasion. That uncertainty makes the test necessary.

| Key | Evidence threshold | Accountable owner | Observable failure | Reversal right |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Capacity | Every material model, supplier, and compute expansion has an authorization record before restricted training or regulated deployment | Public regulator or chief technology officer (CTO) | Undeclared capacity or an unapproved model enters the controlled process | Stop the run or deployment and suspend the supplier |
| Claim | Every automation case identifies who receives the savings, who loses taxable income, and what enforceable share returns to workers or the public | Treasury or chief financial officer (CFO) | AI-created value rises while the defined public or worker share falls | Pause the business case and rewrite the tax, contract, or ownership terms |

Sweden offers a practical boundary test. The [EU AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, sets risk-based rules for AI developers and deployers, including documentation, transparency, and oversight. It does not decide who should own automation gains, so a Swedish board can meet a control requirement and still fail the claim key.

I think the board record should stay small: name the CTO who owns capacity evidence, the CFO who owns distribution evidence, a quarterly review, and a stop right. An undeclared model entering an employment decision fails the first key. An automation case that counts savings but cannot locate lost wages, taxes, or transition costs fails the second.

The frame makes a bounded prediction. Where AI materially replaces taxable human work and remains observable, two-key organizations and jurisdictions should outperform one-key peers through 2032 on unauthorized-use incidents and the measured public or worker share of automation gains. Matching outcomes weaken the idea; an inability to detect hidden capacity or captured ownership defeats it in practice.

I think that is the contribution of reading these works together. The public record places The Three Crucibles' economic argument before AI 2040, while Plan A supplies a much more detailed control system. No advanced-AI strategy deserves to scale until both keys can turn and either one can stop it.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Two-Key Test requires verifiable control of dangerous AI capacity and an enforceable broad claim on machine-created value. Each key needs an owner, evidence threshold, failure signal, review horizon, and stop right. If either stop right is missing, the strategy fails.</p>
</div>
