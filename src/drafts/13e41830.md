In 1976, Alaska voters decided that an oil boom should leave something behind. A constitutional amendment directed at least a quarter of certain mineral revenues into the Alaska Permanent Fund. The fund invests that principal, and eligible residents first received dividend payments in 1982.

That choice settled an ownership question before the new wealth became normal. Fifty years later, the [AI Futures Project's *AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), a July 2026 scenario and policy proposal, uses the same precedent for wealth created by artificial intelligence. So does my 2026 speculative book, [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), which was already public in March. Both arrive at a Citizen's Dividend: a public claim on output that no longer depends mainly on human labor.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska turned temporary resource income into a lasting public asset. Both works ask whether AI-created wealth needs a similar claim for citizens.</p></div>

## What Plan A actually proposes

The AI Futures Project is a nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and recommends policy responses. Four of the five authors of its 2025 scenario about a rapid path to superhuman AI, *AI 2027*, also helped write Plan A. The new document calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” which matters given the precision of its dates.

In its preferred path, the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029 to prevent a 2030 race in which AI research and development would become fully automated. Development continues with systems in the human capability range until a 2035 pause around AI that equals or surpasses top human experts in every field. Scaling resumes in 2040 toward superintelligence, meaning AI far more capable than humans across the work that shapes society.

The economic shift is just as important. In the scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, while real GDP, or economic output adjusted for inflation, grows by about 50 percent in 2032. As paid work shrinks relative to machine output, wage income and labor-tax revenue weaken. Plan A proposes tradable permits for robots and new compute, the computing capacity used to train and run AI systems, with part of the permit income returned to citizens.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A combines a staged technical slowdown with economic reform: limit and verify AI capacity, then return part of its value to citizens.</p></div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

My book describes three pressures rather than one arrival date. **The Correction** is the point where AI productivity shifts bargaining power from labor toward the owners of productive systems. Its line “Productivity = Capital, and labor solves to zero” captures that direction in compressed form.

**The Transformation** is the unstable period when income and demand must adapt. The book proposes a Citizen's Dividend funded by AI infrastructure because high output is of little use if households cannot buy what the economy produces. Robots do not buy iPhones.

**The Alignment** is the political question that follows: whether society becomes a steward of abundance or lets control settle around a small group of owners. It includes technical safety, but also the alignment of ownership and public benefit with the productive system.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction changes the value of labor, The Transformation rebuilds distribution, and The Alignment decides who governs and benefits.</p></div>

## The overlap is a sequence

The shared claim reaches beyond the dividend label and the Alaska precedent. Both works trace the same mechanism: AI takes on more valuable work, systems built around wages come under pressure, and society needs another way to connect production with purchasing power. Plan A's dates can be wrong while that sequence remains useful.

Their funding methods differ. Plan A charges for scarce capacity before it is built through permits for compute and robots. The Three Crucibles puts more weight on AI labor taxation and ownership spread beyond a few firms once the systems produce value. One governs entry to the system; the other distributes its returns.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works agree on the economic chain but choose different levers: permits ration capacity, while taxation and broader ownership distribute returns.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership answer different risks

Plan A's main safeguard is verification through tracked chips, inspected datacenters, research transparency, coordinated pauses, and international oversight. Its appendix tests that system against a covert project using smuggled AI chips. The scenario assigns below a 10 percent chance that such a project reaches top-expert-dominating AI, systems beyond the best human specialists, without detection before 2043.

My book concentrates on another failure. A system can remain under technical control while its benefits become dangerously concentrated. Yet ownership cannot replace verification, because a widely shared claim on an uncontrollable system offers little protection. I do not know which layer will prove harder. I am fairly sure we need both.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A tries to make powerful AI visible and enforceable. The Three Crucibles asks who retains power and income when enforcement works.</p></div>

## Preparation without prophecy

Structural convergence does not make either document correct. AI progress may slow, physical limits may matter more than expected, and political coordination may fail in ways no scenario captures. No one knows what waits beyond the next few years.

Still, if Plan A and my book are even directionally right, the road ahead is likely to be bumpy. That is the purpose of *The Three Crucibles*: a preparation document for decisions about verification, distribution, and ownership before events force those decisions on us. A forecast can miss its dates and still make the right questions difficult to ignore.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>We cannot know the route or timing. We can prepare for the pressures both works identify before they become emergencies.</p></div>
