In 1976, Alaska voters changed the state constitution so a share of mineral revenue would enter the Alaska Permanent Fund. This publicly owned investment portfolio turns finite oil wealth into long-term public wealth. Since 1982, its earnings have helped finance an annual cash dividend for eligible residents.

That choice matters again because [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) uses Alaska as the model for a much larger citizen dividend funded by artificial intelligence. My 2026 speculative book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), reaches for the same precedent and mechanism. Its framework was public by March 2026, months before Plan A appeared in July.

Neither work is proof. AI 2040 is a scenario, and my book is a preparation document. Their independent agreement still raises a serious question: when machines produce more of the value, what claim do citizens retain on the result?

## AI 2040 is a proposal for controlled acceleration

AI 2040 is a July 2026 scenario and policy proposal from the AI Futures Project, the nonprofit research group behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its April 2025 scenario about rapid progress toward superhuman AI. The authors call Plan A “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” so its dates describe a proposed path rather than an inevitable future.

That path begins with a United States–China agreement in 2029. It moves through near-automated AI research, a pause in 2035 around the level of the best human experts, and a planned move toward superintelligence in 2040. Superintelligence means AI that performs far beyond the best humans across almost all intellectual work.

Control depends on compute, the processing capacity provided by advanced chips and data centers. Plan A would track chips, inspect facilities, require research transparency, and use permits to limit expansion. This is verification: making promises between states and companies checkable. In the scenario, AI systems perform a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, the tax base comes under severe pressure, and permit income funds a public corporation with one share for every citizen.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A imagines rapid AI growth made governable through a US–China agreement, visible compute, inspections, and public distribution of some resulting wealth.</p>
</div>

## The three crucibles, in plain language

My book describes three social passages that may overlap and move at different speeds:

- **The Correction:** AI productivity shifts value away from labor and toward the owners of models, chips, and machines. Wages and labor-based tax revenue then lose some of their economic role.
- **The Void:** The old promise that disruption will create enough replacement jobs stops working, while a new way to distribute income has not yet settled.
- **The Alignment:** Society decides who owns productive AI and how its abundance is shared. This is broader than technical AI alignment, which asks whether a system follows its developer’s intended goals and values.

These are pressure points rather than three tidy calendar phases. Their purpose is to make the transition visible before its choices become emergency choices.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Correction weakens labor’s role, The Void exposes the missing social settlement, and The Alignment decides who owns and shares AI-created abundance.</p>
</div>

## The agreement is about distribution

The deepest overlap appears when productivity stops guaranteeing broad purchasing power. Companies can produce more with fewer workers, which weakens wages and the taxes built on them. If ownership remains narrow, rising output does not give households the income needed to buy it. As my book puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.”

Plan A answers with tradable permits for compute and robot labor. A tradable permit is a state-created right to use a limited resource; selling those rights captures part of the gain for public use. The scenario directs much of that income to a Compute Dividend Corporation modeled on Alaska’s fund. My book is less detailed about the machinery, but argues for the same result: citizens need a direct claim on AI infrastructure’s productivity.

The documents diverge over what protects that claim. Plan A emphasizes verification through chip tracking, declared data centers, inspections, and research transparency. My book emphasizes ownership after work stops being the main route to income. Verification can restrain a dangerous race, but it does not distribute the outcome. Broad ownership can distribute value, but it cannot make advanced AI safe. These are different layers of the same problem.

## Preparation matters because certainty is unavailable

This convergence cannot tell us whether the key break comes in 2031, 2040, or much later. Plan A depends on geopolitical cooperation and enforcement that may fail. The crucibles simplify a messy transition whose stages will probably blur.

That uncertainty is the reason to prepare. My book exists as a preparation document: a way to consider income, ownership, and political legitimacy before the pressure peaks. No one knows what awaits us. But if AI 2040 and my book are directionally right, the road ahead will be bumpy, and our room to make calm choices may be shorter than we expect.
