One of Alaska's most consequential oil choices came before the windfall. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was being built to carry crude from Prudhoe Bay, the large oil field on Alaska's North Slope, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/). The fund would save part of the state's mineral income, and its first resident dividend followed in 1982. Alaskans secured a public claim while the terms were still open.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a 2026 policy recommendation expressed through a scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that studies advanced AI and how governments might navigate it. Four of the five authors of [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's 2025 scenario about a rapid path to superhuman AI, also worked on Plan A. Its authors are explicit: this is primarily a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast.

My book [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) was [published on March 22, 2026](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GTK53C9H). A [public link to Plan A appeared on July 9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). That chronology does not establish influence, and Plan A is a scenario rather than external data that can confirm my dates. Its canonical sequence is the Correction, the Transformation, and the Alignment. I think the stronger claim is more useful anyway: the two maps reach a remarkably similar economic fork, then expose different requirements for control.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A does not validate The Three Crucibles as a forecast. It strengthens a specific economic frame, while the differences between the two works show where that frame needs additional controls.</p></div>

## The economic sequence converges

The Correction is the book's financial reckoning: overheated AI valuations fall, weaker firms disappear, and companies face sharper pressure to automate. Plan A starts from geopolitics rather than a market correction, but its scenario reaches a related labor shock. By mid-2031, it has AI performing a third of all cognitive labor, meaning work done mainly through information, analysis, communication, and judgment.

The deeper problem is not unemployment alone. [Plan A's 2032 economy](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17) grows by roughly 50 percent in real terms while its labor-based tax system starts breaking. As paid human work falls, personal-income and payroll taxes fall with it. Companies are also investing heavily in robots, factories, and data centers, and those costs can reduce taxable profit through deductions and depreciation. Production can soar while public revenue and household demand weaken. As *The Three Crucibles* puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.”

The Transformation, the second crucible, asks what can replace wages as both household income and fuel for demand. The book proposes a [Citizen's Dividend](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36), a direct public claim on the productivity of AI infrastructure. Plan A uses the same name and the same Alaska precedent. In its scenario, the United States sells tradable permits to build robots or computing capacity, the chips and facilities used to train and run AI. A fixed share of the fees belongs to a Compute Dividend Corporation; every US citizen receives one share, and profits are distributed equally. The projected adult payment rises from $45,000 in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035, measured in 2025 dollars.

The funding methods differ. *The Three Crucibles* proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax tied to the tax and social contributions a person in an equivalent role would have generated. Plan A charges for permission to add physical capacity. I think the convergence matters because both works connect automation to shrinking labor income and a need to distribute machine-generated wealth. Their emphasis differs: Plan A quantifies the tax-base failure, while the book names the demand failure. You do not need to accept Plan A's speed or dollar figures to take that shared mechanism seriously.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Correction and Transformation meet Plan A in the same economic chain: automation can raise output while weakening labor income and the tax base. The book adds the demand problem; both works converge on a public claim to machine-generated wealth.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership solve different problems

The Alignment, the third crucible, is the political test. The public book page describes a choice between owners who become “stewards of abundance” and owners who become “the new feudal lords.” Plan A sees the same danger of concentrated AI power, but approaches it through takeoff governance. Takeoff is the period when AI can automate enough AI research to accelerate its own improvement, potentially turning a technology race into a much faster and harder-to-control process.

In [Plan A's timeline](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=4), a 2029 agreement between the United States and China prevents fully automated AI research from leading to superintelligence by the end of 2030. Superintelligence means systems that outperform the best humans across fields while also working faster and more cheaply. Development continues within the human range until a 2035 pause at top-human-expert AI, roughly the capability of the strongest specialists, before scaling to superintelligence in 2040. These are stages in the authors' proposed path, not observed milestones.

The plan depends on verification: evidence that countries are obeying the agreed limits. Major chip and data-center transactions are declared, rival powers inspect infrastructure, and AI research becomes almost entirely transparent. Yet the report also imagines a covert Chinese project with roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents installed in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station. An H100-equivalent is a normalized measure of computing performance, not a literal chip count. Ongoing dam construction provides cover, while small modular nuclear reactors and gas turbines supply power before the dam is complete. The authors estimate that only about 200 people would know.

That appendix makes *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film about intelligent machines running a simulated reality, a useful metaphor. Its green code looks weightless, but the machine world behind it still needs industrial hardware and energy. Advanced AI has the same physical shadow: chips, electricity, cooling systems, and buildings create evidence that inspectors can seek, even when the software remains hidden.

Inspection still does not settle ownership. Plan A gives each citizen a legal share in the dividend corporation, not in the chips, models, or companies producing the value. *The Three Crucibles* argues for [distributed AI ownership](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=56), including infrastructure held by individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, with open-source models that can run locally or on an owner's premises. Licenses and degrees of openness vary, so local operation does not automatically mean unrestricted use. I think the distinction is fundamental: verification asks whether actors follow limits; ownership asks who has a durable claim on capacity and output. I don't know which balance can survive geopolitical pressure, and Plan A's authors do not claim certainty. A system can be visible to inspectors while a small group still sets the terms of access.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses verification to constrain hidden computing capacity, while Alignment asks who controls visible infrastructure and receives its gains. The two controls reinforce each other, but neither can substitute for the other.</p></div>

## Practical control requires an exit test

The comparison leaves one more boundary: whether an institution can change suppliers without losing the process that now carries out its work. *The Three Crucibles* imagines one insurer retaining 5,000 claims adjusters while a rival deploys coordinated AI agents, software systems that divide and execute the claims process. The rival cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. It is a thought experiment rather than a reported case, but it shows why adoption pressure can move faster than a buyer's ability to leave.

My take is that every critical AI contract should require exit proof before renewal: a supplier-funded handover test showing that a replacement team can run one working process. A useful first scope is one claims workflow, one portable data extract, one replacement team, and a 30-day test, with the chief information officer accountable for the renewal decision. The buyer should own the past cases used to evaluate the replacement.

The handover package can be grouped by function. Workflow configuration covers saved state, model instructions, and tool connections. Access and source records cover permissions, source material, and data lineage, meaning a record of where each input came from and how it changed. Restoration instructions explain how the replacement team can rebuild the process. The team may change the model, instructions, retrieval method, or workflow design as long as it reproduces the agreed business result on the buyer's evaluation cases within fixed limits for error, time, and cost.

Failure also needs a contractual meaning. Missing records, blocked access, results outside the agreed limits, or decisions that cannot be traced to their sources should fail the test. That failure should stop automatic renewal and trigger funded transition assistance; a second failed test should let the buyer leave without a termination fee. The leverage exists before the contract is signed.

Regulation covers a related but different boundary. Since August 2, 2025, the [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law, has applied obligations to general-purpose AI models, models with significant generality that can competently perform a wide range of tasks and be integrated into downstream systems. Most of the law began applying on August 2, 2026, including transparency duties for specified AI interactions and AI-generated or manipulated content. Those rules can improve visibility, but they do not prove that a buyer can transfer a working process to another supplier.

I think this is the second-order failure both maps leave exposed. Governments may verify computing capacity and citizens may receive part of its return, yet a hospital, insurer, or public agency can remain trapped inside the systems carrying out its work. Alaska secured a public claim before oil money became routine. Our claim on AI should include the practical power to leave before dependence becomes the default.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof turns supplier choice into a funded acceptance test with an owner, a deadline, and consequences for failure. The machines will be built either way; the right to leave remains ours to secure.</p></div>
