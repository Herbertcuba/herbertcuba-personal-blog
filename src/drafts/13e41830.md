Two very different maps point to the same economic break. [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), from a team that includes four authors of [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), has AI doing a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031. [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) uses a fictional insurer that cuts its 5,000-person claims team by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months ([printed p. 20](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21)). The company gains while wages supporting demand shrink.

I think the overlap is useful because it gets unusually specific. Both maps reach a Citizen's Dividend and cite Alaska's oil dividend. Their agreement is thematic convergence, while their disagreement exposes three separate controls: verification for hidden AI capacity, ownership for the gains, and an exit proof for an institution's power to change suppliers.

The book PDF entered its [public repository on March 23, 2026](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8). A [Hacker News post linking to AI 2040](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) appeared on July 9. This public sequence leaves private origins unknown, so the comparison tests convergence rather than priority.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Two separately authored maps connect automated work to weaker labor income and a Citizen's Dividend. Their deeper value lies in the different forms of power they expose.</p></div>

## The Void is where the maps meet

The manuscript names its three crucibles as the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment ([printed p. 5](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6)). The Correction marks the break, the Void covers output recovering without equivalent job recovery, and the Alignment asks who owns the machines. Inside the Void, “The Transformation” chapter says “Robots do not buy iPhones” and proposes a Citizen's Dividend to support buying power ([printed p. 35](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36)).

Plan A reaches the same pressure through the timetable it recommends. In its scenario, a 2029 US-China agreement stops an AI research race from reaching superintelligence by the end of 2030. Systems stay within the human range, pause in 2035 at the level of the best human specialists, then resume toward superintelligence in 2040 ([p. 4](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=4)).

As machines take more work, payroll and personal-income tax revenue shrinks. Plan A's scenario sells transferable permits, government rights to add robot or computing capacity that buyers can resell. Most fees fund a Citizen's Dividend, reaching $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035 within the scenario ([pp. 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17)). The book instead proposes a charge on each human-equivalent unit of deployed AI labor ([printed p. 36](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37)).

I think the dividend is the direct match. The timetable and funding method diverge. Rising output alongside falling labor income, payroll receipts, and household demand would be early warning evidence consistent with both maps, rather than proof of either forecast.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The book's canonical crucibles are Correction, Void, and Alignment; Transformation is a policy chapter inside the Void. Plan A shares its dividend answer but funds it differently.</p></div>

## Verification cannot settle ownership

Plan A puts verification at the center, checking whether declared chips, datacenters, and frontier research match reality. It combines major-transaction declarations, reciprocal inspections, and broad research transparency. Its stress test imagines 500,000 H100-equivalents, a measure of high-end AI computing power, hidden near the Medog Hydropower Station while roughly 200 people know ([pp. 45–50](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45)).

This is the *Rogue One* problem in policy form. A state can divide knowledge, yet a large AI project still leaves physical traces through electricity and cooling. Plan A puts its chance of beating top human experts undetected before 2043 below 10 percent.

The Alignment covers another risk. The book calls for distributed AI ownership through open infrastructure, open models, and legal limits on a private intelligence monopoly ([printed p. 56](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57)). Inspection can coexist with concentrated gains, while broad ownership can coexist with a covert state project.

I don't know which map will prove closer to events, and Plan A separates its recommendation from its best guess. My take is that both controls are needed. I think substituting one for the other leaves a dangerous gap.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification constrains hidden capacity, while ownership determines who receives the gains. Each control leaves the other's failure mode open.</p></div>

## Exit proof belongs in the renewal decision

Verification and broad distribution can still leave an institution trapped inside one supplier's system. I would add an exit proof, a small, timed handover of one working AI service: one claims workflow, one data extract, one replacement team, and 30 days. The CIO owns the renewal decision, while the contract funds the test and transition support.

The buyer owns the evaluation set: a sample of past decisions, the agreed answer for each, and the scoring rule. The supplier provides a record of where the data came from and how it changed, decision logs, versioned model and prompt settings, connections to other tools, permissions, and a restore guide. The replacement may change the model, prompts, retrieval method, or workflow while preserving the agreed business result. It passes by matching the agreed answers within the error threshold, deadline, and cost ceiling. Missing access, a missed deadline, or an error above the threshold fails the test.

Renewal should depend on that result. Failure should trigger transition help, continued access during the move, and supplier-funded repair. I think this gives a board evidence of bargaining power before a critical service is at risk.

Since August 2, 2026, [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50) has required certain disclosures for AI interactions and generated content. Those disclosures can reveal AI use, while migration requires working records and access. A failed handover predicts supplier lock-in even with compliant disclosures: the buyer will accept terms it would otherwise reject because switching remains untested.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An exit proof gives the CIO a funded, pass-or-fail condition for renewal. When one working workflow cannot move on agreed terms, the supplier owns the choice.</p></div>
