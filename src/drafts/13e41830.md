The useful comparison between AI 2040 and *The Three Crucibles* begins where validation claims end. A safety-policy scenario reaches the same economic break as my economic framework, then reveals why one safeguard is not enough.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 2026 policy recommendation expressed through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and proposes policy. Four of the five authors of [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's April 2025 scenario about AI automating research on better AI, are among Plan A's six authors. [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my book about the economic and political stages of this transition.

A [repository commit](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) dated March 23, 2026 added the book PDF and its download link. The current book PDF has June 24, 2026 metadata; Plan A's has July 11, 2026 metadata. These records establish which file came first, not influence or forecast accuracy. Plan A also calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction” and says the scenario is not the authors' best guess.

I think the useful signal is structural. Both works connect declining labor income to a new public claim on machine output and a fight over concentrated power. Their disagreement shows that technical control and economic rights can fail separately.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The chronology supports comparison rather than validation. Two different approaches reach the same economic break, then point toward separate safeguards for technical control and economic rights.</p></div>

## The overlap survives different clocks

In Plan A's scenario, a 2029 United States-China agreement prevents fully automated AI research in 2030, when AI would otherwise do most of the work of designing better AI. Development remains within the human range until a 2035 pause at top-human-expert level, then resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. The document defines superintelligence as systems significantly better than the best humans at everything while also faster and cheaper ([AI 2040, pp. 4–5](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

*The Three Crucibles* uses another clock. The Correction, dated 2026, shifts productivity from labor toward capital until labor loses economic weight. The public page calls the 2027–2032 middle stage The Transformation; the PDF's opening map calls it “The Void” and later develops The Transformation within it. Wage-backed demand breaks and a new distribution system becomes necessary. The Alignment, dated 2035 onward, asks whether AI's owners become stewards of abundance or new feudal lords.

I think the mismatch helps. Neither work has earned the right to treat its dates as a timetable, but both move from weaker labor income to rebuilt distribution and then to a choice over control. Earlier citizen payments and public ownership funds mean the shared language may reflect common prior ideas. The overlap is worth studying, but it is not proof.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The timelines differ, but the causal sequence holds: labor income weakens, distribution must be rebuilt, and control becomes political. That is a shared mechanism, not confirmation of either forecast.</p></div>

## The same dividend has different collection gates

In Plan A's United States scenario, inflation-adjusted output grows by about 50 percent in 2032 while personal-income and payroll-tax revenue weakens as AI systems and robots replace paid work. Corporate tax does not automatically close the gap because firms can reinvest in datacenters, factories, and robots, then expense or depreciate those investments before tax ([AI 2040, pp. 16–17](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). Output rises while the public claim on it falls.

Both works call their response a “Citizen's Dividend,” a direct payment backed by revenue or ownership from productive assets. Both cite the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://pfd.alaska.gov/Division-Info/Historical-Timeline), established through a 1976 constitutional amendment and first paying residents in 1982. Alaska attached a public claim to a scarce asset before the later income arrived.

Plan A caps robots and AI computing capacity, the processing power supplied by chips and datacenters to train and run AI, then auctions tradable permits to build more. In the scenario, permit fees reach $50 trillion in real 2025 dollars in fiscal year 2032 and the modeled payment to American adults reaches $45,000 ([AI 2040, pp. 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). These are scenario outputs, not economic evidence.

*The Three Crucibles* instead proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax: a company would pay for each human-equivalent unit of AI labor it deploys, in proportion to the public revenue a person doing that work would have generated. Its demand-side point is blunt: “robots do not buy iPhones.” Capacity can grow while customers lose the wages needed to buy its output.

I think the difference matters more than the shared label. A permit charges at the capacity gate, before useful work occurs, but can miss hidden or offshore facilities. A labor-equivalence tax follows deployment, but invites disputes over what counts as equivalent human work and whether a task was replaced. The diagnosis converges while the collection system remains unsettled.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A charges for scarce capacity before deployment; The Three Crucibles taxes human-equivalent AI labor after deployment. Each route can fund a dividend, and each creates a different measurement and avoidance problem.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership can fail alone

Plan A requires major datacenter owners and upstream suppliers, including chip manufacturers, to declare major purchases and sales. It adds inspections, monitoring for new model-training runs, broad research sharing, and safety cases. A safety case is an evidence-backed argument that a new system will not cause irreversible catastrophe, tested by outside critics ([AI 2040, pp. 7–8 and 13](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

The document stress-tests that premise in a fictional branch. China hides 500,000 H100-equivalents in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station project. An H100-equivalent expresses processing power in units of the H100 AI accelerator made by chip designer Nvidia; it is not a count of identical chips. Legitimate construction provides cover, and underground heat is harder to detect by infrared satellite ([AI 2040, Appendix D, pp. 45–47](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). Plan A later imagines confidence that no more than about one percent of capacity remains hidden. That tolerance belongs to the scenario, not the real world.

*The Three Crucibles* gives more weight to distributed ownership: infrastructure and models held beyond a small group of firms, with legal limits on private monopoly. Plan A's Compute Dividend Corporation also receives a defined share of permit fees, with one share for every United States citizen. Yet hidden capacity can escape both the permit record and the dividend.

Plan A is the strongest nearby combined model because it already pairs compute verification with citizen ownership. Its safety cases and Alaska precedent also show that the components are not my invention. I think the Two-Key Test adds one narrower discriminator: classify the failures separately before outcomes are known. If one owner, record, threshold, and remedy consistently controls both, or if the two outcomes always move together, the distinction is redundant.

I don't know which mix of permits, taxes, citizen shares, and distributed systems would survive real pressure. I do know that success on one measure cannot be allowed to hide failure on the other.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A already combines verification and citizen ownership. The sharper test is whether the two safeguards can fail independently; if they always move together under one enforceable control, the two-key distinction adds nothing.</p></div>

## Run the test before signing

Call it the Two-Key Test, but run it as a predeclared design audit. The capacity key covers computing above a threshold fixed in law or a license. The claim key covers a share of machine-created value required by law. I think each needs an owner, evidence, a pass condition, and a transition the system must reject.

| Test | Owner | Required record | Pass or reject |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Public capacity | Regulator created by law | Assets, supplier declarations, inspections, and maximum tolerated hidden capacity | Pass when covered capacity is recorded and stoppable; reject an off-record run or a breach that cannot be halted |
| Public claim | Lawmakers plus a treasury or fund created by law | Revenue, beneficiaries, payment date, audit, and appeal route | Pass when the required share arrives on time; reject diversion, underpayment, or unaudited money |
| Company deployment | Board and named operating functions | Baseline, system version, benefit floor, error ceiling, cost limit, affected roles, and consultation | Pass only within every threshold and with pause rights; reject a changed version, failed threshold, or unapproved role change |

Sweden's [Co-Determination Act of 1976](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-1976580-om-medbestammande-i-arbetslivet_sfs-1976-580/) provides a useful boundary. Section 11 generally requires an employer bound by a collective agreement to negotiate with the relevant worker organization before an important change to the business or working conditions, although exceptional reasons can allow a decision first. That puts worker voice in the company process, but creates neither a national capacity register nor a citizen claim on AI income.

The book's two-insurer thought experiment makes the company row concrete. One insurer keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; another deploys AI agents, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and overwhelms its rival within six months. This is not case evidence. In an illustrative pre-contract audit, the board could use the previous twelve months as its baseline, require the promised premium reduction, allow no rise in successful customer appeals, cap spending, lock the system version, and require negotiation before a planned role reduction.

Business and finance would own performance and cost evidence, technology the version record and tests, HR and legal the workforce process, and procurement the pause right. A model swap or premature role reduction would stop deployment. Restart would require a tested version, thresholds within bounds, completed negotiation, and new authorization. These company controls cannot create a Citizen's Dividend; that remains a decision for lawmakers.

My take is that the leverage exists before you sign. Capacity control and economic rights need separate records because the strongest actor will otherwise grade its own promise. Machines get built either way. Who can stop them, and who owns what they produce, are still open.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Two-Key Test assigns public capacity, public distribution, and company deployment to actors with different powers. Predeclared evidence and stop conditions make failure visible before one successful control is used to excuse another.</p></div>
