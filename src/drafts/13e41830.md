In 1976, Alaska voters decided that some mineral wealth should outlive the boom. They amended the state constitution so at least a quarter of certain mineral revenue would enter the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a publicly owned fund that invests the money. The related Permanent Fund Dividend began paying eligible residents in 1982. Its first cheque came from surplus oil revenue, while fund earnings supported later payments.

The same arrangement sits inside two attempts to think through an AI economy: [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) and my 2026 speculative book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf). My book's framework was in writing more than six months before this article; Plan A appeared in July 2026. Timing proves neither correct, but the overlap deserves attention.

The downloadable edition names three crucibles. **The Correction** begins when machine intelligence pushes down the value of paid cognitive work. **The Void** is the unstable passage in which wages, tax revenue, and consumer demand weaken before new ways of sharing income catch up. **The Alignment** is the choice over who owns AI infrastructure and whether its returns spread through society or concentrate among a few owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows how a public claim can be built around a shared resource before its proceeds are distributed. Both AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles bring that question into the AI economy.</p></div>

## What AI 2040 asks us to imagine

The [AI Futures Project](https://www.aifutures.org/about), a US nonprofit studying advanced AI and policy, published Plan A in July 2026. Four of its six authors also worked on *AI 2027*, the group's 2025 scenario about a rapid race toward advanced AI. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.”

Its path starts with a US–China agreement in 2029, followed by nearly automated AI research in 2030 and controlled scaling. In 2035, development pauses around systems that can match leading human experts across most important cognitive work. In 2040, the plan allows a move toward superintelligence, meaning AI far beyond human ability across almost all cognitive tasks.

The economic transition matters just as much. In the scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031 and real GDP grows by roughly 50 percent in 2032. As paid work shrinks, wage income and labor-tax revenue fall even while output rises. Plan A answers with permits on **compute**, the chips and datacenters used to train and run AI. Every US citizen receives one share in a Compute Dividend Corporation, whose portion of permit revenue rises from 25 percent in 2032 to 75 percent in 2035.

This ownership plan sits beside technical verification: tracked chips, inspected datacenters, documented safety evidence, and international coordination. Plan A even models a hidden Chinese datacenter in an appendix, quietly admitting the design's limit. Verification can reduce uncertainty, but it cannot eliminate concealment.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A combines managed technical progress with public ownership: control scarce computing infrastructure, charge for its use, and return a growing share of the proceeds to citizens.</p></div>

## The overlap is a second-order forecast

The Correction maps to Plan A's automation shock and weakening tax base. The Void maps to the interval in which output rises faster than wages, forcing a new link between production and purchasing power. Both documents call that link a **Citizen's Dividend**, a direct public share of AI-created income, and both point to Alaska. The Alignment maps to Plan A's aim to “Diffuse AI Broadly” and its one-share-per-citizen structure.

AI 2040 does not provide observed data that confirms my book. It is a second map drawn from a different starting point. The convergence supports a more useful proposition: the political fight over AI may shift from regulating model behavior to deciding who holds a claim on its infrastructure and income.

The documents still differ. Plan A goes further on verification, international agreements, and coordinated pauses. The Three Crucibles puts more weight on demand, distributed ownership, and the social passage before superintelligence. Ownership cannot solve technical loss of control, while inspections cannot decide whether wealth is shared fairly. We need both questions in the same room.

That gives us a test before 2040. Look for compute permits tied to public revenue, citizen ownership of productive capacity, or taxes that follow machine output rather than only human payroll. A Nordic country need not copy an American corporation, but it must answer the same question: what public claim remains if more production sits inside privately controlled chips and datacenters?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The shared forecast is not a date for superintelligence. It is an early shift from governing model behavior alone toward allocating ownership, tax claims, and returns around AI infrastructure.</p></div>

## Preparation is the point

No one knows what waits in 2040. Plan A may be too fast, too orderly, or wrong about the path. My stages may unfold differently. Yet waiting for certainty is also a choice, because ownership and tax rules are difficult to redesign after power has settled.

That is why The Three Crucibles is a preparation document rather than a prophecy. If Plan A and I are even directionally right, the road ahead will be bumpy: old links between work, income, and public revenue may weaken before replacements earn trust. We cannot know the destination, but we can enter the difficult part with better questions and room to choose.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Uncertainty weakens the case for prophecy, not preparation. The practical work is to shape ownership and distribution while those choices remain open.</p></div>
