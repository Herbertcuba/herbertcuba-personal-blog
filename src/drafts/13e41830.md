Two very different AI maps arrive at the same uncomfortable place. Machine productivity can rise while the wage income that keeps markets alive falls. [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) reaches this point through geopolitics. [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) reaches it through the economics of firms replacing human work.

The book names three stages. The Correction begins when automation cuts labor costs and weakens household income. The Transformation rebuilds demand through a Citizen's Dividend funded by AI productivity. The Alignment is the political choice between widely shared abundance and control by a small group of owners. The stages form one connected argument about production, purchasing power, and control.

Plan A comes from six authors at the AI Futures Project, including four authors of [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/): Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Eli Lifland, and Daniel Kokotajlo. They call Plan A “primarily a recommendation” and separate it from their best guess. I think the overlap is useful corroboration of a shared concern. Outcomes remain uncertain.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both frameworks connect automation to falling labor income, a Citizen's Dividend, and a choice about who controls the resulting abundance.</p></div>

## The same economic chain

Plan A imagines a 2029 US-China agreement that slows takeoff, meaning the speed at which capable AI systems improve. AI capability rises within the human range through 2035, pauses at top-expert level, and moves toward superintelligence in 2040. I think the calendar can fail while the economic logic remains relevant.

In the scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031 (p. 13). Labor taxes shrink, so the US auctions permits for new robots and computing capacity. Most of the proceeds fund a Citizen's Dividend worth $45,000 per adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035 (pp. 17–18). The book follows the same chain with a different funding tool: a tax on deployed AI labor. Its blunt demand-side warning is simple: “Robots do not buy iPhones.”

The public sequence supports a modest claim. A [March 23, 2026 repository commit](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) added the book PDF to my site. Plan A entered public discussion on [July 9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). The phrase Citizen's Dividend is older, while this full economic chain was already public in my framework before that July discussion.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The maps share an economic sequence but fund it differently. Plan A prices new machine capacity, while The Three Crucibles taxes machine labor in use.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership solve different failures

Plan A is strongest on verification: checking whether declared chips, datacenters, and AI research match reality. This addresses a state-level failure, where a country signs an agreement and secretly builds a more capable system anyway.

Its own Appendix D stress-tests that defense. A fictional Chinese project hides computing power equal to 500,000 Nvidia H100 chips in tunnels near the Medog Hydropower Station, with roughly 200 people aware. Like the surveillance system in *Andor*, the treaty can watch the visible machinery while a small network works through its seams.

The Three Crucibles starts with an economic failure. People can still lose practical control when a few firms own the infrastructure and its output. The book calls for distributed ownership and open AI infrastructure. Plan A offers a related mechanism through a Compute Dividend Corporation that gives every citizen one share. A share creates a claim on returns; open systems create another route for users and buyers.

My take is that ownership, verification, and supplier choice reinforce one another. I don't know which failure arrives first. I think these layers should be designed together because each protects a different kind of agency.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification checks secret development, broad ownership gives citizens a claim on AI output, and supplier choice keeps control useful in practice.</p></div>

## Put the theory through an exit test

The book's insurer thought experiment brings the issue into a normal business. One company keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. A rival uses a group of AI agents, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The speed looks attractive, but dependence grows as claims decisions move into the supplier's system.

At the next supplier review, run an AI exit test. The business owner should export the case records, decision rules, and access permissions needed to operate elsewhere. A replacement team then replays a sample using evaluation cases, the examples used to check whether the system reaches acceptable decisions, and restores service within an agreed time and cost. Transparency tells the buyer what can be inspected. The test shows whether the buyer can act.

Both maps point to Alaska for a reason. Its permanent fund was established in 1976, and the first resident dividend followed in 1982. Alaska settled a concrete ownership question early: part of the return from a shared resource would remain a public asset and reach residents. An AI contract makes a smaller version of that choice when it assigns export rights, transition support, and recovery limits before dependence deepens.

I think every AI strategy needs evidence from a live exit test. Control begins with the proven ability to leave.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A supplier promise is weak evidence. A live export, replay, and recovery exercise shows whether the buyer can change providers without losing the service.</p></div>
