What happens if machines make society much richer while work stops being the main way people receive income? That question sits beneath [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/), a global scenario released in July 2026, and beneath my 2026 speculative book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/).

Both documents reach back to Alaska for an answer. In 1976, voters amended the state constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenue entered the Alaska Permanent Fund, whose principal is invested for the future. The first Permanent Fund Dividend followed in 1982. The model matters here because it gives residents a claim on resource wealth without tying that claim to a job extracting the resource.

The framework in my book was written more than six months ago and was publicly available by March 23, before Plan A appeared in July. The timing is worth noting, but agreement between two speculative maps does not prove the future. It tells us which questions may deserve earlier attention.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles ask how income and power should work when machines produce more value than human labor, and both use Alaska as a precedent for shared wealth.</p></div>

## What AI 2040 asks us to imagine

AI 2040 comes from the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and recommends how governments might respond. It follows the group's 2025 scenario, AI 2027, but Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Its dates are conditional on governments following the plan.

The scenario moves from a United States–China agreement in 2029 to a pause at top-human-expert AI, systems roughly as capable as the best human specialists, in 2035. It then imagines a controlled move toward superintelligence in 2040. Superintelligence means systems that are much better, faster and cheaper than the best humans across essentially every task. Control depends heavily on monitoring compute, the processing capacity concentrated in advanced chips and data centers.

The economic mechanism is just as important. As AI takes over cognitive labor, meaning work based mainly on reasoning and analysis, wage income and the labor-tax base shrink relative to output. Plan A projects that AI performs a third of this work by mid-2031 and that real economic output grows by about 50 percent in 2032. Permits on chips and robots then finance a Citizen's Dividend projected at $45,000 per person in 2032 and almost $1 million in 2035. These are scenario numbers, not observed results.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A imagines governments slowing and monitoring advanced AI while redirecting part of its extraordinary economic output to every citizen.</p></div>

## The three crucibles, before the comparison

My map has three passages. **The Correction** is the point where abundant AI labor pushes down the economic value of routine cognitive work and makes ownership more important. **The Void** is the unstable period in which jobs, taxes and consumer demand no longer fit the new production system, so a citizen dividend becomes a bridge. Some editions use **The Transformation** for this middle passage; the label differs, but the economic problem is the same. **The Alignment** is the later choice between broadly shared abundance and concentrated control.

The overlap with Plan A is unusually specific. Both maps connect automation to a weaker labor-tax base, both use the name Citizen's Dividend, and both point to Alaska. They differ on funding: my book proposes an AI labor tax, a levy on value created by automated labor, while Plan A sells permits for scarce chips and robots. Both mechanisms try to preserve demand when machines produce but do not consume.

The overlap matters as a warning rather than as confirmation. One scenario cannot validate another. It can still show that different routes through the problem lead to the same pressure point: productivity alone does not decide who benefits.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two maps share the same economic sequence, from disrupted work to a citizen dividend and then a political choice over how abundance is distributed.</p></div>

## The point where the maps separate

Plan A concentrates on verification: tracked chips, inspected data centers and safety cases, which are structured arguments backed by evidence that a system can be operated safely. The Three Crucibles concentrates on ownership. Its central question is who keeps a durable claim on output when employment no longer distributes purchasing power.

Those are separate tests. Permits may improve control by limiting access to compute, yet the scarcity they create can also strengthen firms already able to buy access. Strong control with narrow ownership produces orderly concentration. Broad ownership with weak control spreads returns from systems that nobody can reliably stop.

Plan A even stress-tests its own verification bet. Under its assumptions, a covert project has less than a 10 percent chance of reaching top-expert-dominating AI, a system outperforming leading human specialists, without detection before 2043. The estimate is reassuring only inside the scenario. The larger lesson is that a complete plan needs both an enforceable stop right and a credible answer to who receives the gains.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical control cannot guarantee fair ownership, and broad ownership cannot make an uncontrollable system safe; a durable settlement needs both.</p></div>

<p>Kurt Vonnegut's 1952 novel imagines an automated United States that can produce plenty while leaving most people without meaningful economic power. The mapping is direct: production has been solved, but ownership, purpose and authority have not, which is the same gap both AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles are trying to prepare for.</p>

## What preparation means when certainty is impossible

No one knows whether either timeline will survive contact with reality. By 2030, the shared thesis should weaken if labor's share of income, the portion paid to workers, remains steady, median wages rise with AI productivity, and ownership of AI infrastructure becomes less concentrated. It should strengthen if output rises while wages and public revenue lag and ownership concentrates.

Uncertainty gives preparation its purpose. If AI 2040 and I are broadly right, the road ahead will be bumpy even if the destination is materially richer. That is what The Three Crucibles is for: a preparation document for choices that become harder once the new wealth and power have already found their owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>No forecast deserves blind belief, but the shared warning is testable enough to prepare for: watch work, wages, public revenue, control and ownership before the transition hardens around us.</p></div>
