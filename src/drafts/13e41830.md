Alaska voters created the Alaska Permanent Fund from mineral revenue in 1976; dividends began in 1982. Fifty years later, two AI futures use the name Citizen’s Dividend.

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 2026 conditional policy scenario from the nonprofit AI Futures Project. Its authors call it a recommendation, neither a prediction nor their best guess. Four of five front-page authors of [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its April 3, 2025 rapid-AI-race scenario, return.

My book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), was on my site by March 23, 2026; its PDF was updated on June 24. AI 2040 added its PDF link on July 9. This is file order, not influence or validation.

I think AI 2040 put numbers around a shift my framework had named and exposed its gaps. Together they separate infrastructure visibility, durable citizen rights, and supplier exit.

## The maps meet at the economic break

The Three Crucibles opens with The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. The Void and The Transformation develop the middle stage; the landing page uses The Transformation. The map moves from labor losing weight to another route from machine output to human income, then ownership.

Plan A models a similar break. In its scenario, AI performs one third of cognitive labor, work based on reasoning and judgment, by mid-2031. For 2032 it projects about 50 percent growth in real gross domestic product, or inflation-adjusted output, while job losses weaken income- and payroll-tax revenue. Machine-investment deductions also reduce taxable corporate income. The report says GDP cannot map directly to 2025 purchasing power, so it does not establish weaker household demand.

Plan A responds with capacity permits, tradable permissions to build scarce robot or computing infrastructure. In its scenario, US permit fees reach $50 trillion in fiscal 2032. A fictional Compute Dividend Corporation receives part of the fees, gives every US citizen one share, and pays $45,000 per American adult in real 2025 dollars. These are modeled outcomes.

[The Three Crucibles PDF](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) also proposes a Citizen’s Dividend and an AI Labor Equivalence Tax: a levy for each human-equivalent unit of AI work, tied to public revenue from a person in that role. Its own demand argument is blunt: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” I think the convergence is narrow but useful. Both works expect automation to strain a system financed through human work and require a legal route from machine output to citizens, although one taxes capacity and the other equivalent labor.

A framework this large should be able to lose. [Statistics Norway](https://www.ssb.no/en), Norway’s statistics agency, publishes industry data on [AI use](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/13265), [compensation](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/09170), and [productivity](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/09174), plus [tax and social-contribution data](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/10799) at sector rather than industry level.

A 2032 test could use 2023–2025 as baseline and match fast and slow AI adopters on earlier productivity. If fast adopters gain ten percentage points more productivity growth while employee compensation stays within two points of its baseline share of value added, that counts against the mechanism; a fall above five points supports it. Sector tax receipts remain a national cross-check, not industry evidence. Tax reform, oil shocks, reclassification, or results between thresholds are inconclusive. I think a strategic claim improves when it names the evidence that could defeat it.

## Verification and ownership solve different failures

Takeoff means AI automating AI research and accelerating its own improvement. Plan A’s conditional timeline reaches a US–China agreement in 2029, pauses in 2035 at top-human-expert capability, and resumes in 2040 toward superintelligence: AI better than the best humans at everything, while faster and cheaper.

Its restraint system depends on compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI. Major chip transactions would be declared, datacenters inspected, and research made transparent. Developers would submit safety cases, written arguments that a system will not cause irreversible catastrophe, for public and expert criticism.

Plan A stress-tests that design through a fictional Chinese covert project. Hidden chips feed a datacenter in tunnels beside the unfinished Medog Hydropower Station, where construction offers cover and a tunnel removes heat. The operation still needs equipment, electricity, and people. I think industrial AI capacity leaves physical traces, but inspectors may find them too late.

Ownership prevents another failure. Plan A’s corporation would hold a claim on permit fees, not productive AI assets. My book proposes AI infrastructure ownership by individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities. One spreads income; the other spreads claims on the machinery.

The distinction sharpens at each map’s end. Plan A’s 2040 story hands institutions and equipment to AI until humans could no longer shut everything down. The Alignment asks whether owners become “stewards of abundance” or “the new feudal lords.” I do not know which of us is right about governance, and neither do they. My take is that verification and economic rights belong together: evidence about machinery cannot decide who benefits, while ownership cannot establish safety.

<p>Plan A makes dangerous capacity visible; The Three Crucibles pushes ownership outward. Visibility exposes machinery, while economic rights determine who holds a durable claim on its output.</p>

## Exit applies the same logic inside an organization

The move from citizens’ rights to supplier contracts changes scale. A claim on automated wealth limits an owner’s power over income; a tested right to move a critical workflow limits a supplier’s power over operations. I think both secure bargaining power before dependence hardens.

My book compares two fictional insurers. One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; the other uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that handle parts of the claims process, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. Adoption can move faster than reversibility.

Reversibility capture occurs when a supplier retains data, licences, interfaces, permissions, certification, or compute needed to run a workflow elsewhere. Exit proof is a live handover before renewal using buyer-controlled historical cases. Require it when failure could stop a critical operation or harm people, or when two of three risks are high: sensitive data, supplier concentration, and replacement difficulty. Low-risk tools need only a tested export and documented settings.

The closest practice is a disaster-recovery exercise. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology, the federal technical standards agency, says exercises validate restoration capability. Portability testing shows that data and settings can move. Exit proof adds buyer cases, an acceptance record, and a renewal consequence. The leverage only exists before you sign.

| Decision field | Contract rule |
| --- | --- |
| Pack | Freeze model and environment versions, tools, data, buyer-controlled cases, expected results, and the grader, the approved scoring method. |
| Decision | Run three times. Each run meets safety, permission, and evidence rules; the median meets error, time, and cost limits. One retry covers a recorded external outage. A changed version, missing log, or unavailable grader is indeterminate and requires a full rerun. |
| Record and cost | Store versions, case ID, scores, logs, cost, decision, and approver. The business owner accepts residual risk; the technology team restores service. The buyer funds its team and reviewer, supplier fees are fixed, and the supplier funds repairs for missing access or documentation. |
| Renewal | Failure triggers repair and rerun, then support, repricing, or non-renewal unless the buyer’s risk-governance group approves a time-limited exception. |

An illustrative claims-triage test could allow 30 days and 15 person-days for 200 cases. Median error would stay within two points of the incumbent; every run would finish within an hour and under a pre-agreed cost. These are proposals, not a standard.

The [European Union’s 2024 AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, is a risk-based law that became generally applicable on August 2, 2026. Rules for general-purpose AI models, which support many tasks, applied from August 2, 2025; some high-risk rules transition later. Documents and logs support exit proof, but cannot demonstrate a handover.

A handover can still leave citizens without a claim on automated wealth, just as distributed ownership can coexist with hidden systems. My take is that each control repairs a different concentration of power. Alaska’s lesson is timing: the public claim came before oil income became ordinary. The machines may be built with these rights or without them; who holds the durable claim is still open.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Exit proof is a risk-tiered handover test agreed at signing and run before renewal against fixed versions, buyer-controlled cases, measurable limits, named owners, and allocated costs. Visibility, citizen rights, and exit must be secured before concentrated control becomes ordinary.</p>
</div>
