In 1976, Alaska voters amended the state constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenue would enter the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a publicly owned investment fund. The state invested part of a temporary resource windfall instead of spending all of it. Since 1982, eligible residents have received an [annual dividend](https://pfd.alaska.gov/Division-Info/About-Us) supported by the fund's investment earnings. A shared natural resource became a lasting public claim.

That old institutional choice sits inside two very different AI futures. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) applies the Alaska idea to AI infrastructure. [My 2026 speculative book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), uses the same precedent and the same phrase: a Citizen's Dividend, meaning a public share of the value created by AI infrastructure. My framework was in writing by February 3, 2026, and public on my site by March 23; Plan A appeared on July 9. The overlap does not prove that either future will happen. It makes the shared warning harder to dismiss.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows how temporary resource wealth can become a permanent public claim. Two AI scenarios now ask whether machine productivity needs the same treatment.</p></div>

## Plan A is a recommendation, not a prophecy

Plan A is a 90-page scenario and policy proposal from the AI Futures Project, a US nonprofit that studies advanced AI. Four of its six authors also created [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's 2025 scenario about a rapid AI race. Plan A describes itself as “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” which is essential context for every date and number that follows.

Under its preferred path, a US-China agreement in 2029 prevents fully automated AI research from producing superintelligence in 2030. Superintelligence means systems that outperform people across almost all cognitive work. The scenario instead allows AI capability to grow within the human range until 2035, pauses at systems that can dominate top human experts, and moves toward superintelligence in 2040 after new safeguards are established.

The control mechanism is compute: the chips and datacenters used to train and run AI. Plan A proposes tracking hardware, inspecting facilities, sharing research information, and requiring safety cases, which are documented arguments that a system is safe enough for a specified use. Its own appendix tests the weak point, estimating below a 10 percent chance that a covert project reaches top-expert-dominating AI without detection before 2043.

The economic mechanism is just as ambitious. Companies buy tradable permits to use specified compute or robots, and the permit revenue capitalizes a citizen-owned corporation. In the scenario, its dividend rises from $45,000 per person in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035. Those are conditional outputs from extraordinary assumptions about growth and international coordination, not ordinary forecasts.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A slows the AI race through international verification, then uses permit revenue from scarce compute and robots to give citizens a share of the resulting wealth.</p></div>

## The three crucibles describe the passage

My book gives this transition three stages before trying to solve it.

**The Correction** is the moment when rising output no longer requires a matching rise in human labor. The claim is not that every job vanishes at once. It is that wages and labor taxes can weaken as society's main way of distributing purchasing power even while production grows.

**The Transformation** is the unstable rebuilding period. It includes universal basic income, a regular payment to everyone regardless of employment, and the Citizen's Dividend as a direct claim on AI infrastructure's productivity. Income has to reach consumers through a new channel because machines can produce, but they do not create human demand.

**The Alignment** asks who controls the abundance that follows. Here alignment is larger than making an AI system follow instructions. It is the political choice between broad stewardship and concentrated ownership.

Plan A follows much the same passage. Its collapsing labor-tax base resembles the Correction. Its Alaska-inspired dividend resembles the Transformation almost line for line. Its concern about irreversible concentration of power reaches the Alignment. Neither work invented resource dividends, basic income, or public ownership. What catches my attention is the order in which both expect these problems to arrive.

The main disagreement is institutional. Plan A puts its weight on verification: governments should know who has powerful systems, how much compute they control, and whether they obey agreed limits. My book puts more weight on ownership: people need an enforceable claim on productive infrastructure and its output. Verification can limit what powerful actors do; ownership decides who benefits. A serious plan probably needs both.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works converge on economic correction, a new distribution system, and an ownership struggle. They diverge on whether verification or ownership carries more of the institutional load.</p></div>

## Preparation is the honest response

The shared economic claim can be tested before anyone builds superintelligence. By the end of 2030, look at the United States and European Union: if labor's share of national income and labor taxes' share of public revenue remain within five percentage points of their 2026 levels after broad AI adoption, the Correction needs serious revision. If output rises while both measures fall beyond that band, the concern gains weight. That result would test the mechanism, not prove the later stages.

No one knows what waits for us. Plan A may be wrong about speed, and my book may be wrong about the sequence. But if the two are even directionally right, the road to abundance will become bumpy long before it feels abundant. That is why my book is a preparation document. It cannot prove the future; it can make the questions of income, power, and ownership harder to postpone.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Uncertainty is not a reason to treat the transition as smooth. The useful response is to test the economic warning early and prepare public institutions before ownership hardens.</p></div>
