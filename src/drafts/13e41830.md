Alaska made its most important oil decision before the windfall arrived. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was still being built and before large revenues from Prudhoe Bay crude began flowing, voters created the Alaska Permanent Fund to save part of the state's mineral income for the public. The first dividend followed in 1982. Alaskans had established a public claim while the terms were still open.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a 2026 policy recommendation from the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that studies advanced AI and how governments might respond. Four of the five authors of [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's 2025 scenario about a rapid race toward advanced AI, also worked on it. The authors call Plan A a way to test their recommendations, not their best-guess forecast.

My book [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) was [published on March 22, 2026](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GTK53C9H). It names the transition as the Correction, the Transformation, and the Alignment. Plan A followed more than three months later, in July. That order does not establish influence or prove that either timeline is right. I think the useful result is more precise: the two maps reach the same economic fork, then expose different requirements for control.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works share an economic sequence, but their overlap supports a strategic question rather than validating either forecast.</p></div>

## The economic sequence converges

The Correction is the book's financial reckoning. An economy built on AI hype and inflated valuations collapses, removing the protection around work that companies have not automated yet. Once cheaper AI becomes competitive, keeping the same number of employees stops being a neutral choice. Plan A does not share that opening mechanism, but its scenario reaches a similar labor shock: by mid-2031, AI performs a third of all cognitive labor, meaning office work based mainly on analysis and judgment.

That shift creates a problem beyond unemployment. When paid human work falls, wage income and the personal-income and payroll taxes collected from it fall too. Production can keep rising while the tax system and household demand weaken, because machines produce but do not earn wages or shop. As *The Three Crucibles* puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.”

The book's second crucible, the Transformation, asks what replaces wages as both income and social structure. Its [Citizen's Dividend](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36) gives people a direct claim on the output of AI infrastructure. Plan A uses the same name and the same Alaska precedent. In its scenario, the United States sells tradable permits to build robots or computing capacity, the chips and facilities used to train and run AI. A dividend corporation owns a rising share of those permit fees, each US citizen owns one share in the corporation, and the projected payment, in inflation-adjusted 2025 dollars, grows from [$45,000 per American adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18).

The funding rules differ. [*The Three Crucibles* proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37), which taxes each human-equivalent unit of AI work according to the income-tax, social-security, and payroll revenue a person in that role would have generated. Plan A charges for permission to add new physical capacity. I think the convergence still matters because it goes beyond a general call for basic income: both works connect automation to weaker wages and demand, then answer with a public claim on machine production. You do not need to accept Plan A's speed or dollar figures to take that structure seriously.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Correction and Transformation meet Plan A in the same economic chain: automated cognitive labor weakens wages, taxes, and demand, creating pressure for a Citizen's Dividend even though the proposed funding methods differ.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership are different controls

The Alignment, the third crucible, is the political test of who controls AI infrastructure and on what terms its gains are shared. Plan A approaches this question through takeoff governance. Takeoff is the period when AI begins doing enough AI research to speed up its own improvement, potentially turning an ordinary technology race into a much faster one.

In Plan A's scenario, a 2029 US-China agreement limits the immediate race that could otherwise produce superintelligence by the end of 2030. Superintelligence means AI that outperforms the best humans across fields while working faster and more cheaply. Development then continues within the range of human ability until a separate pause in 2035 at top-expert AI, roughly the level of the strongest human specialists, before resuming toward superintelligence in 2040. The 2029 agreement manages a near-term race; the later pause governs a different level of ability.

The plan rests heavily on verification, meaning evidence that countries are obeying agreed limits. It calls for declarations of major chip and data-center transactions, reciprocal inspections, and almost completely public AI research. Its fictional covert-project appendix tests the weak point: roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents, a measure of computing performance rather than a literal chip count, are installed in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station, a huge dam under construction in Tibet. The construction provides cover, and before the dam is finished, small modular nuclear reactors and gas turbines provide power.

This is where [*The Matrix*](https://www.warnerbros.com/movies/matrix), the 1999 science-fiction film about intelligent machines running a simulated reality, becomes a useful metaphor. The film's green code appears weightless, but its machine world still needs physical machinery and power. Advanced AI leaves physical traces too, which gives inspectors something to find.

Inspection does not decide ownership. Plan A gives citizens a share in a dividend corporation, which is a claim on its profits rather than a share of the chips, models, or companies producing the value. [*The Three Crucibles* argues for distributed ownership](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=56): AI infrastructure that individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities can own, with models that run locally or on their own premises. I think these mechanisms address different risks. I don't know which balance can survive geopolitical pressure, but a system can be visible to inspectors while a small group still sets the terms of access and distribution.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses verification to constrain hidden computing capacity; Alignment asks who controls the capacity everyone can see and receives what it produces. Visibility and ownership solve different problems.</p></div>

## Practical control requires an exit test

The missing layer becomes clearer inside a company. [*The Three Crucibles* imagines](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21) one insurer keeping 5,000 claims adjusters while a rival uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that divide and carry out parts of the claims process. The rival cuts staff by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. This is a thought experiment, not a reported case, but it shows why adoption pressure can move faster than a buyer's ability to leave the supplier behind the automation.

My take is that a critical AI contract should renew only after exit proof: a supplier-funded handover test showing that a replacement team can run one working process. A useful first test could cover one claims workflow and a portable copy of its case data over 30 days, with the chief information officer accountable for the renewal decision. The buyer should own the past cases used to evaluate the replacement, while the supplier provides the needed permissions, connection details, source records, and saved versions of workflow settings and model instructions.

The replacement may change the model, its instructions, how it retrieves source material, or the workflow itself. What matters is whether it reproduces the agreed business result within fixed limits for errors, time, and cost. Failure should block automatic renewal and trigger funded transition help; a second failure should allow the buyer to leave without a termination fee.

I think this is what can break next. A state may verify computing capacity and citizens may receive part of its return, yet an institution can remain trapped inside the system carrying out its work. Control becomes real only when the right to choose survives a failed handover.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof turns supplier choice into a real contract test: if a replacement cannot reproduce the business result, the supplier still controls the workflow regardless of what its disclosures promise.</p></div>
