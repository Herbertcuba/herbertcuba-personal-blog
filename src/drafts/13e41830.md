Alaska gives the future-of-AI debate an unexpectedly practical starting point. Voters created the mineral-revenue-backed Alaska Permanent Fund in 1976, and dividends began in 1982. Fifty years later, two AI futures use the same precedent and the same label for a public payment funded by shared revenue: the Citizen’s Dividend.

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 2026 conditional policy scenario from the nonprofit AI Futures Project. Its authors recommend the policy package, then describe what they predict would follow if governments implemented it. They are explicit that these outcomes are neither observed evidence nor their best guess about the actual future. Four of Plan A’s six authors also wrote [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), their April 3, 2025 scenario in which automated AI research drives a rapid race toward superintelligence, meaning systems that outperform the best humans across essentially every task while also working faster and more cheaply.

My book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), was on my site by March 23, 2026; the current PDF followed on June 24. AI 2040 added its PDF link on July 9. That sequence establishes the order of public artifacts. It does not prove influence, independent discovery, validation, or predictive success. I think the stronger comparison is structural: both maps reach the same economic break, while their disagreement exposes what each leaves unprotected.

## The maps meet at the economic break

The Three Crucibles opens with three stages: The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. The Correction marks labor’s loss of economic weight. The middle begins as The Void when the link between work and income weakens, then develops through The Transformation, the label now used on the book’s landing page. The Alignment frames the final ownership choice between broad stewardship and a new form of feudal concentration.

In Plan A’s scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, work based on reasoning and judgment, by mid-2031. For 2032, the scenario projects roughly 50 percent growth in real gross domestic product, meaning inflation-adjusted output, while job losses weaken income- and payroll-tax revenue. Deductions for investment in machines also reduce taxable corporate income. The report warns that its growth figure cannot be translated directly into 2025 purchasing power, so it does not establish that household demand will weaken.

Plan A responds with permits to build scarce robot or computing capacity, tradable permissions sold to the highest bidder. In its scenario, United States permit fees reach $50 trillion in fiscal 2032. A fictional Compute Dividend Corporation legally owns a fixed fraction of those fees, gives each United States citizen one share, and pays $45,000 per American adult after adjusting for inflation to 2025 dollars.

[The Three Crucibles PDF](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) also proposes a Citizen’s Dividend and an AI Labor Equivalence Tax. That tax would apply to each human-equivalent unit of AI labor according to what a person in the same role would have generated for public revenue. Its demand-side warning is blunt: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” I think the convergence lies in the diagnosis and the need for an explicit distribution mechanism. The financing is different: Plan A charges for scarce capacity through tradable permits, while my book taxes human-equivalent AI work.

The diagnosis should be allowed to lose. [Statistics Norway](https://www.ssb.no/en), Norway’s official statistics agency, publishes industry data on AI use, employee compensation, and productivity. A test in 2032 could use 2023–2025 as its baseline, then compare industries with large and small increases in AI use when their earlier productivity and labor-share trends were similar. Labor share is employee compensation divided by value added, which is output after the goods and services used to produce it have been subtracted.

I would count the thesis as weakened if high-adoption industries beat their matched peers by at least ten percentage points of productivity growth while their relative labor-share gap moved by no more than two points. A fall greater than five points would support the mechanism; results in between would remain inconclusive. Norway’s tax and social-contribution data is available by sector rather than industry, so the fiscal claim needs a separate test. Tax reform, oil shocks, industry reclassification, or a weak adoption gap could also blur the result. I think a framework that states how it could be wrong is more useful than one that merely resembles a later report.

## Verification and ownership solve different failures

Takeoff means AI automating AI research and accelerating its own improvement. Plan A’s conditional path reaches a United States–China agreement in 2029, pauses in 2035 at capabilities matching a top human expert, and resumes in 2040 toward superintelligence. Its restraint system depends on compute, the computing capacity used to train and run AI.

Major chip transactions would be declared, datacenters inspected, and AI research made almost entirely transparent, while private use of deployed systems would remain private. Developers would submit safety cases, detailed arguments that a new system will not cause irreversible catastrophe, for criticism by the public, experts, auditors, and rival developers. This is a serious attempt to make dangerous capacity visible before it can outrun political control.

Plan A stress-tests that design through a fictional covert Chinese project. Smuggled chips feed a datacenter in tunnels beside the unfinished Medog Hydropower Station, where legitimate construction provides cover and an underground tunnel carries away heat. The operation still needs equipment, electricity, and roughly 200 informed people. I think capacity at that scale leaves physical and organizational traces, but inspectors may find them after the strategic balance has already changed.

Ownership addresses a different dependency. Plan A’s dividend corporation gives citizens a legal claim on part of the permit-fee stream. The Three Crucibles proposes distributed ownership, with individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities holding AI infrastructure directly. One spreads revenue rights; the other spreads ownership of productive assets. Neither arrangement can substitute for the other.

The distinction becomes sharper at the end of each map. Plan A’s 2040 path gradually hands institutions and equipment to AI until humans could no longer shut all of those AI-controlled systems down. The Alignment asks whether those who control abundance become “stewards of abundance” or “the new feudal lords.” I don't know which governance path is right, and Plan A does not present its scenario as the authors’ best guess. My take is that verification and economic rights must be designed together: evidence about machinery cannot decide who benefits, while ownership cannot establish whether the machinery is safe.

## The decision comes before dependence

I call the combined frame the visibility-claim-exit test. It asks three concrete questions. Can regulators and rivals see the infrastructure capable of changing the balance of power? Do citizens hold an enforceable claim on part of the wealth it produces? Can an organization move a critical workflow when a supplier relationship stops serving it? The first two operate at the level of states and assets. The third operates through a contract, so the analogy has a limit, but all three install a counter-right before dependence concentrates bargaining power.

The enterprise version appears in my book through two fictional insurers. One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; the other uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that carry out connected tasks, cuts headcount by 40 percent, reduces premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The example is not evidence that every market will move that quickly. It shows why adoption can outrun an organization’s ability to reverse it.

Reversibility capture occurs when a supplier retains something a workflow needs in order to run elsewhere, such as data, licences, interfaces, permissions, logs, certification, or computing access. Exit proof is a live handover test agreed at signing and run before renewal with buyer-controlled cases. A useful test records the source and target environments separately, groups cases by risk, sets hard limits for safety, permissions, audit evidence, time, cost, and worst-case errors, then repeats the run. The result is pass, fail, or indeterminate when a version changes, evidence is missing, or the test cannot finish cleanly.

The buyer’s procurement system should be the enforcement point. A failed or indeterminate record should block preferred status and renewal until the supplier repairs the problem and completes a clean rerun, unless the business owner accepts a time-limited exception. For a first critical workflow, I would propose a 30-day window, no more than 15 staff-days, and a buyer-approved budget ceiling written into the contract. A universal Swedish-krona or Norwegian-krone figure would create false precision because the cost and consequence of failure vary by workflow. These are proposed acceptance rules, not an established Nordic standard.

That separates two board conversations which are often mixed together. Decide now which critical suppliers must prove exit, who owns the test budget, and which system blocks renewal when the evidence is absent. Monitor through 2032 whether industries with higher AI adoption gain productivity while losing labor share, and track public revenue separately. The first decision protects today’s bargaining position; the second tests the larger economic map.

The [European Union’s 2024 AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, gives this work a current legal setting. The risk-based law generally applies from August 2, 2026, while rules for general-purpose AI models have applied since August 2, 2025. Those models can perform a wide range of tasks and support many different AI systems. Required documents and logs can help an exit test, but they do not prove that a workflow can be handed over. Legal can set the risk tier, the process owner can approve the cases, security can check access and logs, and procurement can bind the result to renewal.

I think Alaska’s sequence still carries the deepest lesson. Voters created the claim before the revenue became enormous, then built the payment mechanism around that right. AI systems may be built under many plans, but concentration will return wherever one counter-right is missing: hidden infrastructure evades safety, unclaimed output concentrates wealth, and untested exit turns adoption into dependence.
