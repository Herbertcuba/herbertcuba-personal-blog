In 1976, as Alaska prepared for oil revenue from Prudhoe Bay, the state amended its constitution to put part of its mineral income into a permanent fund. Investment returns financed the first resident dividend in 1982. The important choice came before the money became enormous: Alaskans established a public claim while the terms were still open.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a 2026 policy recommendation from the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that studies advanced AI and how governments might respond. Four of the five authors of [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's 2025 scenario about rapid AI development, also worked on it. The authors are clear that Plan A is not their best-guess forecast.

My book [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) describes the transition through the Correction, the Transformation, and the Alignment. It was already public when Plan A appeared in July 2026. That sequence cannot prove influence, independent discovery, or predictive accuracy. I think it supports a narrower and more useful claim: the two maps reach the same economic fork, then disagree about what control requires.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works share an economic sequence, but their overlap strengthens a strategic question rather than validating either forecast.</p></div>

## The economic sequence converges

The Correction begins when cheap AI makes employing fewer people a competitive necessity rather than a choice. Plan A places this break inside its scenario: by mid-2031, AI performs a third of all cognitive labor, meaning work based mainly on analysis, research, and judgment. As payroll falls, personal-income and payroll taxes shrink even while production rises. That is how an AI boom can weaken the tax base built for a labor economy.

The Transformation asks what sustains demand once wages stop carrying most household income. The book's line is blunt: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Its Citizen's Dividend is a direct public claim on the productivity of AI infrastructure. Plan A uses the same name and the same Alaska precedent, but funds the payment through tradable permits to build robots or computing capacity, the chips and facilities used to train and run AI. Every US citizen receives one share in a dividend corporation funded by a growing portion of the permit fees. In the scenario, the payment rises from [$45,000 per American adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18).

The funding methods still differ. *The Three Crucibles* proposes taxing deployed AI work according to the public revenue an equivalent human worker would have generated; Plan A charges for permission to add robots or computing capacity. I think the convergence lies in the economic mechanism: automation reduces wage income, which weakens both tax revenue and consumer demand, so citizens need a claim on machine output. You do not need to accept Plan A's timetable to take that arithmetic seriously.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both works connect automated cognitive labor to weaker wages, taxes, and demand, then answer with a Citizen's Dividend funded through different mechanisms.</p></div>

## The maps split at Alignment

The Alignment is the political fork: will the owners of AI infrastructure act as stewards of abundance, or become a new class of feudal lords? Plan A approaches that question through verification, meaning evidence that states are following agreed limits. In its sequence, a 2029 US-China agreement blocks a race that could otherwise reach superintelligence by the end of 2030. Superintelligence here means AI that outperforms the best humans across fields while working faster and more cheaply. Development continues within the range of human ability until a separate 2035 pause at top-expert AI, roughly the level of the strongest human specialists, before resuming in 2040.

Plan A's verification system requires declarations of major chip and data-center transactions, reciprocal inspections, and almost entirely transparent AI research. Its fictional covert-project appendix stress-tests that system. In the scenario, roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents, a measure of computing performance rather than a literal chip count, are installed in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station. Ongoing dam construction provides cover, temporary reactors and gas turbines supply power, and only about 200 people know.

This is where [*The Matrix*](https://www.warnerbros.com/movies/matrix), the 1999 science-fiction film about intelligent machines running a simulated world, becomes useful rather than decorative. Its green code appears weightless, but the machine city still needs hardware, power, cooling, and concrete. Advanced AI has physical traces too, which gives inspectors something to find.

Inspection, however, does not settle ownership. *The Three Crucibles* argues for AI infrastructure owned by individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, with models that can run locally or on an organization's own premises. Plan A gives citizens a share in a dividend corporation, not in the chips, models, or companies producing the value. I think verification and ownership address different risks. I don't know which balance can survive geopolitical pressure, but a system can be visible and still leave power highly concentrated.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses inspection to constrain hidden computing capacity; the Alignment asks who controls lawful capacity and receives what it produces.</p></div>

## Control also needs a tested exit

The missing layer becomes easier to see inside a company. *The Three Crucibles* imagines one insurer retaining 5,000 claims adjusters while a rival uses coordinated AI agents, cuts staff by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. It is a thought experiment, not a reported case. Its point is that adoption pressure can move faster than a buyer's ability to leave the supplier behind that automation.

My take is that a critical AI contract should renew only after exit proof: a supplier-funded test showing that one working process can move to a replacement team. A useful first test would cover one workflow and one usable data extract over 30 days, with the chief information officer accountable for the renewal decision. The supplier would provide prior case decisions, the credentials and permissions needed to operate the process, saved versions of settings and model instructions, software connections, records showing where source information came from, and recovery guidance.

The buyer should own the past cases used to evaluate the replacement. The new team may change the model, its instructions, how it finds source material, or the workflow itself, but it must reproduce the agreed business result within fixed limits for errors, time, and cost. Failure should block automatic renewal and trigger funded transition support; a second failure should let the buyer leave without a termination fee.

I think this gives the comparison a practical consequence. Verification governs concealed capacity, ownership governs the distribution of gains, and exit proof tests whether an institution can exercise bargaining power. If disclosures look complete but the handover fails, supplier dependence is no longer an opinion. It is a measured result.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof turns freedom of choice into a renewal test: when a replacement cannot reproduce the business result, the supplier still governs the workflow.</p></div>
