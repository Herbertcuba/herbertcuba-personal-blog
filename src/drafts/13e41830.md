In 1976, Alaska voters amended their state constitution to place at least a quarter of certain mineral revenues into the Alaska Permanent Fund. The fund invests that money rather than spending its principal, and dividend payments to eligible residents began in 1982. A shared, finite resource became an asset that citizens could benefit from directly.

Fifty years later, the same institution appears in two accounts of an AI-shaped future. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) models a national dividend funded by permits for computing capacity and robots. My 2026 speculative book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), also uses Alaska to explain a Citizen's Dividend. The book has been public on my site since March 2026, months before Plan A appeared in July.

That overlap deserves attention without becoming proof. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” and my book is a preparation document. Both maps point toward the same pressures around work, income, control, and ownership.

## What Plan A is proposing

Published in July 2026, AI 2040 is a scenario and policy proposal from the AI Futures Project, the forecasting group behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its 2025 scenario about rapid progress toward superhuman AI.

Plan A starts with a 2029 US-China agreement designed to avoid a 2030 race in which AI automates the research needed to improve AI, rapidly approaching superintelligence, or systems far beyond humans across almost all intellectual tasks. Development instead stays within human capability until a 2035 pause at “top-expert AI,” systems that outperform leading human specialists. After more safety work, scaling resumes toward superintelligence in 2040.

The plan depends on verification: checking that agreed limits are followed. It would track compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI, alongside chip controls, data-center inspections, and research transparency. Its appendix estimates below a 10 percent chance that a covert project reaches top-expert AI without detection before 2043. That is a stress test, not an assurance.

The economic scenario is equally dramatic. AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, or thinking-intensive work, by mid-2031, while real GDP, meaning inflation-adjusted economic output, grows by about 50 percent in 2032. Part of the permit fees for compute and robots funds a Compute Dividend Corporation, with one share for every US citizen and profits paid as dividends.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A combines staged AI development and verification with a permit-funded citizen dividend.</p></div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

The Three Crucibles describes three social pressures rather than one precise technical timetable.

**The Correction** begins when AI lets companies produce more with fewer workers. Output can rise while employment, wages, and the public revenue tied to them weaken.

**The Void** follows when the economy recovers but many jobs do not. Displaced workers lose the purchasing power needed to buy what automated companies produce. As the book puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Its bridge is a Citizen's Dividend supported by an AI Labor Equivalence Tax: a levy on value produced by AI labor when it replaces human employment, redirecting part of that value to citizens and replacing lost wage-based tax revenue.

**The Alignment** is the political choice that follows. If machines produce more of society's wealth, ownership determines whether people become stewards of abundance or live under a new concentration of power. It asks who owns the productive systems and who holds a lasting claim on their output.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction breaks the job-growth link, The Void opens an income gap, and The Alignment settles ownership.</p></div>

## Where the maps meet and separate

The closest match is economic. Both maps expect automation to weaken wages and the labor-tax base, then reach for the same Citizen's Dividend and Alaska precedent. Their funding differs: Plan A charges for permission to build scarce compute and robots, while my framework taxes value produced by AI labor as it replaces taxed human work.

Their deeper difference concerns stability. Plan A puts more weight on verification between states and AI developers. My book puts more weight on distributed ownership and a lasting citizen claim on output. Verification can reveal a broken limit, but it cannot decide who benefits from compliant systems. Ownership can spread economic power, but it cannot stop a dangerous system from escaping control.

The comparison remains useful even if Plan A's dates are wrong. AI 2040 adds a detailed safety and governance layer; The Three Crucibles adds an ownership and legitimacy layer. Each exposes a question the other cannot settle alone.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The maps share an economic diagnosis but emphasize different remedies: verifiable limits and distributed ownership.</p></div>

## Preparation without prophecy

No one knows whether advanced AI will follow either sequence. Progress may be slower, international agreements may fail, and economic adaptation may take forms neither document sees. Agreement cannot remove that uncertainty.

It can still sharpen preparation. If both maps are directionally right, technical risk, lost labor income, and concentrated ownership will arrive as connected pressures while the technology advances. My book exists as a preparation document for that difficult passage, not as a calendar that must come true.

The dates may move, and some events may never happen. The choices about control, ownership, and distribution are already visible.
