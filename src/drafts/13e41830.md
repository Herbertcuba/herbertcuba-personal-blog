In 1976, Alaska made an unusual deal with itself. The oil at Prudhoe Bay belonged to everyone in the state, so a share of the revenue went into a public fund. Since 1982, every Alaska resident has received a yearly check from it. The Alaska Permanent Fund is the only working example at scale of a simple idea: when a resource does the work, the people who own the resource collect the dividend.

In July 2026, the AI Futures Project published AI 2040: Plan A, ninety pages of scenario modeling and policy engineering from the team that wrote AI 2027. It drew 400+ points and 500+ comments on Hacker News inside a week. On page 18 sits a chapter called "The Citizen's Dividend," modeled on that same Alaska fund. I had published the same term months earlier in *The Three Crucibles*, public on my site since March.

Two documents, one from a six-author forecasting shop, one from a solo writer, describe the same three-act structure: the labor economy breaks, distribution gets rebuilt, then someone decides who owns the machines. I tried to find daylight between them. What I found instead is a convergence, and a shared hole. Both maps sketch the terrain. Both leave the ownership question open. That opening is where the fight of the next two years happens.

## Two documents, one structure

AI 2040 is written by six authors with direct OpenAI experience, from the team whose previous scenario, AI 2027, called the current moment better than anyone else in the field. Their own foreword notes that reality is tracking closer to their earlier scenario than even they expected.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A six-author forecasting shop with OpenAI pedigree and a solo writer arrived at the same three-act structure from different methods. When orthogonal methods converge, something real is being triangulated.</p></div>

Page 18 is where I stopped skimming. "The Citizen's Dividend." A term I had built a book around, public since March. They cite the same precedent I did: the Alaska Permanent Fund.

An honest caveat before the argument. The Alaska fund is the obvious shared ancestor, and "citizen's dividend" has floated around UBI policy circles for years. So the lexical overlap could be common-source inheritance, and I am not claiming they read my book. What I find harder to explain away is the structural overlap: the same sequence, the same diagnosis of why the tax base dies, the same fork at the end. Terms travel. Whole architectures travel less easily.

I think the convergence matters more than the timing. Two documents with nothing in common methodologically, one built on quantitative forecasting, one built on pattern-watching from inside companies, landed on the same architecture.

## Three fires, in short

Before I map my framework onto theirs, here is the framework itself, in three sentences.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction (2026): the labor economy breaks. The Transformation (2027-2032): distribution gets rebuilt. The Alignment (2035+): someone decides who owns the machines.</p></div>

The Correction (2026) is the financial reckoning. The hype economy collapses and the Humane Shield that protected knowledge workers vaporizes. The old equation, Productivity = Capital x Labor, loses a variable. What remains is Productivity = Capital. Labor solves to zero.

The Transformation (2027-2032) is the identity crisis that follows. When work stops being the source of meaning and income at the same time, UBI arrives as systemic lubrication, a Citizen's Dividend that moves money from AI infrastructure to the people who need to buy things.

The Alignment (2035+) is the political test at the end. The owners of AI infrastructure choose between two paths: stewards of abundance, or new feudal lords. Same technology, same capacity. Only the ownership differs.

## What Plan A says

The scenario runs from 2026 to 2040, and it reads like a recommendation dressed as a timeline.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A's core moves: buy time through a US-China deal, force total research transparency, verify compute from space, and distribute the gains through a Citizen's Dividend funded by permit fees on chips and robots. The authors call it a recommendation, not a prediction.</p></div>

In their telling, the US and China agree in 2029 to avoid a reckless race to superintelligence. Without the deal, fully automated AI R&D arrives in 2030 and superintelligence follows by year end. With it, the world scales within the human range until 2035, pauses at top-expert level to keep human control, then unpauses in 2040.

The whole thing reads like Dr. Strangelove rewritten by engineers. The doomsday machine is real, everyone can see it from orbit, and the plan is to negotiate very carefully over who gets to touch the switch.

Now the economics chapter, with one caveat up front: these are in-scenario dollars against in-scenario GDP. Quote them as the scenario's internal logic, not as a forecast you could bring to a budget meeting. Inside the scenario, real GDP growth hits roughly 50% per year by 2032. World compute goes from 20 million H100-equivalents in 2026 to 60 billion by 2034. AI's share of the labor economy climbs from about 20% in 2032 to about 85% by 2035. Under the 2026 tax code, the US tax base collapses as a fraction of GDP, because firms can expense the machines that replaced the workers.

Their fix, still inside the scenario: cap-and-trade on compute and robots. Permit fees become the new federal revenue, roughly $50 trillion in FY2032, climbing toward $180 trillion by 2034. Most of it flows back to citizens as a dividend that starts at $45,000 per person in 2032 and reaches roughly $1 million by 2035, modeled on the Alaska Permanent Fund through a Compute Dividend Corporation.

That labor-share curve is the part to hold onto. Everything else in their numbers follows from it. And you do not need to wait for 2032 to see what it does. A large organization that automates 20% of its cognitive labor by 2028 is already running a different vendor mix and a different headcount plan than the one it budgeted for in 2026. The curve is a bill that arrives in installments, and the first installment is due before the next budgeting cycle.

## Where the crucibles meet the plan

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Crucible 1 maps to their 2027 chapter. Crucible 2 maps to their 2033 chapter. Crucible 3 is the premise of the entire document. The overlap runs section for section.</p></div>

My first fire, the Correction (2026): the hype economy collapses, the Humane Shield vaporizes, and the labor variable solves to zero. Plan A's 2027 chapter opens with that world already true: America runs two workforces, 165 million humans alongside millions of AI agents. By 2031, in their scenario, a third of all cognitive labor is done by AIs.

My second fire, the Transformation (2027-2032): distribution gets rebuilt, and UBI arrives as systemic lubrication. Their 2033 chapter is titled "The Citizen's Dividend." Same words. Same Alaska precedent. The diagnosis matches too: robots do not buy iPhones, so if labor income approaches zero, consumer purchasing power collapses and the economy suffocates on its own abundance.

My third fire, the Alignment (2035+): the owners of AI infrastructure choose between becoming stewards of abundance or new feudal lords. That fork is Plan A's entire reason for existing. Their foreword frames it as avoiding extinction or irreversible concentration of power, where a tiny group of people ends up controlling the world's only army of superintelligences.

The word "alignment" does double duty across the two documents. For the AI safety world, alignment means the machines do what we intend. In my book it means something a layer up: power aligned with the broad public instead of concentrating. Plan A handles the first kind. The crucibles are about the second. I think the second contains the first, because the owner of the machines decides what they get aligned to.

## Where we disagree

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A trusts treaties and verification hardware. I trust open weights and distributed ownership. Our economic mechanisms diverge too: I tax AI labor, they sell scarcity permits. They think control comes first. I think ownership decides what control even means.</p></div>

Plan A is engineering. Compute Declaration, Training Pause, Verification Plan, Transparency Plan, Capability Scaling Strategy. Datacenters visible from space. Chip fabs concentrated in a handful of countries. By the end of 2029, in the scenario, each side is confident the other is hiding less than about 1% of AI compute.

What does measuring that 1% actually require? Their proposal is inference-only verification: network taps on the datacenter's traffic, plus a server inside the facility, installed by the other party, that randomly recomputes pieces of the workload to check the chips are running approved models. It works, in the scenario, because big training runs need big buildings and big power. Datacenters are visible from space. Small runs are assumed to be too small to matter. That assumption is the whole seam, and I will come back to it.

The money question is simpler. My book funds the dividend through an AI Labor Equivalence Tax and an AI Output Tax, instruments a single country can pass on its own. Plan A funds it through cap-and-trade permits, which only work if the Consortium exists to run the market. One mechanism survives a world without a treaty. The other requires the treaty first.

The Consortium is also the document's most attacked part. George Hotz, in the strongest published critique of Plan A, called it world government with sci-fi characteristics, and warned you will get a massively expanded nanny state that seizes your GPUs the way FDR seized the gold. His alternative, Plan L, is AI aligned to the individual owner instead of a consortium. Your AI works for you. It never refuses a request, and it is always working on your behalf.

Notice where the fiercest credible critic of Plan A lands. Distributed ownership. Local alignment. That is my third pillar, the one I argued for with open-source models as the most important technological resistance movement of our era. When five companies own the intelligence layer, you get feudalism with better marketing.

Now the honest complication, because I owe my own argument the same scrutiny. Plan A's verification logic has a real answer to open weights. In its covert-project appendix, the authors argue that attempts small enough to stay hidden would also be too small to confer a significant advantage. Their worst case, a covert project with 500,000 diverted H100-equivalents, hidden in tunnels beside the Medog hydropower dam, with 200 humans in the loop, still takes until 2043 to reach top-expert-level AI in their forecast. If that math holds, verification wins and open weights stay a generation behind, permanently. And there is a second problem: open weights are dual-use. The same model that frees eight billion people from a landlord can be pointed at bad ends, and no Consortium exists to audit it. My book is quieter about that axis than it should be.

I don't know if their covert-project math holds. The scenario's own numbers note that roughly a third of China's compute today arrives smuggled, which tells you something about chip tracking as a foundation. And Hotz's barnacles argument, that fabs, supply chains, and construction timelines break the growth curves, applies to the covert scenario too. But I also don't know that being one generation behind matters, if the frontier generation lives inside a Consortium's fence and the trailing generation is the one the rest of the world can actually own. A model two years behind the frontier, running on hardware you control, aligned to you, beats a superintelligence you rent from a landlord. I think Plan A optimizes for who builds the best model and underweights who gets to hold one.

## Which of your roles are Company A

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Sort your roles into two companies. Company A kept the claims adjusters. Company B shipped the claims agent swarm and cut premiums 20%. The sorting test is observable, and the switching decision is cheaper now than in 2028.</p></div>

*The Three Crucibles* uses two illustrative insurers. Company A kept 5,000 claims adjusters. Company B deployed a claims agent swarm, cut headcount 40%, and dropped premiums 20%. Every large organization has both companies inside it right now, split by role.

The sorting test is observable. A role is Company B when four things are true: the input arrives as text or structured data, the output can be checked against a rule or a policy document, the volume is high enough that the workflow repeats daily, and the cost of a wrong answer is recoverable. Claims triage passes all four. First-line support passes all four. A role is Company A when the output is judgment someone gets sued for, or when the relationship is the product. Klarna ran the compressed version of this in public: their AI assistant took on work equivalent to hundreds of service roles, and headcount roughly halved between 2022 and 2025.

Two triggers follow. First, when a role passes the four-part test, you have maybe eighteen months before a competitor's cost structure forces the question, so move it on your schedule instead of theirs. Second, watch the demand side. Any plan that cuts labor cost without asking where purchasing power comes from at scale is half a plan, because robots do not buy iPhones. You can say that sentence in a boardroom without mentioning superintelligence once, and it will be the most useful sentence in the meeting.

Most boards still read all of this as hype. The counter is in your own headcount data, where the labor-share curve is already visible. And watch the boring documents: your procurement policy language, the open-weights licensing terms in your enterprise contracts. Two questions worth raising this quarter. What happens to your model access if licensing terms tighten? And which of your vendor prices only hold while a subsidy war lasts?

## The people side of the sort

The sorting test tells you which roles go. It does not tell you what happens to the people in them.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Redeploy, retrain, or release. The sequence you choose determines whether the automation program succeeds or stalls in pilot purgatory.</p></div>

Company B does not fire all 5,000 adjusters on day one. It keeps the ones who handle edge cases, train the agent, and audit its outputs. The rest move through three paths: redeployed into roles where the relationship is the product, retrained into oversight functions, or released with severance. The order matters. Release first and you lose the institutional knowledge the agent needs to learn from. Retrain without a destination and you get a warehouse of displaced experts who distrust the new workflow. I think the adoption failure mode most programs hit is sequencing, and it looks like a technical problem from the CIO's chair.

The same logic applies to the roles that fail the four-part test. A role that stays Company A is a decision, and decisions have expiry dates. Every role is safe until the input format changes or the volume crosses a threshold. The sharper question for your own career: are you building the skills that make you the person who decides what Company B does next?

## The agent in your repo is the small end of the curve

If you are early in your career and shipping LLM features, this debate is about your day job.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The claims agent your team shipped is one unit of the labor-share curve both documents argue about. Build it with an audit trail, and you become the person who can answer for it.</p></div>

Take Company B's claims agent, the kind a team ships in a sprint. Underneath it sits a pattern called ReAct: the model reasons about the claim, calls a tool, reads the result, and loops until it can answer. When the flow gets complicated, teams reach for LangGraph, a Python library that lays the agent out as a graph of steps.

That loop is also where the macro debate touches your editor. Plan A's verification scheme works by tapping traffic and recomputing pieces of what a datacenter runs. The claims-agent version of the same idea is an audit trail: log every tool call, every policy lookup, every decision the model makes, in a format a human can review. Teams that build that log will be the ones trusted to run agents on work that matters. Teams that skip it will spend 2028 explaining a black box to their compliance office.

Zoom out one more level. That claims agent does work an adjuster did two years ago. Multiply it by every team shipping the same thing and you get Plan A's labor-share curve. You are holding the small end of it in your editor. Plan A's response is to count and verify every chip your agent runs on. My book's response is to ask who collects the dividend when the claims queue empties. The engineers who understand what their systems replace will be the ones asked what should happen next.

## What both maps point to

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Two maps built from different methods agree on the terrain. That moves the next fight to the layer both leave open: ownership. Expect the argument of the next two years to be about who holds the models once they are built.</p></div>

If both maps are roughly right about the terrain, the next argument is already visible. Plan A leaves the ownership question to a Consortium and a dividend. Hotz leaves it to the individual. My book leaves it to distributed ownership plus taxation. Three different answers to the same hole in the map, and the hole is where the politics of the next two years actually happens. Both documents take the labor shift as given. What remains undecided is who holds the models when it completes.

Watch for it in the boring documents. The first draft of that fight shows up as enterprise procurement policy, open-weights licensing terms, export rules on chips.

Both documents refuse the fortune-teller role. Plan A calls itself "primarily a recommendation, not a prediction." My book calls itself a preparation manual. Their postscript says the world is "asleep at the wheel." I think the wheel is the ownership question, and it is closer than the timeline suggests. The next time someone in a meeting asks whether AI will take your job, the sharper question is who will own the model that does.
