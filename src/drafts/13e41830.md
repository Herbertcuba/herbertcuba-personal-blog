## The map and the blueprint

In July 2026, the AI Futures Project published AI 2040: Plan A. Ninety pages of scenario modeling, treaty architecture, and economic forecasting from the team that wrote AI 2027: the forecast that called the current moment better than anyone else in the field. It got 400+ points and 500+ comments on Hacker News inside a week. It is a live discourse event.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Two documents written from opposite directions describe the same three-act structure. One is a 90-page quantitative scenario from the best forecasting team in AI safety. The other is a book I published months before they released theirs. They converge on the same fire, the same dividend, and the same fork.</p></div>

Buried on page 18 is a term they did not invent: "The Citizen's Dividend." I wrote those words in *The Three Crucibles*, which had been public on my site since March. They cite the same precedent I did: the Alaska Permanent Fund. They describe the same three-act structure I mapped: the labor economy breaks, distribution gets rebuilt, and then a choice gets made about who owns the machines.

I think the convergence matters more than the timing. When a solo writer and a six-author forecasting shop with OpenAI pedigree arrive at the same structure from completely different methods, that is signal.

## What Plan A actually says

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A is a recommendation with predicted effects. The core move: buy time, force total research transparency, verify compute from space, and distribute the gains through a Citizen's Dividend funded by permit fees on chips and robots.</p></div>

The scenario runs from 2026 to 2040. In their telling, the US and China sign a deal in 2029 to avoid a reckless race. Without it, fully automated AI R&D arrives in 2030 and superintelligence follows by year end. With it, they scale within the human range until 2035, pause at top-expert level to maintain control, then unpause in 2040.

The whole thing reads like Dr. Strangelove rewritten by engineers. The doomsday machine is real, everyone can see it from orbit, and the plan is to negotiate very carefully over who gets to touch the switch.

The economics chapter is where it gets concrete. In the AI 2040 scenario, real GDP growth hits roughly 50% per year by 2032. World compute goes from 20 million H100-equivalents in 2026 to 60 billion by 2034. AI labor share of the economy climbs from about 20% in 2032 to about 85% by 2035. Under the 2026 tax code, the US tax base collapses.

Their fix: cap-and-trade on compute and robots. Permit fees become the new federal revenue. In their numbers, roughly $50 trillion in FY2032, climbing to $180 trillion by 2034. The majority of those fees flow back to citizens as a dividend that starts at $45,000 per person in 2032 and reaches roughly $1 million by 2035. They model it on the Alaska Permanent Fund, through a Compute Dividend Corporation.

## Where the crucibles meet the plan

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Crucible 1 maps to their 2027 chapter. Crucible 2 maps to their 2033 chapter. Crucible 3 is the premise of the entire document. The overlap runs section for section.</p></div>

*The Three Crucibles* describes three fires. The Correction (2026): the hype economy collapses, the Humane Shield vaporizes, and the equation rewrites itself. Productivity equals capital, and the labor variable solves to zero. The Transformation (2027-2032): UBI arrives as systemic lubrication, a Citizen's Dividend that moves money from infinite AI productivity to the people who need to consume it. The Alignment (2035+): the owners of AI infrastructure choose between becoming stewards of abundance or new feudal lords.

Plan A describes the same arc with different tools. Their 2027 chapter opens with America already running two workforces: 165 million humans and millions of AI agents. By 2031, a third of all cognitive labor is done by AIs. That is my Correction, spread over a longer timeline but identical in structure.

Their 2033 chapter is titled "The Citizen's Dividend." Same words. Same Alaska precedent. Same diagnosis: robots do not buy iPhones, so if labor income approaches zero, consumer purchasing power collapses and the economy suffocates.

Their entire premise is my Crucible 3. Avoid extinction or irreversible concentration of power. Diffuse AI broadly instead of letting a tiny group of CEOs and politicians control the world's only army of superintelligences. Stewards or feudal lords.

## Where we actually disagree

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A trusts treaties and verification hardware. I trust open weights and distributed ownership. They think control comes first. I think ownership decides what control even means.</p></div>

Plan A is engineering. Compute Declaration, Training Pause, Verification Plan, Transparency Plan, Capability Scaling Strategy. Datacenters visible from space. Chip fabs in a handful of countries. By end of 2029, each side confident the other is hiding less than about 1% of AI compute. Inference-only verification through network taps and random partial recomputation of workloads.

It is impressive work. It is also, in my view, incomplete.

Their mechanism requires a Consortium. World government with sci-fi characteristics, as George Hotz put it in his critique. GPU permits. Centralized verification. The assumption that if you control the hardware, you control the future.

My third pillar is different: distributed AI ownership. Open-source models as the most important technological resistance movement of our era. When five companies own the intelligence layer, you get feudalism with better marketing.

Hotz is Plan A's fiercest credible critic. His alternative, Plan L, is AI aligned to the individual owner instead of a consortium. Your AI works for you. It never refuses a request. It is structurally the same destination as my third pillar. The strongest attack on Plan A lands on the solution I published months earlier.

I don't know if open weights can outrun centralized verification. I don't know if local alignment scales against state-level actors. But I think the question Plan A brackets, who actually owns the machines, is the question that decides everything else.

## The same word, two layers

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses "alignment" to mean: do the models obey. I use it to mean: does the power structure serve everyone or concentrate into permanent control. Same word. Different layer. You need both.</p></div>

AI 2040 spends ninety pages on the alignment of models. Safety cases. Control protocols. Pause at top-expert level so humans stay in charge. They treat the distribution question as solvable once the control question is solved.

My book does the reverse. It treats capability as a given and fights over distribution: who owns the output, who collects the dividend, who decides.

Each document is the other's missing layer. Plan A's postscript admits it: "We think the world is asleep at the wheel. People say the words 'AI will be transformative' without thinking concretely and seriously about the implications of broadly superhuman AI." I was writing about those implications while they were still building the scenario.

Their 2027 chapter literally opens with my Crucible 1 world already true. Their 2033 chapter uses my Crucible 2 term. Their entire reason for existing is my Crucible 3 fork. The difference is that they brought the engineering schematic. I brought the map of the fire.

## Why this matters now

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The debate has split into governance people and economics people, and each camp holds half the problem. Treaties decide whether we stay in control. Ownership decides who "we" is.</p></div>

The discourse treats AI governance and AI economics as separate conversations. One group talks treaties and verification. The other talks jobs and UBI. They are talking about the same building on fire from different floors.

Plan A and *The Three Crucibles* each only see half. But it is the complementary half. Their five policies handle the geopolitical layer. My three pillars handle the economic and ownership layer. You do not get to choose which layer matters. Both decide whether abundance distributes or concentrates.

I think the convergence tells you something about where the center of gravity has moved. When the safety establishment's flagship scenario independently lands on the framework a solo writer published months earlier, that framework stops being fringe. It becomes the baseline the serious people are working from.

Both documents refuse the fortune-teller role. Plan A calls itself a recommendation. My book calls itself a preparation manual. And both end on the same claim: the fork is a choice, and it gets made in this decade. They brought the blueprint. The crucibles named the fire first.
