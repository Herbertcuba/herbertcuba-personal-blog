In 1976, with production at Prudhoe Bay, the large oil field on Alaska's North Slope, about to turn a finite resource into state revenue, Alaska amended its constitution to reserve part of that revenue before it entered normal spending. The Alaska Permanent Fund paid its first resident dividend in 1982. It made public ownership concrete while the money was still politically negotiable.

That design reappears in [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), a 2026 policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, the forecasting group whose team overlaps with the authors of the 2025 rapid-AI scenario *AI 2027*. Plan A is a recommendation, not its authors' best-guess forecast. It proposes charging for permits to build robots and AI computing capacity, meaning the specialized chips, data centers, electricity, and supporting infrastructure used to train and run advanced AI systems. Most of those fees would finance a Citizen's Dividend, a public payment modeled on Alaska.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my book about three stages of AI-driven economic change, reaches the same term and precedent. Its PDF was public in my site's repository on March 23, 2026; the edition used here is dated June 24; and a public post linked to Plan A on July 9. That establishes sequence, not private origin or influence.

I think the comparison is valuable because it exposes three different control problems. Verification means using inspectable evidence such as chip sales, construction, and power use to determine whether a state is secretly building prohibited AI capability. Ownership decides who may use the systems and receive their returns. A company then faces a more immediate question: can it prove that it could hand a working process to another supplier, or is its right to leave merely contractual language?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works share a specific economic response to AI-driven automation, but their public chronology proves neither influence nor prediction. The useful comparison is between the different controls they make visible.</p></div>

## The closest match sits inside the Void

The [June 24 PDF edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment as its three crucibles. Transformation is a chapter within the Void, not the second crucible's canonical name. The Correction describes the market pressure that forces companies into rapid automation. Plan A begins with an international response to dangerous AI development, so it has no direct match for that opening mechanism.

The overlap appears in the Void, when machines create more value but displaced jobs do not return. Its Transformation chapter argues that falling labor income eventually weakens consumer demand: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” It proposes a [Citizen's Dividend](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36) as a direct transfer from AI infrastructure to the people who must still buy what the automated economy produces.

In Plan A's scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, work based on analysis, communication, research, or judgment, by mid-2031. As employment falls, labor-tax revenue weakens. The government sells permits to build robots and computing capacity, then directs most fees to a dividend projected at [$45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18). My book instead proposes taxing each unit of machine work comparable to human labor. The diagnosis and destination converge; the funding mechanisms do not.

The Alignment asks whether AI infrastructure becomes broadly owned or concentrated among a few private actors. Plan A gives each US citizen a claim on permit revenue through a Compute Dividend Corporation, yet its main machinery is treaty inspection and safety rather than distributed ownership of the systems themselves. I think this is a meaningful thematic cross-check, not validation of the book's dates or every part of its argument.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction has no direct Plan A equivalent. The strongest match lies in the Void's income-and-demand problem, while the Alignment reveals a remaining disagreement over who controls AI infrastructure.</p></div>

## Plan A can inspect capacity without settling ownership

Plan A tries to slow a takeoff, a feedback loop in which AI automates research and accelerates its own improvement. In its scenario, a 2029 US-China agreement prevents a route to superintelligence by the end of 2030, meaning systems that exceed humans across almost all intellectual work. Development continues within the human range, pauses at the level of leading human experts in 2035, then resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. Those pauses work only if each side can see what the other is building.

The proposal therefore requires declarations of major chip and data-center transactions, reciprocal inspections, and almost entirely public AI research. Its covert-project appendix stress-tests the system with computing power equal to 500,000 high-end AI chips hidden in tunnels beside the planned Medog Hydropower Station in Tibet, where construction and hydroelectric power could conceal the facility. About 200 people know in this scenario. Like *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film whose virtual world still depends on physical machinery, advanced AI leaves material traces in chips, power lines, cooling systems, buildings, and supply chains.

I think those traces make inspection plausible, but inspection cannot answer the Alignment's ownership question. Finding a machine does not determine who may use it, collect its output, or stop its operator. The book answers with open infrastructure, open models, and legal limits on private monopoly. I don't know whether that approach or Plan A's regulated network would better survive sustained geopolitical pressure, and Plan A's authors do not claim certainty either.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses physical evidence to make hidden national capacity harder to conceal. The Three Crucibles focuses on a different risk: visible, lawful systems can still concentrate use, returns, and decision power.</p></div>

## The same control problem reaches the contract desk

The scale changes inside a company, but the logic carries. State inspection tests whether hidden national capacity exists. A buyer's handover test asks whether dependence on a supplier has made departure impractical. Both replace assurance with evidence, while ownership determines whose interests the system ultimately serves.

The book makes the pressure concrete through a fictional insurance example. One company keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; an AI-powered rival cuts staff by 40 percent, reduces premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months ([printed p. 20](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21)). That speed rewards adoption before anyone tests whether the resulting workflow can be transferred.

My take is that renewal should depend on exit proof: a supplier-funded, 30-day handover of one workflow and one usable data extract to one replacement team. The chief information officer should own the decision. The supplier must provide the data history, versioned settings and model instructions, permissions, connected-tool details, and operating and recovery guidance. A replacement may change the model, instructions, information-retrieval method, or workflow, but it must reproduce the agreed business result on a buyer-owned evaluation set, a sample of accepted historical cases, within fixed limits for errors, time, and cost.

Missing access, unusable data, absent operating material, or a result outside those limits should fail the test and block automatic renewal. The supplier then funds correction and transition; a second failure lets the buyer leave without a termination fee. [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's main AI law, has applied since August 2, 2026, and requires disclosure in certain AI interactions plus software-readable markings for some AI-generated content. Those markings help software detect that content was generated or altered by AI. They reveal use, but they cannot prove that another team can keep the business process running.

I think that is the failure waiting beyond transparency. An institution can inspect capacity and negotiate a share of the gains, yet still lose practical bargaining power if no one has tested the path out. The right to leave becomes real only when a replacement has demonstrated it before renewal.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A funded handover turns supplier exit from a promise into a tested capability. If another team cannot reproduce one agreed business result within known limits, compliant disclosure has not prevented dependence.</p></div>
