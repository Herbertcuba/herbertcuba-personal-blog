Forecasts turn strategic debate into a fight over dates. Much of the argument about advanced AI now turns on when systems might automate the research needed to improve themselves. I think the harder question is what starts changing before any such forecast can be tested.

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 2026 policy recommendation told through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit focused on advanced-AI futures. Its authors are explicit that Plan A is not their best guess about what will happen. The group had previously published [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/) on April 3, 2025, a best-guess scenario about a race toward increasingly capable AI; four of its five credited authors appear among Plan A's six named contributors.

[The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my 2026 book about the economic passage from AI hype to automated production and, eventually, a fight over ownership. Its page and PDF download were public on March 23, 2026. The current PDF edition is dated June 24, and AI 2040 was publicly available by July 9. That sequence establishes chronology, but it does not establish influence, independent development, or predictive success.

What it does give us is a useful comparison. AI 2040 follows the clock of capability: how fast AI improves, how states could slow it, and how anyone could verify the pause. The Three Crucibles follows the clock of social settlement: what happens to jobs, tax revenue, demand, and ownership as machine-produced value grows. The clocks meet, but they do not move at the same speed.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI 2040 is conceptual corroboration, not observed proof. Read beside The Three Crucibles, it shows that AI capability and the institutions that distribute its gains are advancing on different timelines.</p>

## The capability clock can be slowed

Plan A begins with takeoff, the proposed feedback loop in which AI automates AI research and accelerates the creation of better AI. In its scenario, the United States and China make a deal in 2029, avoid a rapid route to superintelligence in 2030, scale within the range of top human experts until 2035, pause there, and resume in 2040. The report defines superintelligence as AI that is significantly better than the best humans at everything while also working faster and more cheaply.

The mechanism is visibility. Major datacenter owners and chip suppliers declare large purchases and sales, foreign inspectors check facilities, and monitoring devices sample workloads. Existing models may still be used for inference, meaning the model applies what it has learned, while new training is paused. AI research later becomes broadly transparent even as private use of existing models remains private. Compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI, becomes something states try to count in the way arms-control agreements count missiles or nuclear material.

Plan A also treats hidden development as a first-order problem. Its covert-project branches ask whether a state could conceal enough chips, electricity, and people to train a leading system outside the agreement. I think this is the report's strongest contribution: it turns restraint from a promise into a problem of measurement, inspection, and enforcement.

Yet verification answers only whether declared limits are being followed. It does not tell citizens who benefits from the systems that remain legal. I don't know whether the proposed inspection regime could survive a severe geopolitical crisis, but even perfect compliance would leave the economic question open.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A tries to govern AI capability by making chips, datacenters, training, and violations visible. That could slow a dangerous race, but visibility alone does not distribute the value produced inside the verified system.</p>
</div>

## The settlement clock moves earlier

The [current PDF of The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) names its stages The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. The book's landing page calls the middle Crucible The Transformation, while the PDF places “The Transformation” as a chapter inside The Void; I use the PDF's three-part sequence here.

The Correction is a market and financial reset. In the book's mechanism, the collapse of AI hype destroys the “Humane Shield,” its name for the informal willingness to protect jobs even when automation would be more efficient. Cost pressure then turns a technical option into an economic requirement. The Void follows when AI starts producing real value without restoring the displaced jobs, weakening both labor income and consumer demand. The Alignment is the later political fork: who owns the productive machines, and who receives what they produce?

AI 2040 reaches much of the same economic structure from a different route. In its conditional scenario, AI performs one third of cognitive labor, work based mainly on reasoning, analysis, and communication, by mid-2031. It models roughly 50 percent inflation-adjusted economic growth in 2032, but warns that the tax base, the income and activity government can tax, shrinks as employment-linked revenue disappears and companies deduct heavy investment in datacenters, factories, and robots.

Plan A responds with paid, tradable legal permissions to build robot or computing capacity. These permits are sold to the highest bidder under a cap-and-trade system. The scenario projects $50 trillion in US permit revenue in fiscal 2032 and $180 trillion in 2034, then directs most American permit fees to a Citizen's Dividend for adults. Its projected payment rises from $45,000 per person in 2032 to roughly $1 million in 2035, measured in 2025 dollars. A related Compute Dividend Corporation gives every US citizen one share in an entity that receives a fixed portion of permit revenue and divides its profits equally.

My book also proposes a Citizen's Dividend, alongside an AI labor levy tied to the tax revenue a displaced human role would have generated. It treats that payment as a direct transfer from the productivity of AI infrastructure, then makes distributed ownership of the infrastructure a separate pillar. That distinction matters because a dividend can spread income without changing who owns the machinery that creates it.

I think the shared phrase matters less than the shared mechanism. Common wording proves neither origin nor influence, and Plan A's enormous figures remain scenario projections. The stronger signal is that both maps encounter the same circulation problem: output can rise while the fiscal and consumer systems built around human wages weaken.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The economic convergence is specific: weaker wage-based revenue creates pressure for a new public claim on machine output. AI 2040 funds that claim through paid capacity permits, while The Three Crucibles adds an AI labor levy and treats ownership itself as the deeper issue.</p>

## The dangerous gap is between the clocks

The deepest disagreement concerns the unit of control. Plan A concentrates on technical loss of control, the risk that a powerful AI will not reliably remain under human direction even if it is actively trying to cause catastrophe. It proposes safety testing, inspection, transparent research, and an international boundary around further training. The Three Crucibles largely brackets that technical problem and instead argues for distributed ownership, with individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities holding AI infrastructure, plus a public income that scales with automated output.

Neither lens can carry the transition alone. Distributed ownership does not make an unsafe system controllable. Inspection does not stop a safe system from concentrating income and political leverage. Plan A recognizes distribution through its dividend corporation, but that claim is created by a centralized permit regime. The Three Crucibles pushes ownership further outward, but offers less detail on how rival states could verify a shared technical limit.

My take is that policy should follow the clock that moves first: the economic settlement. We should watch whether wage-linked tax revenue falls as machine output rises, whether citizens receive an enforceable claim on that output rather than a temporary payment, and whether verification covers the computing capacity that could move AI beyond agreed limits. These are observable institutional tests; none requires us to pretend that a 2031 or 2040 outcome has already occurred.

I think the comparison will lose force if labor income and public revenue remain resilient through broad automation, or if concentrated ownership produces durable abundance without leaving citizens dependent on owners who can revoke access or set the terms. It will gain force if output accelerates while wages, tax receipts, and public leverage lag behind. The point of a framework is to expose that gap early enough to act on it.

When machine output scales faster than the public claim on it, the transition is already off course, whatever year superintelligence arrives.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Technical verification and distributed economic power solve different failure modes. The decisive task is to build both before machine output outruns the wage-based institutions that still give citizens income, revenue, and leverage.</p>
</div>
