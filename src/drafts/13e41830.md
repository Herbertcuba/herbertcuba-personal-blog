The headline question in *AI 2040* is when AI systems might move far beyond the best human experts, a threshold the report calls **superintelligence**. I think its more consequential warning appears earlier, in a sentence about public finance: “Without reform, the tax base will collapse as a fraction of GDP.” GDP measures the value of goods and services an economy produces; the tax base is the income and activity a government can tax. The report imagines a society becoming dramatically more productive while losing the revenue system that pays for the state.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) is a 2026 policy scenario published by the AI Futures Project, a nonprofit that researches how advanced artificial intelligence could develop and how societies might respond. Its link was public by July 9, 2026. The document describes Plan A chiefly as a recommendation, not the future its authors consider most likely. Four contributors had also written [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the project's scenario from April 2025 about states racing toward increasingly capable AI; they represent four of that work's five credited authors.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my 2026 account of how AI could reshape the economy and political power, became available on cubagarcia.com on March 23, 2026; the current PDF edition dates from June 24. Its stages are **The Correction**, when production sheds human labor; **The Void**, when work-tied income and identity erode; and **The Alignment**, when ownership decides whether abundance spreads or concentrates. There is one naming inconsistency: the book page labels the second stage **The Transformation**, whereas the PDF places a chapter with that name inside The Void. The labels need cleanup, but the sequence is clear.

Those dates establish chronology and nothing more. They do not prove that the works developed independently, that one influenced the other, or that my forecast has been confirmed. The useful result is that both maps reach a fiscal break and a **Citizen's Dividend**, a public payment drawn from automated wealth, then separate over where lasting control should live.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The earlier public date of The Three Crucibles establishes timing, not foresight. The overlap raises a governance problem with two halves: restraining powerful AI systems and giving the public a claim on the wealth they produce.</p></div>

## Inspection and ownership govern different risks

Plan A supplies the technical-control layer that my book leaves underdeveloped. It addresses **technical loss of control**, the risk that highly capable AI systems no longer reliably follow human direction. Under its verification system, major datacenter operators and chip suppliers disclose large hardware holdings and transactions. Rival inspectors check facilities, while monitoring equipment samples workloads to distinguish **inference**, running an existing approved model, from new training. Research on the newest and most capable systems would also be widely visible.

Verification therefore concerns operational power: can institutions see a major training run, identify undeclared hardware, and restrain development? *The Three Crucibles* concerns distributive power instead: who can use the infrastructure, who can be excluded, and who receives the return? Its proposal for distributed ownership would let individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities run AI models on infrastructure they own rather than depend entirely on a few companies.

I don't know whether an inspection agreement would survive the moment one major power became convinced that another was cheating. I also do not assume that locally owned systems would remain safe simply because control is spread more widely. I think the two approaches belong in one design because each fails beyond the boundary the other covers. That difference matters well before superintelligence, because income and public revenue can shift even while the machines remain under human direction.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification can reveal and restrain dangerous AI development, while ownership determines access, dependence, and economic returns. A system may pass one test and still fail the other.</p></div>

## The fiscal break arrives before the handover

[Plan A's timeline](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) turns on a proposed United States-China agreement in 2029. The bargain postpones an automated-research path that the scenario says could have reached superintelligence during 2030. Development then stays within human capability until 2035, when the leading systems equal or surpass top human experts across the fields in the scenario. The **Consortium**, an international body created by the agreement, pauses at that level because it believes human institutions can still control it. In 2040, the limit is lifted and the scenario deliberately proceeds to superintelligence.

Yet the economic rupture comes before that handover. By the middle of 2031, AI accounts for one third of all **cognitive labor** in the scenario, meaning work whose main inputs are reasoning, language, and professional judgment. Plan A then models roughly 50 percent growth in inflation-adjusted economic output in 2032 while warning that the taxable base shrinks relative to the economy. Personal-income and payroll revenue fall with employment, while companies can deduct large investments in datacenters, factories, and robots from taxable profit.

The Correction and The Void describe the same mechanism from the household side. When machine output grows faster than wages, people lose both purchasing power and the employment link through which much public revenue is collected. The exact dates may fail, but I think the shared diagnosis is stronger than the familiar claim that automation merely destroys some jobs and creates others. It says the feedback loop between production, income, and public capacity may have to be rebuilt.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both works locate an institutional crisis in the link between work and money. Output can surge while wages, consumer purchasing power, and employment-based tax revenue lose their role in circulating that wealth.</p></div>

## One dividend, two theories of power

Both works call their answer a **Citizen's Dividend**. The shared phrase is not evidence that either originated the term. What deserves attention is the surrounding design: each links automated wealth to a public payment and invokes the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/who-we-are/history-of-the-alaska-permanent-fund/). In 1976, voters changed the state constitution so that a portion of mineral income would be saved; the first resident dividend was paid in 1982. Resource income had become an enduring public claim.

Inside the scenario, Plan A creates permits for robots and for **compute**, the processing power of computer hardware used to train and operate AI systems. In its modeled American version, most permit fees eventually fund payments to all American adults. Measured in real 2025 dollars, the scenario starts the annual amount at $45,000 for each recipient in 2032 and raises it to roughly $1 million by 2035. It also assigns every US citizen an equal share of a Compute Dividend Corporation that receives part of the permit revenue. These are conditional figures, not observed outcomes.

*The Three Crucibles* places the claim elsewhere. It proposes a levy on machine-executed work, calibrated to the tax contribution of the human role being displaced, with the proceeds supporting the Citizen's Dividend. The tax is one pillar; wider ownership of AI infrastructure is another.

My take is that a payment and an ownership right solve related but different problems. A permit-funded dividend can preserve demand while leaving ownership of productive assets concentrated. Wider ownership can spread bargaining power and future returns, but it cannot by itself guarantee a universal income. The choice of funding is already a choice about power.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>In its US model, Plan A gives citizens a share of regulated permit income; The Three Crucibles would add a public claim on machine work and wider ownership of productive assets. Both distribute wealth, but the book also puts ownership of the underlying infrastructure on the table.</p></div>

## Convergence becomes useful when it can fail

Calling Plan A confirmation of my book would be tempting, and I think it would be wrong. No observation from 2031 or 2040 exists in 2026, and Plan A's authors do not present their document as a forecast of what will happen. Its value here is as an external stress test: a detailed safety proposal reaches the same distribution problem, while showing what *The Three Crucibles* leaves underdeveloped, namely a practical system for auditing and slowing AI capabilities.

The economic claim should now face real evidence. It gains support if AI-intensive sectors raise output while labor income and employment-based tax receipts weaken relative to that output. It loses support if wages, purchasing power, and public revenue remain resilient as adoption deepens. The ownership claim should also be revisable. If permit-funded dividends create broad household income while citizens retain choices among providers and leverage over prices without wider asset ownership, then my stronger case for shared ownership needs to change.

Plan A faces its own practical standard. Governments must be able to verify major AI training, detect meaningful violations, and stop systems at the boundary they claim to enforce. Technical control without a broad public claim on machine output leaves the social contract unstable. Wider ownership cannot protect people from systems that human institutions cannot restrain. The real handover begins when institutions can answer both problems at once.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The overlap between the two works is a reason to measure the fiscal and ownership shifts, not to declare a forecast victory. A credible AI settlement must make technical restraint enforceable and machine-produced wealth broadly claimable.</p></div>
