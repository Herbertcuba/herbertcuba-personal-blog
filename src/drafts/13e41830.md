I tried to read [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) as a distant scenario about governments and superintelligence. By page 18, I was back inside a damaged-car claim from [The Three Crucibles](/books/the-three-crucibles/).

Plan A calls its 2033 chapter "The Citizen's Dividend." My book uses the same name and points to the same Alaska oil-dividend precedent. The book has been public on my site since March 23. AI 2040 appeared in July.

The term existed before both works. I think independent arrival is the useful signal. Four of the five authors behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/) returned for this 90-page scenario. They approach AI through frontier labs and national policy. I approached it through a normal company processing an insurance claim.

## One claim contains the labor-tax problem

The two maps first meet inside a claims department.

The Three Crucibles uses a thought experiment. Company A keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. Company B deploys an imperfect agentic system, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. These are book-scenario numbers.

The First Crucible, the Correction, is the pressure that forces Company A to respond. The Second Crucible, the Void, opens when machine output rises while wages and consumer demand weaken. "Robots do not buy iPhones." The Third Crucible, the Alignment, asks who owns the machines and who receives what they produce.

Plan A reaches the same pressure through federal revenue. Its 2032 chapter says personal income and payroll taxes brought in roughly ten times more revenue than corporate taxes in 2026. When Company B removes salaries from the claims department, taxes tied to those salaries leave too. The report says the tax base would collapse as a share of GDP without reform.

I think this overlap survives a slower AI timeline. Company B needs neither superintelligence nor a secret government project. It needs an agent that handles enough claims at a lower cost. Once a competitor proves that, the Correction starts through price pressure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Three Crucibles begins with automation inside a company. AI 2040 runs the same effect through the tax system. The shared problem appears as soon as machine work removes enough wages from one department.</p></div>

## The same dividend comes through a different bill

Plan A turns Company B's missing payroll into permit revenue on pages 17 and 18.

Plan A starts with a US-China deal in 2029. Takeoff is the period when AI speeds up its own research, letting capability improve much faster than normal software. The deal blocks a 2030 jump to fully automated AI R&D. Countries scale within the human range until a 2035 pause at top-expert AI, then move toward superintelligence in 2040. The authors call this a policy recommendation carried by a scenario.

Inside that scenario, controlled growth is still huge. AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031. Real GDP grows by about 50 percent in 2032. Governments cap advanced compute and robot production, then sell permits. The US collects a projected $50 trillion in permit fees in 2032.

A share goes into a Compute Dividend Corporation, with one share for every US citizen. The projected dividend starts at $45,000 per person in 2032 and reaches about $1 million in 2035. The authors also say their economic model is uncertain.

Alaska established its Permanent Fund in 1976 and paid the first dividend in 1982. Both maps reuse that basic idea: treat a scarce productive resource as shared wealth. My book collects through an AI labor or output tax after an agent does useful work. Plan A charges for scarce chips and robots before the work happens.

I think the dollar amounts will age faster than the structure. Company B still needs a way to return part of its machine output to the people whose wages once carried demand and public revenue. The collection point can change. The ownership claim has to exist.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A charges for compute and robot capacity, then pays citizens from the permit fees. The Three Crucibles taxes AI work after production. Their funding mechanisms differ, while the dividend does the same economic job.</p></div>

## Verification fails where the cluster disappears

The maps separate when Plan A has to prove that every dangerous machine is visible.

Plan A requires major datacenter owners and suppliers, including chip fabs, to declare large purchases and sales. Governments inspect infrastructure and verify the reports. Total research transparency reveals frontier training methods. The verification bet is strong because advanced chips come from few fabs and large datacenters draw visible power.

The report then stress-tests that bet in its covert-project appendix. In one branch, China diverts about 500,000 smuggled H100-equivalents into a hidden project. An H100-equivalent compares compute to one Nvidia H100 GPU. The datacenter sits in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station. The authors estimate that around 200 people could know about it, and they put its chance of staying hidden long enough to win below 10 percent in their scenario.

That low probability still exposes the gap. A registry can describe declared compute. The hidden cluster lives outside the record by design.

Company B has a smaller version of the same problem. It may log every damaged-car decision while depending on a provider whose future terms remain outside its control. In *Alien*, the Nostromo crew discovers what Ash is doing, yet Weyland-Yutani still owns the mission and has already decided whose interests count. Seeing the system gives the crew evidence. Their options remain narrow.

The Third Crucible starts from ownership instead. Give more people a durable claim on machine output, then keep the systems distributed enough to preserve choice. I don't know which layer breaks first. Ownership still needs governance because someone must keep the shareholder registry honest. I think verification and ownership cover different failure modes, with friction left between them.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses declarations and inspections to make compute visible. Its own covert scenario shows how profitable secrecy can route around that system. Distributed ownership gives people an exit, though the ownership registry still needs enforcement.</p></div>

## The fork lands in a StateGraph

A junior engineer can test this argument on Company B's next damaged-car claim.

Suppose the claim agent uses ReAct. ReAct is a loop where the model chooses an action, calls a tool, reads the result, and chooses its next action. For this claim, the tools might read the policy and request a repair estimate. A large payment then moves to human review.

LangGraph is a Python library for building that loop as an explicit graph. A StateGraph connects Python functions as nodes and decides which node runs next. Each node reads or updates shared state. TypedDict tells Python type checkers which fields that state should contain, although it does not validate them at runtime.

```python
from typing import TypedDict
from langgraph.graph import StateGraph

class ClaimState(TypedDict):
    claim_id: str
    repair_estimate: float
    model_id: str
    needs_human_review: bool

graph = StateGraph(ClaimState)
graph.add_node("estimate", estimate_damage)
graph.add_node("review", route_for_review)
graph.add_edge("estimate", "review")
```

Here, `estimate_damage` and `route_for_review` are normal Python functions. The first adds the repair cost. The second checks `needs_human_review` before a large payment can proceed.

An eval is a repeatable test of agent behavior. Company B can save old claims with allowed outcomes, then run them through a fallback model during continuous integration. If the fallback changes a covered repair into a denial, the eval fails before production.

This is where [reversibility becomes an engineering test](/posts/reversibility-is-the-new-correctness/). During the vendor review, require an export format for `ClaimState` and the tool trace. Ask who attests that the production model matches the model your team evaluated. In the exit clause, assign rights to the prompts and eval cases, then state whether any fine-tune can transfer.

I think junior AI engineers should treat that exit path as production work. Procurement can promise portability. The graph has to replay the claim without the primary provider.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>ReAct defines the agent loop, while LangGraph makes its steps and shared state explicit. Portable state plus a tested fallback gives Company B a practical ownership claim: the ability to keep processing the same claim after a provider exit.</p></div>

AI 2040 leaves its hardest work to treaties. Company B leaves part of it to whoever defines `ClaimState`. The damaged-car claim is smaller, and the ownership problem inside it has already arrived.
