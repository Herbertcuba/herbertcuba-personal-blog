In 1976, Alaska made an unusual deal with itself. The oil at Prudhoe Bay belonged to everyone in the state, so a cut of every barrel went into a permanent fund. Since 1982, every Alaska resident has received a yearly check from it. Fifty years later, the fund is the most boring success story in American policy.

In July, the AI Futures Project published [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/): ninety pages of scenario modeling and policy engineering from the team that wrote [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/). Their proposal for the age of superintelligence: a fixed fraction of compute permit fees flows into a "Compute Dividend Corporation," owned one share per citizen, profits paid out as dividends. Their own words: "This is similar to the setup of the Alaska Permanent Fund, except at a much larger scale." Their 2033 chapter is titled "The Citizen's Dividend."

[The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) carries the same mechanism under the same name, public on my site since March.

The shared name is a footnote. Where their map and mine stop agreeing, that is where the actual decision lives.

## The deal

Plan A is the optimistic branch from the people who wrote the pessimistic one.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 is the AI 2027 team's positive scenario: a US-China deal, total research transparency, a controlled scale-up, a pause at top-expert-level AI in 2035. The scenario arithmetic compresses most of the labor transformation into three years.</p></div>

AI 2027 ended in a mad scramble to superintelligence, extinction or irreversible concentration of power. AI 2040 is their answer to the obvious follow-up: what should happen instead. Their footnote one concedes the point: "So far reality is tracking closer to AI 2027 than even we expected."

The scenario draws on direct OpenAI experience and conversations inside the frontier labs. The spine of it is a US-China agreement in 2029 to avoid a reckless race. In 2030, on the cusp of fully automated AI R&D, the leading US company grudgingly agrees to pause, and total research transparency becomes the price of the deal. Between 2030 and 2035, the world scales deliberately inside the human range. In 2035, everyone pauses at top-expert-level AI, the maximum they believe they can control. In 2040, aligned systems take the torch.

The arithmetic is striking even inside the scenario's own hedges. Real GDP growth hits [about 50% in 2032](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf). World compute goes from roughly 20 million H100-equivalents in 2026 to 60 billion by 2034. (An H100-equivalent is a standard unit for counting AI computing power; the hardware is not the point, the multiple is.) The AI share of the labor economy climbs from about 20% in 2032 to about 85% by 2035.

Run that last pair again. A three-year window in which paid human work goes from four-fifths of the economy to one-seventh. The plan is for civilization. The arithmetic is for a claims department.

## Where the maps agree

The overlap runs through the whole economic layer of the scenario.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The maps agree on the economics: the tax base collapses as labor is automated, the state ends up rationing compute and robot capacity, and permit fees flow back to citizens as a dividend. AI 2040 reaches this from alignment risk. The Three Crucibles reached it from the enterprise side.</p></div>

Start with the tax base. AI 2040 shows federal revenue collapsing as a fraction of GDP, because under the 2026 tax code, firms can expense or depreciate the machines that replaced the workers until taxable income reaches near zero. Taxes on labor income, roughly ten times larger than corporate taxes today, evaporate as headcount shrinks. In my book, the same collapse is the First Crucible: the Correction, the moment Productivity = Capital stops being a slogan and becomes quarterly reality. In that world, labor solves to zero, and an economy that taxes labor is an economy with no revenue.

Then the fix. Their mechanism is permits. Robot and compute production gets capped, permits are sold to the highest bidder, and the fees become federal revenue: roughly $50 trillion in FY2032, climbing toward [$180 trillion by 2034](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf). A fixed fraction is legally owned by the Compute Dividend Corporation, every citizen holds one share, and the dividend starts at $45,000 per person in 2032 and climbs to about $1M by 2035. In the book, the same step is the Second Crucible: UBI arrives, framed not as welfare but as systemic lubrication, because robots do not buy iPhones. An automated economy without a distribution channel seizes. The dividend is the channel.

Two different starting points, same map. They start from alignment risk and work outward to economics. I started from enterprise operating models and worked outward to economics. Both maps end at the same place: the old income circuit breaks, the state ends up owning the constraint, and the constraint gets rationed back to citizens as a dividend.

I think the convergence matters more than either document does on its own. When the people whose job is worrying about misaligned superintelligence independently rebuild the ownership structure you sketched from the boardroom side, that structure is probably carrying real weight.

## Where the maps disagree

The fork is the word "deal."

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 bets on verification: declared compute, inspected datacenters, tracked chips. Its own covert-project appendix shows a defecting state running a crash program with roughly 200 people in the know. The fork between the maps is governance versus ownership: police the machines, or settle who owns them.</p></div>

AI 2040's plan rests on verification. Compute gets declared. Datacenters get inspected, because training clusters are big enough to see from space and power-hungry enough to need conspicuous infrastructure. Chips get tracked from the handful of fabs that can make them. The whole architecture assumes the chokepoint holds.

Their own appendix stress-tests that assumption, and the stress test is sobering. In the scenario's covert-project branch, Chinese intelligence quietly diverts about 500,000 smuggled H100-equivalents, 17% of what smugglers bring into China in 2028. The stockpile sits in a warehouse until a covert datacenter is built in the tunnels next to the Medog hydropower dam. Their best guess is that a project like this could run with only around 200 humans aware of it: seven Politburo Standing Committee members, ten senior commanders, a hundred logistics coordinators, thirty researchers, seventy support staff. A superintelligence program with the headcount of a mid-sized startup.

Their map says the chokepoint of physical compute is verifiable enough to bet civilization on, provided inspections, buybacks, and satellite monitoring all hold. My map says any chokepoint that profitable gets routed around, and the durable question is who owns the machines. Governance is a bet on process, and process erodes under pressure. Ownership is a bet on what remains when the process fails.

I do not know which of us is right about the verification regime. I think the honest read of their covert-project math is that neither do they. They wrote ninety pages on Plan A and then an entire appendix on how it fails.

## The bridge in the turbine hall

There is a scene early in *2001: A Space Odyssey* where a monolith appears and the hominids around it change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Like the monolith in 2001, the machine rewires its users regardless of who polices it. The governance question is how to control the build. The ownership question is who has a claim on the output once labor stops being one.</p></div>

The movie never explains the mechanism. It shows the before and the after, and trusts you to understand that the tool rewired the user. In *2001*, the question was what its users become.

The covert datacenter in AI 2040 is the turbine hall version of that scene. A river in Tibet, a dam three times the power of Three Gorges, and underneath it a room full of machines doing the only work that matters anymore, while 200 people keep the secret. Every governance structure in the scenario exists to prevent that room from being built. Every economic structure in both documents exists to answer what happens when rooms like that run the world.

That is the question I think both maps are actually circling. The treaty machinery, the permits, the inspections: all of it surrounds a single variable. When labor no longer buys you a share of the output, ownership is the only remaining claim on it.

## The fork at the contract desk

Pull this down from treaty level to where it lands in practice, because the same fork shows up in ordinary procurement.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The governance-versus-ownership fork lands in procurement: what you ask vendors before signing, and what you can still reclaim after. Ask the treaty question and the exit question while you can still walk away from the table.</p></div>

In the book I used a small thought experiment. Two insurers, same liquidity squeeze. Company A tries to remain humane, keeps its five thousand claims adjusters, raises premiums to cover costs. Company B forces its agentic swarm into production, software agents doing the adjusting work end to end. Imperfect but functional. Cuts headcount forty percent, drops premiums twenty. Company B crushes Company A in six months. In recession, customers do not pay for humanity. They pay for value.

That is the 20% to 85% labor curve translated into an org chart. The macro numbers in AI 2040 read like science fiction until you put them in a claims department. Then they read like next year's renewal conversation.

The fork shows up at contract scale too. A model two years behind the frontier, running on hardware you own, beats a superintelligence you rent. Under the AI 2040 map, you ask your vendors which treaty obligations their training pipeline is subject to and who attests to it, because attestation claims will start appearing in procurement language, and somebody has to verify the verifier's marketing. Under the ownership map, you ask where the model weights live when the vendor's incentives turn, and what happens to your fine-tunes and eval suites on exit. I think both maps imply the same sentence, and it is the only one in this article I would forward to a board: the leverage only exists before you sign.

On August 2, 2026, the bulk of the EU AI Act starts applying. The obligations for general-purpose models have already been in force for a year, since August 2, 2025. Now the rest of the regime catches up. Provenance, accountability, and reversibility stop being conference words and become clause language. The map you choose decides what you put in those clauses.

---

The two documents tell one story from two directions. The lab side has now modeled, in public, the economics I wrote about from the operator side. The scenario arithmetic is theirs. The fork belongs to everyone reading it.

Fifty years ago, Alaska answered the ownership question before the money got big. The oil belonged to everyone, so everyone got a share, and the argument about distribution was settled before the stakes could corrupt it. The AI version of that conversation is starting now, in treaty language and procurement language at the same time. Whether it gets settled by governance or by ownership is the one variable I would watch.

The window is short. In their scenario, the whole labor transformation happens between one renewal cycle and the one after next. The machines get built either way. Who owns them is still open.
