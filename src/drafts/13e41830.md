I tried to read [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) as a distant forecast. By page 18, its authors had reached the same Citizen's Dividend I published in [The Three Crucibles](/books/the-three-crucibles/).

Four of the five authors behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/) returned for this 90-page scenario. They start with a US-China deal to slow a race toward superintelligence. I started with two insurance companies fighting over a claims department.

Plan A's 2033 chapter is called "The Citizen's Dividend." My book uses the same name and the same Alaska Permanent Fund model. It has been public on my site since March 23. AI 2040 appeared in July.

I think the date is useful because the two documents came from different work. Their team studied frontier labs and national security. I looked at what automation does to a company when a competitor cuts prices first.

<div class="article-tldr"><span class="article-tldr__label">Article TL;DR</span><p>AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles both expect paid work to shrink and a Citizen's Dividend to return part of AI output to people. Plan A relies on compute permits and international inspections. My book puts more weight on AI labor taxes and distributed ownership. A junior engineer will meet the same choice while building an agent that must survive a vendor exit.</p></div>

## Start with one damaged car

Company B's claims system keeps the comparison tied to work that exists today.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The insurer in The Three Crucibles shows the national problem in miniature. When agents remove salaries from a claims department, payroll tax falls with them. AI 2040 applies the same pressure to the whole economy.</p></div>

The book uses a thought experiment with two insurers. Company A keeps 5,000 claims adjusters and raises premiums to cover its costs. Company B puts an imperfect agentic system into production. It cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months.

In Plan A, that pressure reaches the national tax base. The scenario estimates that AI and robots perform about 20 percent of labor by economic value in 2032. The share reaches about 85 percent in 2035. Fewer salaries mean less personal income tax and payroll tax, which provided roughly ten times as much US federal revenue as corporate tax in 2026.

The book starts with the Correction, then enters the Void. Paid work loses value against capital, while output recovers before household income. The final Alignment asks who owns the machines once AI becomes common.

I think Company B is the useful unit of analysis. You can disagree with Plan A's takeoff speed and still see the tax problem inside one automated claims department.

## A dividend after the payroll shrinks

Both documents send money from machine production back to the people who need to buy what the machines produce.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A sells permits for scarce compute and robots, then gives citizens a share of the fees. The Three Crucibles taxes AI labor and calls the payment a Citizen's Dividend. They agree on the distribution problem while using different funding tools.</p></div>

Plan A requires permits for advanced chips and robots. A fixed part of the permit fees belongs to a Compute Dividend Corporation. Every US citizen owns one share. The authors describe it as "similar to the setup of the Alaska Permanent Fund, except at a much larger scale."

In their scenario, the annual payment starts at $45,000 per person in 2032 and rises to about $1 million in 2035. The authors also say their economics model is uncertain. I think the amount could be wrong while the mechanism still holds.

The Void in my book reaches the same mechanism through demand. "Robots do not buy iPhones." If wages approach zero, customers lose the money needed to buy the new abundance. The Citizen's Dividend keeps money moving through the economy.

Plan A and The Three Crucibles collect revenue at different points. Plan A charges for permits because compute and robot capacity are scarce. The book's AI labor tax follows the work after it happens, which ties the bill to business output but makes measurement harder.

## Inspections cannot give Company B an exit

Plan A can verify a datacenter while Company B remains trapped inside one vendor's API.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A tries to keep the race visible through compute declarations and inspections. The Three Crucibles focuses on who can use the intelligence and who receives its gains. Company B needs visibility and the right to keep operating.</p></div>

Plan A asks countries to declare compute and let inspectors into datacenters. Chip supply gets tracked from the small number of fabrication plants that can make advanced models possible. Total research transparency lets every major party see how frontier systems improve.

I think this can buy time. In one covert branch, Chinese intelligence diverts about 500,000 smuggled H100-equivalents, a rough measure of AI computing power, into tunnels next to the Medog hydropower station. The authors estimate that only around 200 people would need to know.

My book presses on a different weakness. Its first policy pillar is distributed AI ownership through open infrastructure, open models, and legal rules that prevent private control of the intelligence layer. It spends too little time on treaty inspections. AI 2040 spends too little time on what ownership means below the nation-state level.

I don't know which gap will cause more damage. Picture the crew in *2001: A Space Odyssey* counting every processor connected to HAL 9000. The inventory tells them what exists. Company B still needs a way to run claims when HAL changes the terms of service.

## The fork appears in a StateGraph

The governance debate becomes concrete when a junior engineer builds Company B's next claim flow.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Audit logs show what the claims agent did, but they do not make the workflow portable. Company B needs a tested second model and exportable state. Each decision also needs the exact model version.</p></div>

Suppose the claims agent uses ReAct. ReAct is a loop where the model chooses a step, calls a tool, reads the result, and repeats. For one damaged car, it might read the policy first. Then it can request a repair estimate before sending a large payment to a human reviewer.

LangGraph is a Python library for building that workflow as a graph. A StateGraph is the LangGraph object that connects each step and carries state between them. TypedDict is a Python type that defines the shape of that state. Company B might store a claim ID and repair estimate, along with the model version and a human-review flag.

These code details decide whether Company B can leave. The team should be able to run the same StateGraph against a fallback model. Its evals, which are repeatable tests of agent behavior, should produce comparable results. Tool traces should record the exact model used for each claim.

Before production, the engineer and procurement lead should ask four questions:

- Can the claims graph run against a second model?
- Can we export the state and tool traces in usable formats?
- Which prompts, fine-tunes, and model access survive the end of the contract?
- Can we rerun the same eval set after switching providers?

That is [reversibility as an engineering constraint](/posts/reversibility-is-the-new-correctness/). In a 5,000-person insurer, distributed ownership has a practical meaning: the claims operation can continue when a vendor changes price or access.

I think junior engineers should treat that exit path as a production feature. If Company B cannot survive a vendor exit, it has already chosen the concentrated branch described in the Third Crucible.
