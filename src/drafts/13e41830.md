Alaska voters created the mineral-revenue-backed Alaska Permanent Fund in 1976; dividends began in 1982. Fifty years later, two AI futures use the same precedent and label for a public payment funded by shared revenue: the Citizen’s Dividend.

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 2026 conditional policy scenario from the nonprofit AI Futures Project. Its authors recommend the policy package; the later dates and outcomes are predictions about what would follow if that package were implemented. Those projections are neither observed evidence nor the authors’ best guess about the actual future. Four of Plan A’s six authors also wrote [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), their April 3, 2025 scenario in which automated AI research drives a rapid race toward superintelligence, meaning AI systems significantly better than the best humans at everything while also faster and cheaper.

My book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), was on my site by March 23, 2026; the current PDF followed on June 24. AI 2040 added its PDF link on July 9. That establishes public artifact order, not influence, validation, or predictive success. I think the useful comparison lies elsewhere: the overlap identifies a possible economic break, while the disagreement exposes three controls on concentrated AI power.

## The maps meet at the economic break

The Three Crucibles opens with three stages: The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. The Correction marks labor’s loss of economic weight. The middle begins as The Void after the link between work and income weakens, then develops through The Transformation, the current landing-page label. The Alignment frames the final ownership fork.

In Plan A’s scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, work based on reasoning and judgment, by mid-2031. For 2032, the scenario projects about 50 percent growth in real gross domestic product, meaning inflation-adjusted output, while job losses weaken income- and payroll-tax revenue. Deductions for machine investment also reduce taxable corporate income. The report warns that its growth figure cannot be translated directly into 2025 purchasing power, so it does not establish weaker household demand.

Plan A responds with permits to build scarce robot or computing capacity, tradable permissions sold to the highest bidder. In its scenario, United States permit fees reach $50 trillion in fiscal 2032. A fictional Compute Dividend Corporation legally owns a fixed fraction of those fees, gives each United States citizen one share, and pays $45,000 per American adult in real 2025 dollars.

[The Three Crucibles PDF](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) also proposes a Citizen’s Dividend and an AI Labor Equivalence Tax, a levy on each human-equivalent unit of AI labor based on what a person in that role would have generated for public revenue. Its demand argument is blunt: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” I think the convergence lies in the diagnosis and the need for distribution. The funding differs: Plan A charges for scarce capacity through tradable permits; my book taxes human-equivalent AI work.

That claim should be able to lose. [Statistics Norway](https://www.ssb.no/en), Norway’s official statistics agency, publishes industry data on [AI use](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/13265), [employee compensation](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/09170), and [productivity](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/09174). A pre-registered 2032 test could use 2023–2025 as the baseline, then compare industries with the largest and smallest increases in AI use by pairing industries that had similar pre-AI productivity and labor-share trends. Labor share is employee compensation divided by value added, which is output after subtracting the intermediate goods and services used in production.

If fast adopters gain ten percentage points more productivity growth while their labor share remains within two points of baseline relative to the comparison group, that counts against my thesis. A relative fall above five percentage points supports it; the middle is inconclusive. Norway’s [tax and social-contribution data](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/10799) is available at sector rather than industry level, so it cannot prove an industry effect. Tax reform, oil shocks, reclassification, or a weak adoption gap would also cloud the result. I think a Nordic series that can defeat the framework is better than a decorative example.

<p>Both works reconnect automated production to citizens, but one uses permit fees and the other equivalent-labor taxation. Norway’s industry data offers a future test of the labor-income mechanism; its sector tax data supplies context rather than proof.</p>

## Verification and ownership solve different failures

Takeoff means AI automating AI research and accelerating its own improvement. Plan A’s conditional path reaches a United States–China agreement in 2029, pauses in 2035 at capabilities matching a top human expert, and resumes in 2040 toward superintelligence.

Its restraint system depends on compute, the capacity used to train and run AI. Major chip transactions would be declared, datacenters inspected, and AI research made almost entirely transparent, while private use of deployed systems would remain private. Developers would submit safety cases, detailed arguments that new systems will not cause irreversible catastrophe, for public and expert criticism.

Plan A stress-tests that design through a fictional covert Chinese project. Smuggled chips feed a datacenter in tunnels beside the unfinished Medog Hydropower Station, where legitimate construction provides cover and an underground tunnel carries away heat. The operation still needs equipment, electricity, and roughly 200 informed people. I think capacity at that scale leaves traces, but inspectors may find them too late.

Ownership addresses another dependency. Plan A’s corporation legally owns a fixed fraction of permit fees. The Three Crucibles proposes distributed ownership, with individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities holding AI infrastructure directly. One assigns a citizen payment stream; the other spreads legal claims on productive infrastructure.

The distinction sharpens at each map’s end. Plan A’s 2040 path gradually hands institutions and equipment to AI until humans could no longer shut all of those AI-controlled systems down. The Alignment asks whether those controlling abundance become “stewards of abundance” or “the new feudal lords.” I don’t know which governance path is right, and Plan A does not present its scenario as the authors’ best guess. My take is that verification and economic rights belong together: evidence about machinery cannot decide who benefits, while ownership cannot establish safety.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A makes dangerous computing capacity inspectable; The Three Crucibles spreads claims on productive infrastructure. Visibility reveals machinery, while legal rights determine who receives the gains.</p>

## Exit applies the same logic inside an organization

The scale changes at a supplier contract, but the concentration problem remains. A citizen’s economic right limits an infrastructure owner’s claim on automated income; a tested exit right limits a supplier’s power over an organization’s operations. I think both preserve bargaining power before dependence hardens.

My book illustrates the speed of that dependence through two fictional insurers. One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; the other uses coordinated AI agents, cuts headcount by 40 percent, reduces premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. Adoption can move faster than the ability to reverse it.

Reversibility capture occurs when a supplier retains something a workflow needs to run elsewhere, such as data, licences, interfaces, permissions, certification, or computing access. Exit proof is a live handover test agreed at signing and run before renewal with buyer-controlled cases. Require it for critical or potentially harmful workflows, or when any two risks are high: data sensitivity, supplier concentration, and replacement difficulty.

A disaster-recovery game day tests whether a service can be restored after failure. A portability test checks whether data and settings can move. Exit proof adds a buyer-owned replay of the actual workflow, an acceptance record, and a contract consequence. The leverage only exists before you sign.

The following decision table turns an exit right into a contractually testable handover rather than a supplier promise.

| Decision field | Contract rule |
| --- | --- |
| Scope | Test critical workflows and those with at least two high dependency risks. |
| Test pack | Freeze system versions, allowed tools and data, buyer-owned cases, expected results, error, time, and cost limits, plus the grader, meaning the approved scoring method. |
| Pass rule | Run three times. Every run meets safety, permission, audit, time, and cost limits; the median error stays within the agreed threshold. Allow one retry for a recorded external outage. A changed version, unavailable grader, or partial run is indeterminate and requires a full rerun. |
| Record and cost | Store versions, case ID, scores, logs, cost, result, and approver. The business owner holds the budget and accepts residual risk, meaning risk remaining after the agreed controls or risk responses have been applied. The buyer funds staff, independent review, and downtime; supplier participation has a fixed fee; the supplier funds repairs caused by missing access or documentation. “Case 118: output correct; audit log missing” fails. |
| Renewal | Failure triggers repair and one rerun, followed by portability support, repricing, or non-renewal unless the group responsible for risk approves a time-limited exception. |

A claims-triage test, for the workflow that routes insurance cases, could allow 30 days and 15 person-days for 200 cases. Median error stays within two percentage points of the current system; every run finishes within an hour and the fixed budget. These thresholds are proposals, not a standard.

The [European Union’s 2024 AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, is a risk-based law that generally applies from August 2, 2026. Rules for general-purpose AI models have applied since August 2, 2025. These models can perform a wide range of distinct tasks and serve as the basis for many AI systems. Some high-risk rules apply later. Required documents and logs can support exit proof, but they cannot demonstrate a handover.

My take is that Alaska turned mineral revenue into a citizen claim. AI systems may be built under many plans, but the rights to inspect them, share in their output, and leave their suppliers must be settled before dependence makes those choices for us.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Exit proof is a risk-tiered handover test fixed at signing and run before renewal with buyer-owned cases, acceptance rules, named owners, and allocated costs. Visibility, citizen economic rights, and practical exit must precede concentrated control.</p>
