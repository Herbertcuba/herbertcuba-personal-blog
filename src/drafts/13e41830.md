I tried to read [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) as a forecast. By page 18, it had rebuilt the economic engine I published in [The Three Crucibles](/books/the-three-crucibles/).

Its 2033 chapter is called "The Citizen's Dividend." Compute permit fees flow into a corporation where every US citizen owns one share. The authors compare it to the Alaska Permanent Fund.

My book uses the same name and Alaska model. It has been public on my site since March 23. AI 2040 appeared in July. I claim no ownership of the phrase. The signal is the convergence.

A six-author safety team started with superintelligence. I started with an insurer replacing claims staff with agents. Both reached the same problem: machines keep producing after wages shrink, while people still need income.

<div class="article-tldr"><span class="article-tldr__label">Article TL;DR</span><p>AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles reach the same economic structure: AI replaces paid work, labor taxes shrink, and a Citizen's Dividend returns part of the machine economy to people. Plan A relies on international verification. My framework puts more weight on distributed ownership. Junior AI engineers will meet this fork inside agent architecture and vendor contracts.</p></div>

## One claims system, scaled to a country

The same insurer makes both documents easier to read.

In The Three Crucibles, Company A keeps 5,000 claims adjusters and raises premiums. Company B deploys an agentic claims system, cuts headcount by 40%, lowers premiums by 20%, and wins within six months.

AI 2040 applies that pressure to a whole country. In its scenario, AI and robots perform about 20% of labor by economic value in 2032. The share reaches about 85% in 2035. Personal income and payroll taxes then fall because fewer people receive salaries.

The scenario sells permits for robots and compute. Compute is the chip capacity used to train and run AI models. A fixed share of the fees goes to a Compute Dividend Corporation. Every citizen gets one share.

These are scenario projections. The authors call Plan A a recommendation. You can reject their speed and still see the structure. Company B pays fewer salaries, so the state collects less payroll tax.

I think this is the strongest point of agreement. An automated economy needs a way to return some of its output to people. Robots do not buy iPhones.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Company B shows the national problem in miniature. As machines replace payroll, labor taxes shrink. Both documents fund a Citizen's Dividend from the productive system that replaced the wages.</p></div>

The First Crucible calls the labor shift the Correction. Plan A models it through a shrinking payroll tax base. The Second Crucible uses AI labor and output taxes, while Plan A uses permits. Both collect revenue from machine production.

The final match is the fork. Plan A wants broad benefits while humanity keeps control. The Third Crucible asks whether we become stewards of abundance or feudal lords.

I think the shared structure carries more weight than the phrase. One document begins inside frontier labs. The other begins in Company B's accounts. Both arrive at a broken payroll base and a public claim on machine output.

## Verification and ownership split the road

Company B can pass every audit while renting the intelligence that runs its claims department.

Model weights are the learned numbers that shape a model's behavior. If Company B rents those weights through an API, the vendor can change the model, price, or access terms.

Plan A puts its confidence in verification. Countries declare compute. Inspectors visit datacenters. Chips get tracked from the few factories that can make the most advanced versions. I think this could buy valuable time.

The report also tests that confidence. In one covert branch, Chinese intelligence diverts about 500,000 smuggled H100-equivalents into tunnels beside the Medog hydropower station. Around 200 people know about the project. The authors give it a low chance of reaching top-expert AI without detection before 2043.

I don't know which map will age better. I think Plan A underweights ownership, while my book leaves treaty verification too thin. Picture the control room in *2001: A Space Odyssey*. Counting every chip around HAL 9000 helps the crew understand the system. The power switch decides whether they control it.

Ownership needs rules too. A public registry must say who holds the shares and which rights come with them. A private vendor controlling that registry would recreate the concentration the dividend is meant to reduce.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification tracks chips, datacenters, and model development. Ownership decides who keeps access and receives the gains. A durable system needs both.</p></div>

## The fork arrives in your agent graph

Junior AI engineers will meet this policy debate inside ordinary code.

Company B's claims agent could use a ReAct loop. ReAct means the model chooses a next step, calls a tool, reads the result, and repeats. For one damaged car claim, it might read the policy, request a repair estimate, calculate a payment, then send large payouts to a human.

LangGraph is a Python library for building that flow as a graph. A TypedDict is a Python type that lists the fields carried through the flow. Company B's fields could include the claim ID, policy limit, repair estimate, fraud score, and review flag. A StateGraph connects the steps and passes those fields between them. Think of it as a flowchart that remembers the current claim.

Company B also needs portable evals, which are repeatable tests of model behavior. It needs exportable tool traces, clear rights to prompts and fine-tunes, plus a fallback model tested against the same evals.

<p class="checklist__eyebrow">Ask before signature</p>

Portable evals turn [reversibility into an engineering constraint](/posts/reversibility-is-the-new-correctness/). They let Company B change models while keeping the test for a good claim decision. The contract should protect that exit before the first production call.

My take is simple. Verification tells Company B what its agent did. Ownership gives the company the right to keep operating when a vendor changes course.

I think junior engineers should learn both sides now. The next economy will be shaped inside choices that look small today: where weights live, who can inspect a decision, and whether the system still runs after the contract ends.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Build Company B's claims graph so every decision can be checked and the whole system can move. In an AI economy, the right to leave is the first practical form of ownership.</p></div>
