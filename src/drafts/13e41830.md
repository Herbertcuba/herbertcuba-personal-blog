In 1976, after oil from Alaska's Prudhoe Bay field created a large but temporary source of public revenue, the state amended its constitution to save part of that income in the Alaska Permanent Fund. The first dividend in 1982 gave eligible residents a direct cash claim on returns from a shared resource.

That design returns in [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), a 2026 policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, the research group behind both this report and its predecessor, *AI 2027*. Four of Plan A's six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), a 2025 scenario about a rapid race to advanced AI. The shared authorship matters because Plan A is their positive policy response to the dangers described in the earlier work, although they call it a recommendation rather than their best-guess forecast. It proposes a Citizen's Dividend, a recurring cash payment funded by fees on AI infrastructure, and explicitly models the mechanism on Alaska.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my book about three stages of AI-driven economic change, reaches the same mechanism under the same name. The [public GitHub repository that stores my website's source files](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) shows its PDF on the site on March 23, 2026, and the corrected PDF edition cited here is dated June 24. A [Hacker News submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425), a public technology-forum post, linked to *AI 2040* on July 9. Those records establish a sequence of public artifacts. They cannot prove when either idea began or whether one influenced the other.

I think the useful claim is narrower than validation. The two public works follow the same economic chain, then divide over control. Plan A emphasizes verification, the declarations and inspections used to expose hidden computing capacity: chips, data centers, power, and the other infrastructure needed to develop advanced AI. The book asks who owns that infrastructure and its output. My extension is exit proof, a supplier-funded handover that tests whether another team can run a critical workflow before renewal. Verification governs hidden capacity, ownership allocates gains, and exit proof tests whether an institution still has bargaining power.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works reach the same Citizen's Dividend through a shared economic chain, but their public dates establish sequence rather than influence. Their different answers to control make the comparison useful.</p></div>

## The three crucibles meet Plan A

The [corrected June 24 PDF edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment as the three crucibles. Transformation is a chapter within the Void, not the second crucible's canonical label in this edition. The Correction is the first crash, when AI hype collapses and the market protection keeping less efficient human jobs in place disappears. The Void follows as AI starts producing real value without requiring as much human work: the economy recovers, but many jobs do not. Inside the Void, the Transformation chapter connects that loss of labor income to weaker consumer demand. “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Its policy response is a [Citizen's Dividend that transfers part of AI infrastructure income to consumers](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36). The Alignment asks whether AI infrastructure and models will be broadly owned or controlled by a narrow group.

In Plan A's scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, meaning work based on analysis, research, communication, or judgment. As paid human work shrinks, personal-income and payroll-tax revenue weakens. The US government in the scenario then sells tradable permits, which grant the right to build new robots and AI computing capacity, and directs most permit fees to a Citizen's Dividend projected at [$45,000 per American adult in 2032](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18) and about $1 million in 2035.

The book proposes a different funding rule: an AI Labor Equivalence Tax on each unit of machine work measured against comparable human work. The mapping is therefore precise but incomplete. The Correction converges with Plan A's diagnosis of pressure on labor income and taxes. The Void, especially its Transformation chapter, converges with the dividend response. The Alignment converges with the concern about concentrated power. Their speed, funding instrument, and governing machinery differ. I think that is meaningful thematic convergence, not proof that either timeline will happen.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both works connect automated work to weaker wages, taxes, and consumer demand. The book would tax deployed machine labor, while Plan A would charge for permission to add robots and computing capacity.</p></div>

## Verification does not settle ownership

Plan A centers on takeoff, a feedback loop in which AI automates AI research and speeds the creation of more capable systems. In its scenario, a 2029 agreement between the United States and China avoids a route to superintelligence by the end of 2030. Superintelligence means systems that exceed humans across almost all intellectual work. Development pauses in 2035 at top-expert capability, where AI can match leading human specialists on the relevant intellectual tasks, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040.

The agreement makes major chip and data-center transactions visible, opens infrastructure to reciprocal inspection, and makes almost all AI research public while keeping the use of existing models private. Its covert-project appendix stress-tests that system with a secret Chinese data center built in tunnels beside the proposed Medog Hydropower Station in Tibet. In the scenario, the dam would supply the electricity, while the tunnels would make the facility's heat harder for satellites to detect. Like *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film whose virtual world depends on physical machines, advanced AI still leaves a trail of chips, electricity, cooling, construction, and people.

I think that physical trail makes inspection plausible, but visibility does not settle distribution. Plan A gives each US citizen one share in a public corporation that receives part of the permit revenue, creating a claim on income. The [Alignment calls for distributed ownership](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57) of the infrastructure and models themselves, backed by laws against a private intelligence monopoly. Plan A offers far more detail on treaties and AI safety; the book presses an ownership question that a revenue share only partly answers. I don't know whether Plan A's verification regime could survive sustained US-China rivalry, and its authors do not claim certainty.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification can expose hidden AI development because the required infrastructure leaves physical evidence. A dividend can spread income, but ownership of the models and machines remains a separate source of power.</p></div>

## Exit proof makes control practical

Even when governments can see computing capacity and citizens hold revenue claims, an institution can still lose practical choice. A company may own its final documents while depending on an AI supplier for usable data, permissions, system settings, connections to other tools, and operating knowledge. Exit proof turns that dependence into a test: can a replacement team run the workflow before renewal?

The book's fictional insurer example makes the pressure concrete. One insurer keeps 5,000 claims adjusters, the people who investigate claims and decide payments. A rival deploys an AI system, cuts staff by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months ([printed p. 20](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21)). The numbers are illustrative, but the rush they create is easy to understand: sign first, ask how to leave later.

I would make renewal conditional on a supplier-funded, 30-day handover of one workflow and one complete data export to one replacement team. The package should show where the data came from and how it changed, transfer the necessary permissions, preserve versions of system settings and model instructions, document what each connected tool expects, and include operating and recovery guidance. The buyer owns the evaluation set, a fixed sample of past cases with accepted outcomes. The replacement may change the model, its instructions, how it finds supporting material, or the internal workflow, but it must reproduce the agreed business outcome within fixed limits for errors, time, and cost.

The chief information officer owns acceptance. Missing inputs or results outside the limits fail the test, block automatic renewal, and trigger supplier-funded correction and transition support. That consequence matters because a promise that the system can be moved gives the buyer little power unless failure changes the contract.

Since August 2, 2026, [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's main AI law, has required disclosure for certain AI interactions and machine-readable marking for some AI-generated or manipulated content. Those rules can reveal AI use. They cannot provide the data, permissions, and operating knowledge another team needs.

I think exit proof completes the control picture. Verification governs hidden capacity, ownership allocates the gains, and exit proof governs institutional bargaining power. If a system passes every transparency rule but fails a bounded handover before renewal, that failure predicts supplier lock-in: changing provider has become impractical while the compliance file still looks clean.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof makes renewal conditional on a bounded, supplier-funded handover judged against the buyer's own past cases. If another team cannot achieve the agreed business result, the buyer has transparency but no practical bargaining power.</p></div>
