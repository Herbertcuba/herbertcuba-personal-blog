In 1976, Alaska voters amended the state constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenue would enter the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a publicly owned investment fund. A related annual payment to eligible residents began in 1982. The first payment used surplus oil revenue; fund earnings supported later dividends.

The principle was simple: residents should keep a lasting claim on wealth drawn from a shared resource. Fifty years later, [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), a 90-page scenario and policy proposal, uses Alaska as its model for distributing wealth created by AI infrastructure. My speculative book, [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), uses the same precedent and the same phrase: a Citizen's Dividend.

The framework behind my book was in writing by February 3, 2026, more than six months before this article. The AI Futures Project, a US nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and studies policy, published Plan A on July 9. That timing proves neither influence nor accuracy. The overlap matters because both works see a political problem emerging before machines surpass humans across most cognitive work: work, income, and social legitimacy begin to come apart.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska turned resource wealth into a public claim. AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles use that precedent to ask who benefits when AI produces more of the economy's value.</p></div>

## What AI 2040 actually says

Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Its preferred path begins with a US-China agreement in 2029, followed by chip tracking, data center inspections, and research transparency. In the scenario, those controls prevent fully automated AI research in 2030, meaning AI does not take over almost all the work required to build more capable successors.

Progress continues within the broad human range before a pause at top-expert AI in 2035. That means systems able to outperform the best human specialists across most cognitive tasks. If the safety and governance arrangements hold, the plan permits a move toward superintelligence, systems beyond humans across almost all cognitive work, in 2040.

The economic shock comes first. In the scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor, work based mainly on reasoning and analysis, by mid-2031. Inflation-adjusted economic output grows by about 50 percent in 2032. As wages and labor taxes lose ground, governments cap robots and compute, the processing capacity supplied by advanced AI chips. Scarce permits fund a citizen-owned Compute Dividend Corporation, with its projected annual payout rising from $45,000 per person in 2032 to almost $1 million in 2035. These conditional outputs are more useful for understanding the mechanism than for trusting the exact amounts.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A combines international verification with scarce compute and robot permits, using the permit income to fund a citizen dividend during a rapid but explicitly conditional economic transition.</p></div>

## Three crucibles, one social passage

Before comparing the accounts, here is the three-part framework in plain language:

- **The Correction** begins when AI makes cognitive productivity depend more on capital than human labor, putting jobs, wages, and labor-based taxes under pressure.
- **The Void** is the unstable passage from 2027 to 2032, when the old economic bargain weakens before new ways to distribute income and support demand become secure.
- **The Alignment** begins from 2035, when society must choose between broadly shared abundance and concentrated ownership and power.

The public book page retains *The Transformation* as the label for the middle passage, while the downloadable book uses *The Void*. Its role in the framework remains the same.

Plan A follows those pressures closely. Its automation shock resembles the Correction. Its Compute Dividend Corporation addresses the gap described by the Void, using both the phrase Citizen's Dividend and the Alaska precedent. Its effort to spread AI wealth and power reaches the choice at the heart of the Alignment.

This is a striking convergence, but it does not prove my framework correct. These are speculative works with different methods. The narrower signal is still useful: a social-transition framework and a scenario about technical controls arrive at the same concern. Production can separate from wages before society agrees on a new claim to the output.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both works move from labor disruption to a distribution gap and then an ownership choice. Their overlap is a strategic signal rather than validation by itself.</p></div>

## The real disagreement is how to prepare

Plan A begins with technical control. It asks whether rival states can verify limits on chips, data centers, and AI research. Its appendix models a secret Chinese project and assigns less than a 10 percent chance that it reaches Top-Expert-Dominating AI, the level where systems beat the best human specialists across most cognitive tasks, before 2043 without being detected. Verification remains uncertain even inside their model.

My book begins with the social problem: who owns productive AI and who receives its output when employment no longer carries the distribution system. It says less about international technical controls. Plan A says more about enforcing limits than about maintaining legitimate shared ownership. Each account exposes a gap in the other.

Alaska also has limits as an analogy. Its oil field cannot move, while private chips and data centers can be placed across borders. Plan A creates a public claim through coordinated caps, which make permits scarce and valuable. If the caps fail, that source of dividend income weakens, leaving taxation or direct public ownership to carry more of the load.

The early test is visible before 2040. By 2030, watch whether serious proposals move from protecting jobs toward public claims on AI output, compute, or ownership. If AI use and productivity rise while wage income, labor-tax receipts, and consumer spending remain broadly stable, the shared thesis weakens. If those measures pull apart and ownership becomes the political question, it strengthens.

Nobody knows what waits beyond that point. Plan A is a recommendation, and *The Three Crucibles* is a preparation document for the same reason: uncertainty does not remove the need to prepare. If both accounts are directionally right, the road ahead will be bumpy because our institutions must change while the technology is still moving.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 bets on enforceable verification, while The Three Crucibles emphasizes ownership and social preparation. The shared warning strengthens only if labor income and public revenue begin separating from AI-driven output before 2030.</p></div>
