Every serious story about advanced AI eventually becomes a story about ownership. When human work is no longer the main source of economic value, how do people retain a claim on what the machines produce?

Alaska offers an early, practical answer. In 1976, voters amended the state constitution and placed part of its mineral revenue in the Alaska Permanent Fund, an investment fund designed to preserve a temporary oil windfall. Its earnings have helped finance annual payments to eligible residents since the first dividend in 1982. Both [AI 2040](https://ai-2040.com/) and [my book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), point to that arrangement because it turns shared resource ownership into household income.

My framework was public on my site by March 23, 2026, before AI 2040 appeared publicly in July. That sequence proves neither influence nor correctness. It makes the overlap worth examining because the documents start in different places and arrive at many of the same pressures.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Two speculative maps ask how people keep an economic claim on AI productivity when work no longer performs that role.</p></div>

## What Plan A actually is

*AI 2040: Plan A* is a 2026 scenario and policy proposal from the AI Futures Project, the nonprofit research group behind the 2025 scenario *AI 2027*. Four of that work's five authors are among Plan A's six authors. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” which is how its dates should be read.

Its preferred path begins with a United States–China agreement in 2029. The powers monitor compute, meaning the processing capacity supplied by advanced chips, while researchers disclose more work and datacenters face inspection. The scenario pauses in 2035 at AI that outperforms top human experts, then moves in 2040 toward superintelligence: systems better, faster, and cheaper than the best humans across almost all tasks.

Plan A also imagines tradable permits, limited rights that can be bought and sold, for building robots or compute. A majority of the permit fees would fund a citizen dividend as labor income and the old tax base weaken.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A combines a controlled AI timeline with chip tracking, inspections, research transparency, and a dividend funded by permits on new productive capacity.</p></div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

*The Three Crucibles* organizes the transition as three social tests rather than a diplomatic timeline.

**The Correction** comes when markets recognize that AI makes capital more productive while reducing the economic leverage of labor. **The Transformation** is the difficult period in which societies must rebuild income and demand; the book proposes a Citizen's Dividend, a direct transfer from AI productivity to people, as part of that bridge. **The Alignment** is the political settlement that follows. Here, alignment means bringing ownership and institutions into line with broad human interests, not merely making a model follow instructions.

These are not promises about exact dates. They describe a sequence: economic correction, social redesign, then a struggle over who governs abundance.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction shifts value away from labor, the Transformation rebuilds distribution, and the Alignment decides whether abundance is shared or concentrated.</p></div>

## The overlap begins with income

The first convergence is causal. If AI systems perform more paid cognitive work, wages represent less of the economy and taxes tied to wages shrink. Machine investment can also be deducted or depreciated. Output may rise while the institutions that turn work into income and public revenue become weaker.

The second convergence is the Citizen's Dividend. Both documents use that name and cite Alaska, although their funding designs differ. Plan A specifies permits to build robots or compute. My book names taxation of AI labor as a pillar but does not fully settle the taxable unit in the pages at issue. The shared diagnosis is stronger than the shared policy design.

The final convergence is political. Plan A wants AI's gains spread broadly rather than captured by a small group. The Alignment asks whether we become stewards of abundance or create new feudal owners. This is structural agreement, not evidence that either scenario will happen.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The documents agree on the pressure facing labor income, the need for a citizen claim on AI productivity, and the danger of concentrated ownership.</p></div>

## Their disagreement is about control

Plan A puts enormous weight on verification: states declare compute, inspectors check datacenters, chips are tracked, and research becomes more transparent. Its covert-project appendix estimates a below 10 percent chance that a hidden project could reach AI surpassing top experts without detection before 2043. Low probability is not zero, and verification gets harder when the prize for cheating grows.

My book puts more weight on distributed ownership: who holds the machines, models, and economic claim on their output. Yet ownership does not solve the technical problem of losing control of a capable system. In the other direction, perfect records do not tell us who should receive the wealth. Each map contains a missing layer that the other makes visible.

I do not know which emphasis will matter more. Plan A cannot know either. That uncertainty is why the comparison is useful.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification can constrain hidden capability without distributing its rewards; ownership can distribute power without guaranteeing technical control.</p></div>

## Why prepare for a future nobody knows

Nobody knows whether AI 2040's timetable will survive politics, physics, or human behavior. Nobody knows whether the three crucibles will arrive neatly, overlap, or fail to appear. If both maps are even directionally right, however, productivity and capability may move faster than our income systems and governance.

That is the purpose of *The Three Crucibles*. It is a preparation document, built to help us recognize the transition before its choices become irreversible. Alaska does not tell us how to govern superintelligence, but it shows that societies can decide who owns a valuable resource before distributing its returns. We may need that habit of thought sooner than we expect.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Neither map predicts the future with certainty; together they give us a reason to prepare for a difficult transition in income, ownership, and control.</p></div>
