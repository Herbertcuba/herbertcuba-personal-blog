The most consequential object in [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is not an AI model. It is a permit.

In the document's imagined 2032 economy, the United States sells scarce rights to operate robots and computing hardware. Those permits raise extraordinary sums because human work no longer generates enough public revenue, while companies can deduct much of their investment in machines. The scenario has quietly replaced the economic machinery beneath the state.

Plan A came from the AI Futures Project, a nonprofit research group studying advanced AI, in July 2026. Four of the five people who wrote its earlier [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/) scenario returned for this work. They describe Plan A as “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” so its numbers are proposals under stress, not observations from the future.

That is also the right way to compare it with [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my framework for the shift from labor to machine production. The book was published on March 22, 2026, appeared on my site the next day, and reached its current edition on June 24. The order establishes independence, not accuracy.

I think the independent overlap supports a narrower and more useful claim. Advanced AI may force us to move the fiscal and political claim on production away from the job and toward the machine.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A's permits reveal the deeper forecast: if machines displace enough paid work, governments need a new source of revenue and citizens need a new claim on production. The comparison with The Three Crucibles is strongest at that institutional level.</p></div>

## The forecast hides an accounting revolution

Plan A imagines a 2029 agreement between the United States and China, nearly automated AI research in 2030, a pause at top-expert AI in 2035, and a move toward superintelligence in 2040. Top-expert AI means software that matches the best human specialists across many fields. Superintelligence goes further, exceeding the strongest human capability across almost all intellectual work.

This is a takeoff scenario: AI improves AI research, which can produce more capable systems and accelerate the next round. By mid-2031, the document assigns one third of all cognitive labor to AI. Here, cognitive labor means jobs whose main inputs are data, reasoning, communication, and professional judgment rather than physical action.

The economic mechanism matters more than the speed. Wages and payroll taxes shrink as software does more work. At the same time, AI companies reinvest revenue in datacenters, factories, and robots, then deduct or depreciate those costs before paying tax on profit. Plan A says the tax base would collapse as a share of the economy without reform.

The current PDF map of *The Three Crucibles* names three stages: **The Correction** reprices companies and work around AI productivity; **The Void** begins when output and jobs separate; and **The Alignment** is the choice between broad stewardship and concentrated power. The landing page still labels the second Crucible **The Transformation**; the current edition uses that name for a chapter within The Void.

Plan A does not contain a matching market correction, and its geopolitical machinery is much more detailed. Yet it walks into The Void through its own arithmetic. Production rises while wages lose their ability to distribute purchasing power. I think that is the first real convergence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The timelines diverge, but the causal chain is similar: AI takes on paid knowledge work, wage-linked revenue weakens, and rising production no longer guarantees that households or governments receive enough income.</p></div>

## Two routes from machines to citizens

Plan A answers the fiscal gap with a cap on robots and compute, the chip and datacenter capacity used to train and run AI. Tradable permits allocate that limited capacity to buyers. Under the scenario's unprecedented growth assumptions, they bring the US government $50 trillion in fiscal year 2032 and $180 trillion in 2034.

Part of the revenue goes to a Compute Dividend Corporation. Each US citizen receives one share, and the corporation pays profits equally. The resulting Citizen's Dividend rises from $45,000 per person in 2032 to roughly $1 million in 2035 within the scenario. Plan A compares this structure with the Alaska Permanent Fund, which Alaska voters created in 1976 to reserve part of the state's mineral revenue and which paid its first resident dividend in 1982.

*The Three Crucibles* reaches the same distribution problem with the line, “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Machine productivity cannot sustain a consumer economy when the people who need its products no longer earn enough to buy them. The book also calls for a Citizen's Dividend, but combines it with a tax on AI labor and distributed AI ownership. Distributed ownership means giving people and public institutions enforceable claims on AI systems, infrastructure, or output instead of leaving those claims with a few providers.

My take is that the distinction sits upstream. Plan A regulates concentrated capacity and sends some permit income through a public share; my framework also asks whether ownership of AI capacity can spread. Ownership affects who sets access, price, and purpose before any dividend is calculated.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works independently arrive at a Citizen's Dividend and the Alaska precedent. Plan A socializes part of the income from regulated capacity; The Three Crucibles also seeks to spread claims on the productive capacity itself.</p></div>

## Visibility is not legitimacy

Plan A's central governance tool is total research transparency. Frontier AI developers would share research methods and results, while chip tracking and devices installed in major datacenters would help countries verify that computing capacity is used only for agreed purposes. Verification here means gathering evidence that declared facilities and training runs match the agreement.

The authors test that system through a fictional covert Chinese project. Their branch diverts 500,000 smuggled H100-equivalents to a hidden datacenter near the Medog Hydropower Station. An H100-equivalent measures computing capacity relative to Nvidia's H100 AI chip. Roughly 200 people know about the operation, and the authors estimate its chance of reaching top-expert AI without detection before 2043 at less than ten percent. Every detail belongs to their scenario, not to the public record.

This exercise exposes a genuine gap in *The Three Crucibles*. Distributed ownership and AI taxation do not tell inspectors how to find an undeclared training cluster, nor do they solve the technical problem of keeping highly capable systems under human control. Plan A takes those problems seriously.

Its own gap appears after a project has been found and approved. Accurate chip records can tell governments where productive power sits. They cannot make that arrangement legitimate or ensure that citizens retain a durable claim on its output. I don't know what international bargain could hold these layers together under severe competition. I think neither layer is optional: capacity must be visible enough to constrain, and its benefits must be owned broadly enough to defend.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A is stronger on detecting secret AI capacity and constraining dangerous development. The Three Crucibles is stronger on the economic legitimacy of declared capacity. Verification and ownership close different paths to concentrated power.</p></div>

## Evidence will arrive before superintelligence

We do not need to wait until 2040 to test the shared diagnosis. The first signal will appear in public accounts. If capable AI spreads while wages, household purchasing power, and labor-tax receipts remain resilient, the case for a new fiscal foundation weakens. New taxes or permits tied to chips, robots, energy, or AI-generated output would point the other way.

A second signal will appear in the design of public benefits. Annual cash support creates a different relationship from a dividend backed by an asset, fixed revenue claim, or share. The legal durability of the citizen's claim matters more than its initial amount.

The third signal is whether verification survives contact with rivalry. Inspectors must be able to account for large computing clusters, identify undeclared construction, and distinguish ordinary AI use from prohibited training. A system that works only among friendly governments cannot carry Plan A's weight.

Finally, ownership will show whether The Three Crucibles reaches beyond redistribution. If households and public institutions gain meaningful claims on AI infrastructure and output, distributed ownership is becoming real. If providers retain control while governments recycle part of their income, the center of power has not moved.

This is the standard I want the comparison to meet. Agreement between two scenarios should sharpen what we measure, not license a stronger prophecy. I think the central question is now clear enough to test: as intelligence becomes infrastructure, will citizens receive assistance from its owners, or become owners of its returns?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Watch tax receipts, the legal basis of dividends, the performance of compute verification, and the spread of enforceable ownership claims. The decisive transition begins when citizenship, rather than employment, becomes the durable route to a share of machine production.</p></div>
