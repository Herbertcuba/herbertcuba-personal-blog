In 1969, Alaska received $900 million from oil leases on its North Slope. Much went into public services and infrastructure, so voters decided the next windfall needed a longer life. In 1976, they created the **Alaska Permanent Fund**, a state-owned investment fund that saves part of Alaska’s mineral revenue. Its earnings have supported annual payments to eligible residents since 1982.

The fund established a simple principle: when a scarce public asset creates exceptional wealth, citizens can retain a claim on the return. Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), the AI Futures Project’s 2026 governance scenario, and [my 2026 speculative book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) both apply that principle to artificial intelligence. The book’s public page and PDF link were online by March 23, 2026; *AI 2040* was publicly linked on July 9, 2026. That timing proves no forecast, but the resemblance is hard to dismiss.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska gives both works the same starting idea: if machine capacity becomes the productive asset, citizens may need a claim on its returns.</p></div>

## What AI 2040 actually proposes

The AI Futures Project is a US research nonprofit behind *AI 2027*, its 2025 scenario about a race toward powerful AI. Its new [90-page Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is, in the authors’ words, “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” It describes a path the United States and China could choose if AI capabilities keep rising quickly.

In the scenario, a 2029 agreement prevents the near-total automation of AI research that would otherwise arrive in 2030. Development continues within human ability, pauses around top-human-expert level in 2035, then resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. The report defines superintelligence as AI significantly better than the best humans at everything, while also faster and cheaper.

Control depends on verification, meaning practical proof that limits are being followed. Advanced chip transactions are monitored, data centers inspected, and most AI research made transparent. Compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI, is tied to tradable permits. In the US version, permit income and a citizen-owned Compute Dividend Corporation support a dividend. The scenario projects roughly 50 percent real economic growth and $45,000 per US citizen in 2032. These are scenario outputs, not economic evidence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A delays the fastest AI takeoff through an agreement, verified limits, and a pause, then uses permits and public ownership to spread some of the projected gain.</p></div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

**The Correction** breaks the old economic equation. As AI turns more knowledge work into a return on machines and capital, employment becomes a weaker way to distribute income. That does not mean every job disappears.

**The Void** is the unstable middle period; the current public book page calls it *The Transformation*. Workers receive less wage income, governments collect less payroll-tax revenue, and consumer demand weakens before a replacement is ready. The book proposes a **Citizen’s Dividend**, a direct transfer from AI infrastructure’s returns to the people who still buy what the economy produces. Robots do not buy iPhones.

**The Alignment** is the political choice that follows. Do citizens become stewards of machine abundance, or do a few owners become what the book calls new feudal lords? Here, alignment means bringing ownership and institutions into line with an economy that can no longer rely on wages to share prosperity.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction weakens labor’s economic role, The Void exposes the gap before new distribution exists, and The Alignment decides whether abundance is shared or concentrated.</p></div>

## The overlap is structural, not predictive

Both accounts follow the same chain. AI shifts income from workers toward owners of productive infrastructure, labor-tax revenue shrinks relative to the economy, and a citizen dividend restores some of the purchasing power once provided by wages. Both choose the same name and the same Alaska precedent.

They differ on speed and funding. *The Three Crucibles* treats the correction as already beginning and proposes taxes on AI labor or output. Plan A projects AI performing a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, then funds distribution through permits for robot and compute capacity. Different instruments address the same break.

The deeper difference concerns failure. Plan A tries to make dangerous capacity visible and stoppable. Its covert-project appendix gives a hidden Chinese program less than a 10 percent chance of reaching AI that dominates top human experts without detection before 2043. My book spends less time on loss of control and more on ownership. Verification asks whether society can enforce limits; ownership asks who keeps a claim on the value when enforcement is imperfect. We need both answers.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works share an economic diagnosis but guard against different failures: Plan A focuses on controlling AI capacity, while The Three Crucibles focuses on preventing permanent concentration of its value.</p></div>

## Preparation is the point

No one knows whether AI development will follow Plan A’s timetable, stall on physical limits, or take another route. Its authors do not claim to know, and neither do I. Convergence between two speculative maps is not validation. It is a reason to take their shared structure seriously before events force the decisions.

Alaska did not solve anything as large as superintelligence, but its smaller lesson matters: ownership rules are easier to establish before an asset dominates the surrounding economy. If *AI 2040* and *The Three Crucibles* are directionally right, the road ahead will be bumpy. My book is a preparation document for that road, because uncertainty is precisely when we should decide what we want to preserve.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Nobody can predict the route, but the shared warning is clear enough to prepare for: control dangerous capability, preserve broad ownership, and expect a rough transition.</p></div>
