In 1976, Alaska voters changed the state constitution to protect part of a temporary oil windfall. The result was the Alaska Permanent Fund, a public investment fund that turns some oil revenue into a lasting financial asset. Since 1982, its investment earnings have helped finance annual cash payments to eligible residents.

Fifty years later, that institution appears in two attempts to think through an AI economy: [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) and [my book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/). Both cite Alaska because it answers a basic question: when a shared resource creates extraordinary wealth, citizens can hold a direct claim on it.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Alaska shows that a society can turn a concentrated resource windfall into a durable public asset and a recurring citizen payment.</p>
</div>

## What these two documents are trying to do

AI 2040 is a 90-page scenario and policy proposal published in 2026 by the AI Futures Project, the US nonprofit research group behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its 2025 scenario about rapid AI progress. The authors call Plan A “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” It describes a path they believe governments should try to create.

In that path, the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029 that slows the automation of AI research. The deal makes advanced development more visible and limits compute, the processing capacity used to build and run AI. The scenario pauses in 2035 at systems as capable as the best human specialists, then moves in 2040 toward superintelligence: AI that exceeds the best humans across almost all tasks while working faster and more cheaply. Plan A uses permits on chips and robot labor to finance a large citizen dividend.

*The Three Crucibles* was written more than six months before Plan A appeared. It divides the pressure into three stages:

- **The Correction (2026):** AI shifts productive power from paid labor toward capital, exposing how quickly work can lose bargaining value.
- **The Void (2027–2032):** the old link between jobs, income, and demand weakens before a stable replacement is ready.
- **The Alignment (2035+):** society decides whether machine abundance is broadly stewarded or concentrated among a small group of owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Plan A proposes a controlled route to extremely capable AI; the three crucibles describe the economic and political passage society may face along the way.</p>
</div>

## Their deepest agreement is about income

The shared argument starts with a simple mechanism. If AI systems perform more paid work, wages and labor taxes can shrink as a share of the economy even while production rises. People still need purchasing power, and governments still need revenue. Greater output does not solve either problem by itself. As the book puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.”

Both documents therefore arrive at a **citizen dividend**, a direct payment that gives people a claim on machine-created wealth. Plan A proposes a public corporation with one share per citizen, funded through permits on scarce AI processing capacity and robots. *The Three Crucibles* proposes taxes on AI labor alongside distributed ownership. Both cite Alaska as the practical precedent.

The overlap is specific, but it is not proof. Citizen dividends are older than either document, and both could get the timing wrong. The convergence shows that distribution belongs at the center of the AI question, rather than being left as a repair for later.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Both accounts expect AI to weaken work as the main distributor of income, so both give citizens a direct claim on machine-created value.</p>
</div>

## Where they part is just as important

Plan A puts most of its weight on verification, the ability to check whether states and companies follow agreed limits. It imagines tracked chips, inspected datacenters, transparent research, and international oversight. Its covert-project appendix tests the weak point: the document estimates a below-10% chance that a hidden project could reach AI that dominates top human experts without detection before 2043.

My book puts more weight on ownership. Even a perfectly inspected system can concentrate its returns, while widely shared returns do not make an uncontrollable system safe. Plan A is stronger on how civilization might restrain dangerous capability; *The Three Crucibles* is more concerned with who retains an enforceable claim on the output.

That makes the documents complementary. Verification governs what powerful systems may do. Ownership governs who benefits if those systems work. A serious preparation needs both questions in view.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Plan A focuses on keeping advanced AI visible and controllable; The Three Crucibles focuses on preventing its economic value from becoming permanently concentrated.</p>
</div>

## Preparation matters more than prediction

No one knows whether there will be a US–China agreement in 2029, a pause in 2035, or any clean sequence resembling my three stages. Political conflict, physical bottlenecks, and slower adoption could change the timing. Unexpected capability gains could compress it.

That uncertainty is the point of a preparation document. *The Three Crucibles* is meant to help us recognize the economic stages before they harden into institutions, while Plan A forces the same exercise for technical control and international governance. If the two accounts are even broadly right, we have a bumpy road ahead. We cannot know its exact course, but we can discuss control, ownership, and distribution before the pressure chooses for us.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Neither document knows the future; their value lies in helping us prepare early for a difficult transition whose most important choices may become harder to reverse.</p>
</div>
