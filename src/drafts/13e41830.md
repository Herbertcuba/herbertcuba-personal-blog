[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) is a superintelligence plan with a Citizen's Dividend inside it.

More than six months ago, I wrote [The Three Crucibles](/books/the-three-crucibles/) as a preparation document for the same economic break. The book was public on my site by March 23. AI 2040 appeared in July.

Its scenario moves through a 2029 US-China agreement, a 2035 pause at top-human-expert AI, then superintelligence in 2040. AI takes a large share of work. Wage-based taxation buckles, so machine wealth returns through the dividend.

I think the overlap reaches past dates. Both documents expect machine output to outrun wage income, then ask who receives the abundance and who controls the machinery.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles describe the same economic break from different starting points. Plan A trusts verification, while my book favors distributed ownership.</p></div>

## What AI 2040 actually is

AI 2040 presents a policy recommendation as future history.

Four of its six authors also wrote [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/). They call Plan A "primarily a recommendation." The dates show how it might work and fail.

The 2029 agreement prevents fully automated AI research in 2030. Development stays within human range until a 2035 pause, then resumes toward superintelligence in 2040.

The agreement has four stated principles: Buy Time, Total Research Transparency, Diffuse AI Broadly, and Reversibility. Major datacenter owners and chip suppliers declare large transactions. Inspectors check major sites. Research using large training runs is almost entirely transparent. Small-compute and no-compute R&D stay outside this system. Everyday model use, called inference, stays private.

Plan A projects global compute rising from about 20 million H100-equivalents in 2026 to 60 billion in 2034. An H100-equivalent measures compute against one Nvidia H100 chip.

By mid-2031, AI performs a third of cognitive labor in the scenario. Real GDP growth reaches about 50 percent in 2032. A cap-and-trade system limits robot and compute capacity, then sells permits to build more. Plan A projects $50 trillion in US permit revenue for that fiscal year.

Company B scales those national numbers down to one claims department. This fictional insurer deploys a group of AI agents, cuts headcount by 40 percent, and lowers premiums by 20 percent. Within six months, it beats a rival that keeps 5,000 claims adjusters.

I think Plan A stretches Company B's squeeze across a national economy. Its figures belong to the scenario and show the bill if that world unfolds.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A 2029 agreement and verified compute buy time before a pause near top-expert AI. Explosive growth still forces Plan A to govern the economy as well as the models.</p></div>

## The three Crucibles appear inside Plan A

The Three Crucibles maps the same transition in more philosophical terms.

The book's interior names them The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment.

| Crucible | Company B | AI 2040 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Correction | Automation cuts labor cost and changes the market price. | AI takes more cognitive work and weakens the wage-tax base. |
| The Void | Output rises while fewer workers receive wages. | The tax base collapses as machines replace taxable human labor. |
| The Alignment | Ownership decides who benefits. | The plan diffuses AI to reduce concentrated power. |

The Void is basic arithmetic. The book puts it bluntly: "Robots do not buy iPhones." Company B can process claims cheaply, yet an economy full of Company Bs removes income from many buyers.

Both documents use the same answer and name. The Three Crucibles proposes a Citizen's Dividend, plus taxes on AI labor and computational output. Plan A sends most robot and compute permit fees to American adults through its Citizen's Dividend. Each US citizen owns one share in the proposed Compute Dividend Corporation. Both works cite Alaska's oil dividend.

Their funding mechanics diverge. My book taxes human-equivalent AI labor and automated output. Plan A limits production capacity and sells permission to build more.

I think both mechanisms follow the same arithmetic: shrinking payroll creates a need for a public claim on machine wealth. Two maps can share a wrong turn. The overlap leaves a testable idea: machine abundance stays politically fragile when income and ownership remain narrow.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction moves production away from labor. The Void exposes the demand and tax problem. The Alignment decides whether a Citizen's Dividend becomes a durable public claim or a payment inside a concentrated system.</p></div>

## The maps split over control

Plan A puts enormous weight on verification.

It treats compute almost like spice in *Dune*. Tracking the scarce input gives institutions a way to shape power. Declared chips and inspected datacenters keep the race visible.

The authors test this idea with a covert branch. In that scenario, China builds a hidden datacenter beside the Medog Hydropower Station. It receives about 500,000 H100-equivalent chips, and roughly 200 people know about the project. The US intelligence community estimates that its chance of reaching Top-Expert-Dominating AI undetected stays below 10 percent until 2043. That term means AI that dominates even top human experts.

Verification only works when the project becomes visible in time.

The Three Crucibles puts more weight on distributed ownership. It favors infrastructure and models that people can run on systems they control. Company B could follow every reporting rule while placing its claims workflow behind one supplier's API. A change in access or licensing could interrupt the operation.

Plan A already gives every US citizen a share in its Compute Dividend Corporation. That creates a direct claim on permit revenue. Operational exit and political voice receive less attention.

I think verification and ownership answer separate problems. Verification helps institutions see the race. Ownership decides who can keep operating after an institution or supplier fails.

I don't know which failure arrives first. Plan A is stronger on civilization-scale loss of control. The Three Crucibles spends more time on power after labor loses its bargaining position.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses compute accounting and research transparency to keep dangerous capability visible. The Three Crucibles asks who holds a usable claim on the intelligence layer when verification or a supplier fails.</p></div>

## The global fork reaches a claims workflow

Company B turns the philosophical split into an engineering choice.

Suppose its claims agent uses ReAct, short for reasoning and acting. The model reads a claim, calls a policy tool, then chooses its next action from the result. The pattern joins model decisions with tool use.

LangGraph is a Python library for workflows that branch or repeat. A `StateGraph` connects steps as nodes and uses edges to choose the next step. A `TypedDict` describes expected keys in shared state, such as `claim_id`, `evidence`, and `requires_human`, for static type checkers. Python leaves runtime validation to other code.

Setting `requires_human = True` only flags the claim. An engineer still has to add a conditional edge that sends it to a human-review node. A runtime validator should reject a denial when required evidence is missing.

Before signing a model contract, Company B can replay legally reviewed claims with its current model and a named fallback. Both runs use the same policy and human approval boundary. The team sets a pass threshold in advance.

A provider becomes reversible after that workflow passes the same policy and approval gates elsewhere, with evidence intact. The result applies only to bounded continuity for that workflow.

This is the local version of [who owns the agent layer](/posts/who-owns-the-agent-layer/) and why [reversibility belongs beside correctness](/posts/reversibility-is-the-new-correctness/). I think junior engineers meet this issue when they decide where state lives and how a failed route reaches a person.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>ReAct and LangGraph can make Company B's workflow legible, while `TypedDict` only helps static checking. Preparation also needs runtime validation and a real route to human review. A fallback replay must pass a stated threshold.</p></div>

## A preparation document for a rough future

AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles share a deeper purpose than prediction.

The AI Futures Project tests its policy inside a whole imagined world. My book prepares people and organizations for machine output moving faster than social adaptation.

Company B gives us a small version of that future. Once its agent works and costs fall, technical success opens a political argument about payment and ownership.

The dates may move, and the US-China agreement may never exist. AI progress could hit physical limits that Plan A underestimates. Preparation still takes time because ownership and fallback routes have to be built.

No one knows what waits in 2040. If the AI Futures Project and I are even directionally right, the road there will be bumpy. The Three Crucibles is my preparation document for that road, written while we still have room to choose how much power we surrender and how much we share.
