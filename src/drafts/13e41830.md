On July 9, 2026, a public link to [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) appeared with a detailed path from today’s AI race to a 2040 handover to superintelligent systems. The AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that studies advanced AI futures, describes the document as a policy recommendation told through a conditional scenario rather than a prediction. Its projections are arguments, not observed data.

Plan A follows the group’s April 2025 scenario *AI 2027*, which examined a rapid international race to build AI that surpasses human experts. Four of that work’s five authors also wrote Plan A. Meanwhile, the framework behind my book [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) was public on my site on March 23, 2026, and its current PDF edition followed on June 24. That ordering establishes chronology, not influence.

The overlap is still unusually specific. Both works describe machine production pulling away from labor income, both arrive at a “Citizen’s Dividend,” and both end at a political fork between broadly shared abundance and concentrated power. I think this corroborates the fault line I named, although it does not confirm my dates or prove that either map will be right.

  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Two independent maps arrive at the same economic break and distribution problem. Their agreement is evidence that the mechanism deserves attention, not evidence that either timeline has been validated.</p>

## Production can grow while distribution breaks

In Plan A’s scenario, AI performs one third of all cognitive labor by mid-2031. Cognitive labor means work based mainly on analysis, communication, and judgment rather than physical effort. The scenario then projects roughly 50 percent real economic growth in 2032 while warning that the tax base would collapse as a share of the economy, because payroll taxes shrink and companies can deduct or depreciate the machines replacing workers.

The current edition of *The Three Crucibles* organizes the transition as The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. Its landing page still calls the second crucible The Transformation, while the current PDF places The Transformation as a chapter inside The Void. The Correction describes the repricing that begins when productivity increasingly belongs to capital and the economic value assigned to labor approaches zero; The Void is the unstable period in which societies must rebuild demand and meaning; The Alignment is the choice between stewardship and a new concentration of power.

Plan A has no close equivalent to The Correction because it begins with geopolitical competition over advanced AI. Yet both maps expose the same feedback failure: companies can produce far more with fewer people, while the people displaced from wages lose the purchasing power that makes production valuable. I think that mechanism matters even if AI progress is slower than either timeline assumes.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>The shared claim is not simply that AI removes jobs. It is that production, income, consumption, and public revenue stop reinforcing one another when machine output scales faster than labor income.</p>
</div>

## The dividend is a bridge, not an answer

The most striking convergence is linguistic as well as structural. My book proposes a “Citizen’s Dividend,” a direct transfer from the productivity of AI infrastructure, and Plan A titles its 2033 chapter “The Citizen’s Dividend.” Both point to the Alaska Permanent Fund, created by voters in 1976 to reserve part of the state’s mineral revenue and paying its first resident dividend in 1982. The precedent matters because citizens receive a recurring claim linked to returns from a productive asset, rather than a payment justified only by hardship.

The funding mechanisms differ. *The Three Crucibles* proposes taxing AI labor and spreading ownership of AI assets. Plan A imagines permits for scarce inputs such as advanced computing capacity and robots, with the proceeds flowing into a Compute Dividend Corporation that gives one share to every citizen. In its scenario, the annual payment rises from about $45,000 per person in 2032 to roughly $1 million in 2035. Those numbers depend on Plan A’s extraordinary growth assumptions; they are not forecasts we can bank.

The economic logic is less fragile than the figures. As the book puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.” A dividend keeps demand connected to productive capacity, but my take is that cash alone cannot settle who sets the rules, owns the infrastructure, or can withdraw access. A society can distribute income while leaving control almost completely concentrated.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Both works use the same name and the same Alaska precedent for a dividend tied to productive assets. They disagree on how to fund it, and neither should confuse distributing cash with distributing control.</p>
</div>

## Verification and ownership solve different failures

Plan A puts verification at the center of its answer. Here, verification means producing credible evidence that governments and companies possess no more computing capacity than they declared and are not secretly training prohibited systems. Computing capacity, or compute, is the combination of specialized chips, electricity, and data-center infrastructure used to train and run AI. Plan A’s proposed US-China agreement relies on chip tracking, declared facilities, inspections, research transparency, and a pause before systems reach the level of the best human experts across most cognitive fields.

*The Three Crucibles* asks a different question: who holds an enforceable claim on the output? Its distributed-ownership pillar would spread access to AI models, hardware, and economic returns instead of leaving productive capacity inside a small number of firms or states. Verification tests whether an actor obeys a limit. Ownership determines who benefits while that limit holds.

The distinction exposes the weakness in both maps. A verified system can still concentrate wealth and political leverage, while widely distributed AI can make secret races and safety enforcement harder to control. I don’t know which institutional balance can survive sustained geopolitical pressure. I think any serious plan must measure both axes rather than treating transparency as a substitute for ownership, or ownership as a substitute for safety.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Verification limits hidden capacity; ownership allocates legitimate claims on output. Either can fail without the other, leaving society with transparent concentration or distributed systems that nobody can reliably govern.</p>
</div>

## Test the mechanism before the date

The comparison becomes useful when it produces observations that can prove it wrong. First, watch whether AI-driven output rises while wages, purchasing power, and labor-based tax revenue lose ground. If deep automation leaves those relationships broadly intact, then the economic break described by both works is weaker than claimed.

Second, examine the design of any public payment. Does it scale automatically with returns from AI infrastructure, giving citizens a durable claim, or does it depend on a budget decision that can be withdrawn each year? The amount matters, but the underlying right matters more because it determines whether people participate in the productive system or merely receive relief from it.

Third, separate audit rights from ownership rights whenever a government or company presents an AI governance plan. Ask who can inspect the chips and facilities, then ask who owns them, receives the cash flow, and can challenge a decision. If concentrated ownership repeatedly delivers broad prosperity under enforceable verification, the case for distributed ownership weakens. If inspections cannot expose undeclared capacity, Plan A’s governance architecture weakens with it.

*AI 2040* gives technical loss of control the depth that *The Three Crucibles* lacks. My framework gives ownership and economic demand the weight that Plan A leaves unresolved. I think that is the honest value of the convergence: I named the institutional sequence before this external team published its version, and their work now makes the missing half of mine easier to see. The future will not care which map arrived first; it will care whether we joined safety to ownership before machine abundance hardened into political fact.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>A forecast earns attention when its mechanism can be tested before its headline date. Track the split between output and income, the rights beneath any dividend, and the difference between audited capacity and shared ownership, or we may verify the machine perfectly while leaving its society undefined.</p>
</div>
