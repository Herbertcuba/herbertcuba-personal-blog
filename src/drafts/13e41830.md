An advanced-AI plan can decide who may build the machines while leaving open who is entitled to what they produce. That gap is the clearest lesson from comparing two detailed maps of the next decade.

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 2026 policy recommendation from the AI Futures Project, the group behind *AI 2027*, its April 2025 scenario about AI automating research on better AI. [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my map of the economic and political stages of an AI transition. A March 23, 2026 commit in my site’s source repository added the book PDF and download link; the current book PDF’s metadata is dated June 24, 2026, while the Plan A PDF is dated July 11, 2026.

That sequence establishes which dated artifact came first, not influence or empirical validation. Plan A’s authors call it “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction” and say it is not their best guess. I think the useful signal is narrower: a group starting from international security and the prevention of catastrophic AI behavior reaches much of the same economic structure, then chooses a different way to govern it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The chronology permits a comparison and nothing stronger: a later policy scenario reaches the same economic problem as The Three Crucibles, while its different solution reveals what each map leaves exposed.</p></div>

## The shared sequence goes beyond universal basic income

In Plan A’s scenario, the United States and China make a deal in 2029 that prevents fully automated AI research in 2030, meaning AI would otherwise do most of the work of designing better AI. Development stays within the range of human ability until a pause at top-human-expert level in 2035. In 2040, it resumes toward superintelligence: systems significantly better than the best humans at everything while also faster and cheaper.

*The Three Crucibles* uses a different clock but a related sequence. The Correction in 2026 burns away inflated expectations and the financial protection around inefficient work. The Transformation, dated 2027 to 2032, is the period when machine productivity rises while wages and consumer demand weaken. The Alignment, from 2035 onward, turns the economic break into a political choice between shared abundance and dependence on a small group that controls the infrastructure.

The dates do not match, and neither document has earned the right to treat them as a timetable. Yet I think the shared sequence is unusually specific: human labor loses its central role, job-based revenue and demand weaken, both prescribe a citizen payment, and control of the machines becomes the political question. Plan A reaches it through a race between states; my book starts with the balance sheet and the social contract.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both maps move from labor displacement to a distribution crisis and then to a struggle over power, even though Plan A delays the decisive capability jump and The Three Crucibles expects the economic rupture sooner.</p></div>

## The same dividend rests on different tax systems

Plan A makes the fiscal problem concrete on pages 17 and 18. In its 2032 scenario, inflation-adjusted economic output grows by about 50 percent, but personal and payroll tax revenue falls as AI systems and robots replace people. Corporate tax does not automatically fill the gap because firms reinvest in datacenters, factories, and robots, then expense or depreciate those costs before tax. Output can therefore rise while the public share of that output shrinks.

Its answer is cap-and-trade for computing capacity and robots. Computing capacity means the chips and datacenters used to train and run AI. When governments cap it, companies bid for permits to add more. In the scenario, permit revenue reaches $50 trillion in real 2025 dollars in fiscal year 2032 and $180 trillion in 2034. Most of that revenue eventually supports a “Citizen’s Dividend” for American adults, starting at $45,000 per person in 2032 and reaching about $1 million in 2035.

*The Three Crucibles* reaches the same named payment through another gate. Its AI labor-equivalence tax would charge a company when AI replaces a human role, in proportion to the public revenue that person would have generated. The Citizen’s Dividend would then transfer value from AI infrastructure to the people sustaining demand in an automated economy.

I think this difference is more important than the shared label. A permit system taxes a visible bottleneck before machines work, which may make large datacenters easier to count but leaves hidden capacity as an obvious escape route. A labor-equivalence tax follows replaced work, but it requires governments to define a human-equivalent unit and stop companies from reclassifying jobs or output. The common diagnosis is strong. The collection mechanism remains open.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A captures value at the capacity gate, while The Three Crucibles taxes the work AI replaces. Both can fund a Citizen’s Dividend, but each depends on a different measurement system and invites a different form of avoidance.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership fail in different ways

Plan A’s safety architecture asks states to declare chip holdings, inspect major datacenters, monitor whether approved facilities are training new systems, publish most advanced-AI research, and require safety cases. A safety case is an evidence-backed argument that a system will not cause an irreversible catastrophe. Together, these measures are meant to slow the race without giving one country or company a secret lead.

The document stress-tests that premise in an alternate branch in Appendix D. China diverts 500,000 H100-equivalents, a capacity measure based on the H100 AI chip from chip designer Nvidia, into a covert project beside the Medog Hydropower Station. Legitimate construction provides cover, while underground tunnels hide waste heat from infrared satellites. The branch makes the acceptable amount of unseen capacity a central assumption rather than a technical footnote.

*The Three Crucibles* gives more weight to distributed AI ownership through open infrastructure, open models, and legal limits on private control of the models and infrastructure that supply AI services. Plan A has an ownership mechanism too: its Compute Dividend Corporation receives a fixed share of permit fees, and each US citizen legally owns one share. That claim sits downstream from monitoring, so capacity that escapes the permit system can also escape the dividend.

I think the two maps expose separate failure modes. Verification can limit dangerous, undeclared scaling, but it does not by itself decide who receives the economic gain. Distributed ownership can create a wider claim on that gain, but it does not stop a hidden project from building a dangerous system. I don’t know whether either institutional package could survive the geopolitical pressure imagined here. A plan that cannot state how both failures are detected and acted on is incomplete before the first crisis arrives.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Monitoring protects the boundary around advanced-AI capacity; ownership protects the claim on what that capacity produces. Success on one side cannot compensate for an undefined or unenforceable system on the other.</p></div>

## A credible plan needs separate public records

The comparison becomes useful when the two problems are documented separately. At country level, a capacity record should name the asset, owner and operator, authorized processing capacity, inspection method and date, tolerated unaccounted capacity, and breach response. Plan A depicts each side becoming confident that the other hides no more than about 1 percent of AI computing capacity, which shows what a tolerance can look like. A separate claim record should name the revenue base, payer, formula, beneficiaries, transfer schedule, independent audit, and appeal process.

A company board has a narrower mandate. It cannot create a national Citizen’s Dividend or verify another country’s chip stockpile. For a specific automation decision, it can record the baseline period, unit of output, minimum improvement that justifies deployment, maximum error rate that stops it, evidence owner, provider and system version, and workforce-consultation trigger. It can also secure the contractual right to pause or terminate deployment, set restart conditions, and define how measured gains are shared with named beneficiaries.

Those records make failure observable. Control fails when capacity above the threshold operates outside the record or cannot be stopped on trigger. Distribution fails when measured gains rise but no law or contract transfers the defined share to named beneficiaries. My take is that the documents become strategically useful here: they raise the burden from agreeing with the destination to specifying who can act, what can be measured, and which right survives pressure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Advanced-AI strategy becomes credible only when public authorities can account for dangerous capacity, named beneficiaries hold an auditable claim on machine-created value, and organizations document the powers they actually control.</p></div>
