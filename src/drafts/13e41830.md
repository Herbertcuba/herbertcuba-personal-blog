In 1976, Alaska voters changed the state constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenues would enter the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/) instead of disappearing into annual spending. The fund invests that resource wealth. Since 1982, its returns have helped finance the Permanent Fund Dividend, an annual cash payment to eligible residents. Alaska created a public claim on the oil money before most of it arrived.

Fifty years later, the same choice appears in two documents about AI. [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), a conditional scenario and policy proposal published in July 2026, uses Alaska as a model for sharing AI-created wealth. The framework of my 2026 speculative book, [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), was in writing by February 3, more than six months before this article. Their independent agreement suggests that public finance may have to move from taxing human work toward giving people a direct claim on machine output.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows why ownership rules are strongest when written before a new source of wealth becomes difficult to divide.</p></div>

## What AI 2040 is trying to do

The AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit focused on advanced AI and policy, published Plan A on July 9, 2026. Four of its six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's 2025 scenario about a rapid race toward advanced AI. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.”

Its imagined sequence starts with a United States-China agreement in 2029 and near-automated AI research in 2030. By mid-2031, AI performs a third of cognitive labor, meaning work based mainly on reasoning and information. The scenario then projects roughly 50 percent inflation-adjusted growth in 2032, a pause at top-human-expert capability in 2035, and superintelligence in 2040. Here, superintelligence means systems that outperform the best humans across essentially all tasks while working faster and more cheaply.

Plan A combines safety controls with citizen ownership. Governments track chips, inspect data centers, and issue permits for robots and compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI. A Compute Dividend Corporation gives every US citizen one share; its allocation of compute-permit fees rises from 25 percent in 2032 to 75 percent in 2035. The returns fund a Citizen's Dividend, a direct payment to adults.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A imagines a managed rise to superintelligence in which verification limits danger and citizen ownership spreads part of the return.</p></div>

## The three crucibles describe the social passage

My book approaches the transition through three stages:

1. **The Correction.** AI lowers the price of cognitive work, so employment becomes a weaker way to distribute income and collect tax.
2. **The Void.** The old bargain between work and security breaks before a replacement is stable, making a basic income or Citizen's Dividend necessary to support households and demand.
3. **The Alignment.** Society decides whether AI abundance is held in trust for many people or concentrated among a small group of owners.

The overlap is unusually specific. Both documents expect labor income to lose ground, use the name “Citizen's Dividend,” and cite Alaska. Both also address ownership: my book argues for distributed AI ownership and taxes on AI labor, while Plan A assigns citizens shares funded through permits on scarce machine capacity. The mechanisms differ, but people gain a claim on production that does not depend entirely on having a job.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The crucibles follow the breakdown of the labor bargain, the unstable gap that follows, and the choice between shared stewardship and concentrated power.</p></div>

## The comparison produces a testable forecast

I call the shared signal the public-finance pivot. Before any million-dollar dividend or superintelligence, governments should struggle to tax wages as more value moves into AI infrastructure. Their response should appear in permits, taxes, or public equity tied to compute and robots. If AI-driven output rises while labor income and the wage-tax base remain stable, the thesis loses force.

The difference is mainly one of emphasis. Plan A develops verification through chip tracking, inspections, agreements, and pauses. Its Appendix D stress-tests this system with a hypothetical covert Chinese project; the authors give it less than a 10 percent chance of reaching AI that outperforms top human experts undetected before 2043. This is not a report of a known project. *The Three Crucibles* concentrates on legitimacy, demand, and ownership as the economic bargain moves.

For a Nordic strategist, the test is whether public budgets remain tied to wages while AI-created value accumulates in compute infrastructure owned elsewhere. Verification asks whether agreed limits are being followed. A public claim decides who benefits if the growth arrives. We need both questions.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Watch the tax base before the capability headlines: a gap between AI-driven output and labor-funded revenue would support the shared thesis.</p></div>

## Preparation without prophecy

No one knows what waits for us. I do not know whether Plan A's timeline will hold, and its authors do not claim certainty. Yet if the AI Futures Project and I are directionally right, the road will be bumpy because jobs, taxes, ownership, and political legitimacy will move at different speeds.

That is why *The Three Crucibles* is a preparation document. It names the passage early enough to discuss the institutions we may need before pressure turns design into emergency. Alaska's deeper lesson is that deciding who holds a claim is easier before the money arrives.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Uncertainty is a reason to prepare the ownership and governance choices before the transition forces them on us.</p></div>
