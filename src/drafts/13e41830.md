In 1976, Alaska's voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a public investment fund that receives at least 25 percent of certain mineral revenues. The first dividend followed in 1982. Eligible residents now receive an annual cash dividend funded by the fund's investment earnings, giving the public a lasting claim on a valuable asset.

Half a century later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) uses Alaska as a precedent. It would direct a fixed share of permit-fee revenue from large amounts of computing capacity and robots into a corporation owned equally by citizens, then pay its profits as a Citizen's Dividend. My speculative book, [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), reaches a similar idea from another direction.

The book was written more than six months before this article, and its public page was online by March 2026, before Plan A appeared in July. That timing does not make either account correct. AI 2040 is a policy scenario rather than empirical evidence, but the shared sequence is specific enough to examine.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows how citizens can retain a claim on shared wealth; both works apply that principle to an AI-shaped economy.</p></div>

## AI 2040 proposes a controlled passage

The [AI Futures Project](https://ai-2040.com/about), a research nonprofit focused on advanced AI, calls Plan A “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Four of its six authors also developed [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), their 2025 scenario about rapid AI development and the risk of losing control.

Plan A imagines an agreement between the United States and China in 2029, followed by limits, inspections, public declarations of major infrastructure purchases, and shared research information. Together these create verification, practical ways for each side to check compliance. The scenario pauses around AI matching leading human experts in 2035, then proceeds in 2040 to superintelligence: systems better than the best humans at essentially every task while also faster and cheaper.

The economic mechanism matters as much as the dates. As automation shifts income away from wages, labor-tax revenue weakens. Governments would charge permit fees for scarce compute, the chips and datacenter capacity used to build and run AI, and for robots; part of the fees would return to citizens. That route depends on international limits holding and the fees being collectable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A would slow the race, verify compliance, and turn scarce AI capacity into public revenue before advancing to superintelligence.</p></div>

## The three crucibles describe the social passage

My book divides the transition into three crucibles. **The Correction** begins when AI-driven productivity reduces demand for human labor and exposes an economy built around wages. **The Transformation** is the rebuilding period, when income support, a Citizen's Dividend, and taxes on work performed by AI systems keep demand and public finances functioning. **The Alignment** is the longer choice over who owns and directs the AI systems producing the wealth: broad participation or concentrated control.

Only after those three movements are clear does the comparison become useful. Both works expect the social problem to arrive before superintelligence, while AI still operates within the human range. We may have to repair distribution before we know how capable AI will become.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction exposes the wage-based system, the Transformation rebuilds distribution, and the Alignment decides who controls wealth-producing AI.</p></div>

## Their agreement has a boundary

Both works connect automation, a direct public claim on new wealth, and an eventual struggle over control. They also use the name Citizen's Dividend and cite Alaska. The term is older than either document, so the meaningful convergence lies in the sequence rather than a claim of invention.

Their strongest difference is institutional. Plan A puts much of its weight on verification between states; my book puts more on ownership that survives broken rules or owners moving investment across borders. Plan A even estimates below a 10 percent chance that a covert project could stay hidden long enough to reach AI that outperforms leading human experts before 2043. Verification can expose a broken limit, but it cannot distribute the gains from lawful AI production.

The economic warning is testable. Use 2026 as a baseline and compare annual figures for the United States and the European Union through 2030. It gains weight if output per worker rises while both the share of national income paid to workers and labor-tax receipts relative to the economy fall for three consecutive years. If productivity rises and both shares stay at or above the baseline, the warning weakens.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works share a sequence but trust different safeguards: Plan A emphasizes verifiable limits, while The Three Crucibles emphasizes ownership.</p></div>

## Preparation without certainty

I do not know whether Plan A's dates will survive limits in chip factories and energy supply, geopolitics, or ordinary institutional failure. Its authors do not offer them as settled facts. The convergence with my book is therefore a warning, not a victory lap.

If these accounts are directionally right, the road will become bumpy well before 2040. That is why *The Three Crucibles* is a preparation document. Its value does not depend on guessing the year of superintelligence; it lies in asking which institutions must still work if wages stop carrying income, tax, and social legitimacy. We cannot wait for certainty before preparing for that possibility.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>No one knows the route to 2040. The shared warning is to prepare institutions for economic disruption before the technical future is settled.</p></div>
