A society can become much richer while ordinary people become less necessary to producing that wealth. That paradox sits beneath the louder argument about superintelligence. Capability gets the headlines, while the social contract absorbs the consequences.

Alaska settled a smaller version of this problem before today’s AI debate. In 1976, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), reserving at least a quarter of specified mineral revenues for a public investment fund. Its first resident dividend was paid in 1982. The principle is simple: when a scarce resource creates extraordinary wealth, citizens can hold a direct claim on part of it.

That mechanism now appears in two works published several months apart: [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), a 2026 scenario and policy proposal from the nonprofit AI Futures Project, and [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my 2026 speculative book. Both call it a Citizen’s Dividend: a direct payment linking citizens to AI-generated wealth. Both point to Alaska. This does not make either map correct, but it makes the shared question harder to dismiss.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows the principle behind both works: extraordinary machine wealth raises an ownership question before it raises a distribution question.</p></div>

## First, what the two maps actually say

AI 2040 describes itself as “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Four of its six authors also worked on the group’s 2025 scenario, AI 2027. Plan A imagines a US–China agreement in 2029, nearly automated AI research in 2030, a pause when systems reach roughly the capability of the best human experts in 2035, and a move to superintelligence in 2040. Here, superintelligence means systems that outperform the best humans across essentially every task, faster and more cheaply.

The economic break arrives earlier. In the scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive work by mid-2031, followed by about 50 percent real-GDP growth in 2032. Real GDP means economic output adjusted for inflation. Plan A proposes permits for compute—the processing capacity behind advanced AI—and robots. Every US citizen would receive one share in a Compute Dividend Corporation funded by a growing portion of compute-permit fees.

The Three Crucibles describes the same passage as three social tests:

**The Correction (2026)** is the shock. AI-driven productivity shifts bargaining power from labor toward capital and weakens the wage-based economic bargain.

**The Transformation (2027–2032)** is the rebuild. Universal basic income stabilizes demand, while a Citizen’s Dividend and ownership reform give people a claim on machine output.

**The Alignment (2035+)** is the moral and political choice. Abundance can be stewarded broadly, or controlled by what the book calls “new feudal lords.”

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 supplies a geopolitical and technical scenario; the three crucibles describe the social transition that could unfold around it.</p></div>

## The overlap is a sequence, not a shared calendar

Both accounts describe the same order of events: first the link between work and income weakens; then society needs a new way to distribute purchasing power; finally it must decide who controls the productive system.

AI 2040 funds its dividend through permits on compute and robots. The Three Crucibles leans more heavily on AI-labor taxation—a levy on productive work performed by AI—and distributed ownership. Distributed ownership means citizens hold direct stakes or enforceable claims in the infrastructure producing the wealth.

Neither social dividends nor automation taxes began with these works. Their sharper common claim concerns timing: ownership and distribution must change while productive capacity accelerates, not after wages and labor taxes have already lost their central role.

Alaska proves the principle, not the blueprint. Oil stays where it is; compute can cross borders. If governments cannot enforce coordinated limits, permit revenue shrinks with them. A durable citizen claim may then require taxation, direct public ownership or another legal right to AI-derived wealth.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The maps converge on the social sequence but differ on how citizens should acquire a lasting claim on AI-generated wealth.</p></div>

## Safety and ownership are different problems

AI 2040 is mainly a theory of enforceable coordination. The Three Crucibles is mainly a theory of economic membership.

Plan A proposes supplier and datacenter declarations, inspections, chip tracking and transparent AI research. Its Appendix D even tests this system against a secret project. Within the scenario, an intelligence estimate gives that project a below-10-percent chance of reaching Top-Expert-Dominating AI undetected before 2043. The term means AI able to outperform the best human experts.

That estimate depends on visibility, cooperation and enforcement. My book begins from another risk: even controlled systems can leave most people without a claim on their output. Ownership cannot prevent loss of control, while verification cannot guarantee shared wealth. A serious response needs both because these failures can occur independently.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification addresses dangerous capability; ownership addresses concentrated wealth. Solving one does not solve the other.</p></div>

## Preparation without prophecy

No one knows whether the milestones in AI 2040 will arrive on schedule. Its mechanism may be wrong, and the crucibles may unfold at a different pace. Their convergence does not validate either calendar. It shows that the same institutional fracture is visible from two different directions.

From 2027 through 2030, watch the economy you care about. If labor productivity rises for two consecutive years while real median labor income and labor-tax receipts as a share of GDP fall, the warning becomes stronger. That pattern would not prove AI caused the split. If pay, public revenue and broad ownership rise alongside productivity, the thesis becomes weaker.

That is why my book is meant as a preparation document. If both maps are even directionally right, we should expect a bumpy road—not because abundance is impossible, but because technical capability may move faster than political legitimacy. We cannot settle the future in advance. We can decide whether ownership and verification are designed before a crisis forces the choice.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Prediction remains uncertain, but the work of preparing institutions for a break between productivity, income and ownership can begin now.</p></div>
