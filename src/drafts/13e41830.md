In 1976, Alaska voters changed the state constitution so part of the state's mineral revenue would enter the Alaska Permanent Fund. The state-owned fund invests a share of temporary oil wealth for the long term. Since 1982, its earnings have helped finance annual cash dividends for eligible residents.

That policy now sits inside two very different maps of an AI future. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a July 2026 scenario and policy proposal from the AI Futures Project, the research nonprofit behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its April 2025 scenario about rapid progress toward superhuman AI. Plan A explicitly compares its proposed Compute Dividend Corporation with Alaska's fund.

The framework in [my 2026 speculative book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), was public on my site by March 2026. The Correction is the break in the link between human labor, income, and demand. The Void is the unstable period after that link begins to fail, when distribution has to be rebuilt through measures such as a citizen dividend. The Alignment is the political choice between broad stewardship and concentrated ownership, rather than the technical task of making an AI system follow human intentions.

AI 2040 reaches many of the same pressure points from another direction. The overlap does not prove that either map is right, but it gives us a sharper picture of what may be coming.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows how citizens can hold a claim on resource wealth. Both AI 2040 and my three-stage framework apply that principle to an economy shaped by AI.</p></div>

## What Plan A proposes

The authors call Plan A “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Their path starts with a US-China agreement in 2029 and adds research transparency and international oversight. Development pauses in 2035 near the level of the best human experts, then moves toward superintelligence in 2040: systems that outperform the best humans across tasks, faster and at lower cost.

To manage the economic shock, Plan A proposes tradable permits, which are rights that firms can buy and sell, for building robots or computing capacity. Compute means the processing power used to train and run AI. In its American model, a majority of robot- and compute-permit fees funds a citizen dividend for all American adults. Separately, every US citizen owns one share in a Compute Dividend Corporation that receives a fixed fraction of compute-permit fees.

That model depends on tracked chips, inspected data centers, and disclosed research. Plan A tests this logic with a covert Chinese project, showing how secret capacity could weaken an international agreement.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A combines a managed path to superintelligence with verified limits on AI capacity and a public claim on some of the wealth that capacity creates.</p></div>

## Where the three stages converge

The Correction matches Plan A's warning about the tax base. When machines replace paid human work, governments collect less revenue from wages while a larger share of production moves to capital. The old bargain between employment, consumption, and public finance starts to weaken.

The Void is where the resemblance becomes specific. Both documents use the term “Citizen's Dividend,” and both point to Alaska as a precedent. My book proposes taxes on AI labor as one source of funding; Plan A sells permits for robots and compute. The mechanisms differ, but the social purpose is similar: preserve broad purchasing power when wages can no longer carry the economy alone.

The Alignment also appears in both maps. Plan A tries to prevent AI power and its gains from becoming dangerously concentrated. My framework asks whether society produces stewards of abundance or a new class of feudal owners. In each case, the technology problem becomes a question of political legitimacy.

This is meaningful convergence, not validation. AI 2040 is a constructed scenario rather than economic evidence, and agreement between two uncertain maps cannot turn speculation into fact.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The maps agree on the break, the need for new distribution, and the final struggle over control, even though they fund and govern the transition differently.</p></div>

## Where the maps part

Plan A places enormous weight on verification. Treaties work only if governments can see relevant chips, data centers, and research activity well enough to detect cheating. My book puts more weight on distributed ownership: citizens need an enforceable claim on AI's productive output, not merely confidence that powerful actors followed the rules.

These are different safeguards. Verification can limit hidden capacity without deciding who benefits from permitted capacity. Ownership can spread the benefits while doing too little to stop an unsafe system. I do not know what balance can carry both burdens, and Plan A does not claim certainty either.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification addresses secret power; ownership addresses concentrated benefit. A stable transition probably needs both, although neither map proves how to achieve them.</p></div>

## What preparation means

Neither document is a timetable. Plan A depends on technical progress, diplomacy, and institutions that do not exist. The Three Crucibles names social pressures but cannot tell us when they arrive or how they interact.

My book was written as a preparation document for that uncertainty. If both maps are even directionally right, the road ahead is likely to be bumpy: the link between work and income may weaken before a replacement is ready, while control over AI grows faster than the institutions meant to legitimize it.

Alaska gave residents a claim on resource wealth without knowing every future dividend. AI presents a harder choice. Nobody knows what waits for us, but if Plan A and the three Crucibles are broadly right, the difficult transition will begin before society feels ready.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The value of both maps lies in preparation, not certainty. Their shared warning is that economic disruption may arrive before the institutions needed to distribute and govern AI wealth.</p></div>
