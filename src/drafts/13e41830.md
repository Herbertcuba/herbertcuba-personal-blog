Two AI maps reach the same economic problem. [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) starts with a US-China race toward superintelligence. [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) starts with firms replacing human work. Both ask how people keep buying machine output as wages and taxes shrink.

Four of Plan A's six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/): Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Eli Lifland, and Daniel Kokotajlo. They call Plan A a recommendation, separate from their best guess. I think the comparison is useful because independent routes reach the same structure, even if their calendars fail.

In *The Three Crucibles*, the Correction is the labor shock, the Transformation rebuilds demand through a Citizen's Dividend, and the Alignment decides who controls the abundance. Plan A reaches a similar fork through treaty-backed pauses and shared returns. My take is that the convergence supports the economic frame and the split reveals a deeper control problem.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both frameworks connect machine productivity to falling labor income and a Citizen's Dividend. Their disagreement begins with how society keeps control.</p></div>

## The economic chain is surprisingly close

Plan A imagines a 2029 US-China deal that slows takeoff, the speed at which capable AI improves. In its scenario, systems stay within the human range through 2035, pause at top-expert ability, then move toward superintelligence in 2040. By mid-2031, AI performs a third of cognitive labor (p. 13).

That shift weakens labor-tax revenue. Because the treaty caps new robot and compute capacity, the US sells tradable permits to expand it. The permits price scarce machine capacity, while auction revenue replaces part of the shrinking tax base. In the scenario, most of that money funds a Citizen's Dividend of $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million by 2035 (pp. 17–18).

| Question | AI 2040 | The Three Crucibles | Open gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Economic break | Labor taxes shrink | Labor income and demand weaken | How fast? |
| Distribution | Capacity permits fund a dividend | AI labor tax funds a dividend | Which mechanism works? |
| Control | Treaty inspection and research transparency | Distributed ownership and open infrastructure | Can people exercise choice? |

The book is blunt: “Robots do not buy iPhones” (p. 35). Both works cite Alaska. Plan A gives each citizen a share in a Compute Dividend Corporation and invokes the Alaska Permanent Fund (p. 18, fn. 49). The book cites Alaska's resident dividend, first paid in 1982 (p. 37). Alaska supplies the ownership precedent; the maps differ on how to collect the return.

The dated record is narrow: a [March 23, 2026 repository commit](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) added the book PDF, while a [July 9, 2026 Hacker News submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) linked to Plan A. This establishes the order of those public events. I think the record still matters because an external scenario put the same economic chain under stress.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The maps share a demand problem and a dividend response. Plan A prices new machine capacity, while The Three Crucibles taxes deployed AI labor.</p></div>

## Verification leaves an ownership gap

Plan A is strongest on verification, which means checking whether declared chips, datacenters, and research activity match reality. This addresses a state-level race toward unsafe systems, a problem that an enterprise contract is powerless to solve.

Its own Appendix D shows the limit. In a fictional branch, China hides 500,000 H100-equivalents in tunnels near the Medog Hydropower Station, with roughly 200 people aware. Like the hidden machinery in *Andor*, the project operates outside the visible system. The literal lesson is that treaty monitoring can catch declared facilities while missing concealed compute.

The book answers another failure with distributed ownership, open models, and open infrastructure (p. 56). Ownership gives people a claim on returns; openness can expand buyer choice. A transparent supplier can still hold power through proprietary records and costly contract terms.

I don't know which failure arrives first, but I think they need different defenses. Verification checks powerful development; ownership spreads the benefit. I would add operational reversibility, meaning proof that an institution can move its working service to another provider.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A tackles secret national development, while the book tackles concentrated economic power. Buyer control adds a practical question: can the service actually move?</p></div>

## Run the exit test before renewal

The book's insurer thought experiment makes the risk concrete. One company keeps 5,000 claims adjusters, while a rival deploys AI agents, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months (p. 20). The efficiency is attractive, but the claims operation may become inseparable from its supplier.

At renewal, a business owner should test one claims workflow. Export case records, the decision log, model or prompt version, tool permissions, and a claims sample with documented acceptable outcomes. A replacement team replays the sample and restores service within an agreed time and cost. If it cannot reproduce the sampled decisions under a documented setup, the exit route has failed.

The timing is immediate in Europe. The bulk of the [EU AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai) started applying on August 2, 2026; general-purpose AI obligations had applied since August 2, 2025. Depending on the system and role, the rules can require documentation, logging, oversight, and risk management. Transparency tells us what we can inspect. Exit rights show whether we retain agency when a supplier changes the terms.

A successful handover by another provider would weaken my claim that transparent AI can still leave buyers trapped. That is the point of a useful strategic frame: it should survive a real test. I think the leverage exists before renewal, while the contract and operating model can still change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A live handover tests whether supplier choice is real. The machines will be built, and our freedom to leave has to be built with them.</p></div>
