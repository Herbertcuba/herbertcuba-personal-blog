AI can keep production rising while the income attached to human work falls. That is the economic fork shared by two works published months apart in 2026.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, a forecasting group focused on advanced AI. Four of its six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), a 2025 scenario about a race toward systems beyond top human ability. Plan A proposes an international route for governing superintelligence, meaning AI far more capable than the best people across most intellectual work, but its authors call it a recommendation rather than their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my book about automation, labor, and ownership. Its PDF entered my [public repository](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) on March 23, 2026; this edition is dated June 24. A [public link to Plan A](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) followed on July 9. That establishes sequence, not private origins.

Both works call their proposed public payment from AI-era production a Citizen's Dividend. Both point to the Alaska Permanent Fund, a public savings fund created by constitutional amendment in 1976 to preserve part of the state's oil wealth. It began paying residents annual dividends in 1982, giving the public a financial claim on income from a shared resource.

I think this specific overlap is meaningful thematic convergence, not one-to-one validation. The disagreement offers the more useful frame: verification constrains hidden AI capacity, ownership determines who receives the gains from lawful capacity, and exit proof tests whether an institution can use its bargaining power when a supplier relationship stops working.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Two independently developed maps connect advanced automation to lost labor income and a public dividend. Their divergence separates three controls that are often blurred together: evidence about capacity, claims on its returns, and the practical right to change suppliers.</p></div>

## The maps meet in the Void

The [opening map in the June 24 edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment. The Correction begins when AI raises the return to capital and weakens the value of human labor. The Void follows when production recovers without restoring jobs, leaving households with less income to buy its output. Transformation is the chapter inside the Void that develops the economic response; it is not the canonical name of the second crucible.

Plan A puts scenario numbers on that chain. By mid-2031, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, meaning work based mainly on analysis, communication, and professional judgment. As jobs disappear, personal-income and payroll-tax revenue contracts. The United States in the scenario sells permits to build computing capacity and robots, then directs most proceeds to a Citizen's Dividend projected at $45,000 per adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035 ([PDF pages 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17)).

The funding mechanisms differ. The book's [Transformation chapter](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36) proposes charging for each human-equivalent unit of deployed AI labor, or the work a person would otherwise perform. Plan A charges for permission to add machines. Both answer the same demand problem: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Cheap production cannot sustain an economy if households lose the income needed to buy it.

The Alignment asks where the new wealth goes. Plan A's public Compute Dividend Corporation gives each US citizen one share and pays out most permit revenue. The book calls for distributed ownership of AI capacity and its returns. I think the works share the distribution problem, while diverging on timing and how the dividend is funded.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction, the Void, and the Alignment map onto Plan A's automation, tax-base, dividend, and concentration concerns. The closest match sits inside the Void, where lost labor income leads both works to a Citizen's Dividend, although one funds it through AI-labor charges and the other through permits for new machines.</p></div>

## Verification cannot settle ownership

The comparison breaks at the technical control problem. Plan A models takeoff, when AI-assisted research could improve AI faster than governments can respond. In its scenario, a US-China agreement in 2029 averts superintelligence by the end of 2030; systems then scale within the human range, pause at top-expert level in 2035, and resume toward superintelligence in 2040. *The Three Crucibles* does not model that race or systems escaping human control.

Plan A's answer is verification: evidence that declared chips, data centers, and research on frontier AI, the most capable systems under development, match reality. It proposes declarations for major transactions, chip tracking, reciprocal infrastructure inspections, and almost entirely visible research. A secret project may hide its purpose, but it still needs hardware, electricity, cooling, and construction.

[Appendix D](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45) tests that bet by imagining a Chinese covert data center beside the proposed Medog Hydropower Station, a huge dam project in China's Medog region. Its planned electricity could power the machines, while legitimate construction could mask the secret site. The center contains 500,000 H100-equivalents, a capacity unit that uses the performance of one Nvidia H100 as its benchmark. Nvidia is a US AI-chip designer; its H100 is a high-end data-center processor. Within the scenario, the chance of this project producing AI better than top human experts across domains without detection before 2043 stays below 10 percent.

This is the Death Star problem from *Rogue One*, the 2016 *Star Wars* film about a hidden planet-destroying weapon. The fictional Empire can conceal the project's purpose, but not every trace of the materials and energy needed to build it. Plan A bets that inspectors can likewise find a secret AI project's industrial footprint.

The book's [Alignment chapter](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57) addresses a different danger. A data center can be declared, inspected, and legal while control and income remain concentrated. The book calls for distributed AI ownership, open infrastructure, open models, and laws against a private intelligence monopoly. Open, here, means giving independent operators the model and operating components needed to inspect and run the service without depending on the original provider.

My take is that inspection makes hidden capacity harder to build, while ownership determines where visible gains go. I don't know which control will face the harder test. I think both are necessary, but neither proves that a buyer can move a working AI service.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses declarations, tracking, and inspections to expose concealed computing capacity. The book asks the question those tools cannot answer: who controls lawful capacity and receives what it earns? Neither control proves that an institution can move its running systems to another provider.</p></div>

## Exit proof tests bargaining power

The last problem appears at the contract desk. *The Three Crucibles* compares [two fictional insurers](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21): one keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; the other deploys a coordinated AI system, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The thought experiment shows how competition can force adoption before ownership and governance are settled.

Once a service is live, its supplier may control the working setup, data connections, permissions, and connected tools. A contract can promise choice while those dependencies prevent another team from keeping the service alive. If the buyer cannot switch, power concentrates one contract at a time.

I call the practical test exit proof: a funded handover in which an independent team must reproduce one working AI service within agreed limits. At renewal, the chief information officer should own a 30-day test of one live workflow and one complete data export, with the business owner approving the result. The supplier funds it; the buyer owns the evaluation set, a collection of past cases and accepted outcomes that defines “working.”

The handover package should identify the model version, standing instructions that guide its behavior, information sources, permissions, connected tools, saved state needed to resume work, and rebuilding steps. It should show what each tool expects and returns, plus where important inputs came from and how they changed. The replacement may alter the model, instructions, source-finding method, or workflow, provided it preserves the business outcome within the deadline, error limit, and cost ceiling.

Failure must change the deal. If the replacement cannot replay the evaluation cases or misses an agreed limit, automatic renewal stops, access continues during transition, and the supplier funds repair and handover support. Exit proof then becomes an acceptance test rather than another contractual promise that the service can be moved.

On August 2, 2026, most of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's law for AI systems, began applying. Article 50 requires disclosure for certain AI interactions and AI-generated or manipulated content. That can reveal AI use, but cannot prove a service will survive a supplier change. I think a failed handover predicts dependence better than compliant disclosure proves freedom.

Alaska gave residents an early claim on resource wealth. The machines will be built; what remains open is who can inspect them, who shares in their gains, and who can walk away.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Make renewal conditional on a supplier-funded, timed handover with buyer-owned test cases and contractual consequences for failure. If a replacement team cannot reproduce the business result, the buyer is locked in even when every disclosure is compliant and every right to leave looks valid on paper.</p></div>
