Fifty years ago, Alaska put oil wealth into a public fund. Residents began receiving dividends in 1982. Two AI maps now return to that precedent as they ask who receives the value when machines perform more of the work.

Of the six authors behind [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), four also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/). They call Plan A a recommendation rather than their best forecast. [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) starts inside firms, yet it also connects falling labor income to a Citizen's Dividend and returns to Alaska.

A [March 23, 2026 commit](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) added the book PDF to cubagarcia.com's public repository; a [July 9 Hacker News submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) later linked Plan A. Those dates show sequence alone. I think the stronger evidence is the shared economic chain. My take is that their disagreement is more useful, because one map controls development while the other distributes ownership.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>From different starting points, the two maps reach the same dividend response to weaker labor income. They split over control of the systems producing that wealth.</p></div>

## The same economic chain appears twice

Plan A starts with a 2029 US-China agreement that slows takeoff, the speed at which capable AI improves. In the scenario, the deal averts a 2030 automated research sprint, lets AI spread while its abilities stay within the human range, and caps it around the best human-expert level in 2035. That limit holds until a permitted move toward superintelligence in 2040 (p. 4).

By mid-2031, AI performs a third of cognitive labor in the scenario. As labor-tax revenue shrinks, the US auctions permits for new robots and computing capacity. Most fees fund a Citizen's Dividend projected at $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million by 2035 (pp. 13, 17–18).

The book names three stages. The Correction is competition pushing down the value of human labor; the Transformation rebuilds demand as wages weaken; the Alignment decides who owns productive intelligence. “Robots do not buy iPhones,” it says before proposing a Citizen's Dividend and an AI Labor Equivalence Tax (pp. 35–36). I think this link between wages and demand can survive large errors in either calendar. The funding choice remains open: permits price new capacity, while the book proposes a tax on deployed AI labor.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both maps expect automation to weaken labor-based income and public revenue. Plan A funds citizens through capacity permits, while The Three Crucibles proposes a tax on deployed AI labor.</p></div>

## Their control systems solve different failures

Plan A relies on verification: evidence that declared chips, datacenters, and research activity match reality. Major chip transactions are declared, rival powers inspect infrastructure, and frontier research becomes almost entirely transparent (pp. 7, 11). This is a serious answer to a state racing toward unsafe systems.

Its covert-project appendix tests the limit. In one scenario branch, China hides roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents, a measure of computing power based on Nvidia's H100 chip, in tunnels near the Medog Hydropower Station. Around 200 people know. The mechanism is pure *Andor*: compartmentalization lets a hidden cell build outside the official map. Still, the report puts the project's chance of reaching top-expert AI undetected before 2043 below 10 percent (Appendix D, pp. 45–50).

The Three Crucibles focuses on distributed ownership, open infrastructure, and legal limits on private control (p. 56). That gives citizens a claim on output, while Plan A helps governments inspect dangerous development. I don't know which failure will arrive first. I think both controls are necessary because visibility can expose hidden build-up, while ownership determines who receives the economic gains.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification constrains secret frontier development. Distributed ownership constrains economic concentration, so the two controls cover different ways AI power can escape public control.</p></div>

## Ownership needs an exit proof

The book makes this concrete with an insurer thought experiment. One company keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. A rival deploys AI agents, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months (p. 20). If its claims service depends on one provider, frontier-chip transparency gives the buyer no way to move records or keep operating.

I would add a buyer-level control called an exit proof: a timed handover showing that a working service can move within an agreed cost and recovery time. Before renewal, a named owner runs one bounded claims migration. The export includes records and event history, model or prompt versions, evaluation results, tool interfaces, permissions, and a tested recovery guide. Formats that only the current provider can read fail.

The claim being tested is narrow: this workflow remains dependent on its current provider despite available documentation. A replacement team that can replay sampled decisions and restore the service disproves that claim. A failed replay shows where the dependency still lives.

The bulk of the [EU AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai) started applying on August 2, 2026; general-purpose-model obligations started a year earlier. [Article 50](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50) adds transparency duties for certain systems. Those duties improve inspection, while an export specification and timed migration exercise must still come from the contract. AI transparency tells us what we can inspect; exit proof shows whether we can change suppliers and keep operating. I think this will become Europe's next AI fault line, because ownership becomes real only when institutions can exercise it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>At renewal, require a timed handover that another team can replay and restore. A successful drill disproves supplier dependence for that workflow; a failure reveals the specific records, rights, or process still trapping it.</p></div>
