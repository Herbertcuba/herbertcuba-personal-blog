In [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), one insurer keeps 5,000 claims adjusters while a rival uses AI agents, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months (p. 20). The thought experiment exposes a loop: if every company follows the winner, who keeps the income to buy machine output?

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) scales that loop to the world economy. Four of its six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), and they frame Plan A as a recommendation separate from their best-guess forecast. I think its value lies in that independent structure.

They reveal three controls: state verification for secret frontier development, public distribution and ownership for machine wealth, and institutional exit for supplier dependence. Confusing these levels gives any single control too much credit.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Automation can raise output while weakening labor income and demand. The comparison separates three failures often hidden inside governance.</p></div>

## They converge on the demand problem

Plan A imagines a 2029 US-China agreement that slows takeoff, the speed at which capable AI improves. Systems stay within the human range through 2035, pause at top-expert AI, comparable to the best human experts, and then move toward superintelligence in 2040 (p. 4). By mid-2031, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor in the scenario (p. 13).

The United States auctions permits for new robots and computing capacity, directing most fees to a Citizen's Dividend. The scenario projects $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million by 2035 (pp. 17–18).

The book traces the same cause through three crucibles. The Correction reduces labor's economic value, while the Transformation moves part of the income created by AI infrastructure into households through a Citizen's Dividend, preserving buyers for the output. “Robots do not buy iPhones” is the blunt version (p. 35). The Alignment asks whether abundance produces broad stewardship or concentrated rule.

Both cite Alaska's oil dividend, yet their funding methods differ. Plan A prices construction capacity; the book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax and distributed AI ownership (pp. 36, 56). I think the dates may prove wrong while the wage-and-demand mechanism remains testable through employment income, tax receipts, and household spending.

A [March 23 repository commit](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) contains the book PDF; a [July 9 public submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) later links Plan A. These records establish the sequence, while AI 2040's first release remains unverified. The scenario offers independent convergence; empirical proof must come later.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both connect automation to weaker labor income and answer with a Citizen's Dividend. Plan A uses capacity permits; the book favors a deployed-AI tax and distributed ownership.</p></div>

## Their controls solve different failures

Plan A's governance bet is verification: evidence that declared chips, datacenters, and research on the most capable systems match reality. It calls for declarations of major chip transactions, mutual inspections, and almost complete transparency for frontier research (pp. 7, 11). This state-level control targets a hidden race toward systems that could escape human control.

Appendix D imagines China hiding roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents of computing capacity in tunnels near the Medog Hydropower Station, with around 200 people aware. Like *Rogue One*, a huge program stays obscure because most participants see fragments while a small circle sees the whole. Physical infrastructure still leaves traces, and Plan A puts the chance of reaching top-expert AI undetected before 2043 below 10 percent (pp. 45–50).

The book addresses economic concentration. A declared and inspected AI economy can still direct most gains to a narrow group, so its dividend shapes distribution while distributed ownership shapes control. A third failure sits inside the institution: a company can support both and still depend on a supplier it cannot replace. Exit proof is the narrower operational control.

I don't know which failure will dominate, but I think each control should be judged against its own target. My take is that verification hinders a secret frontier build, ownership sets the economic claim, and exit proof reveals whether a buyer can act when a supplier changes the terms.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification constrains secret frontier development, while distribution and ownership allocate machine wealth. Supplier exit covers a third failure by testing whether an institution can survive a broken commercial dependency.</p></div>

## Put exit proof into the renewal gate

Before renewing one critical AI service, the insurer chooses a single claims workflow, names a business owner, and sets a recovery deadline and cost ceiling. A replacement team receives enough context to reproduce the work: source data, decision records, model and prompt versions, test results, system interfaces, access rules, and transition instructions.

The team must restore the workflow and reproduce sampled decisions under the documented setup. Unusable records, missing permissions, or recovery beyond the agreed boundary disprove the portability claim. A successful drill proves something narrower: this workflow can move under these conditions.

For European buyers, [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50) has applied since August 2, 2026. It creates disclosure duties for certain AI interactions and generated content, helping people identify AI involvement. Usable exports and transition help remain contract matters.

Transparency tells us what we can inspect; an exit proof tells us whether agency survives a supplier change. I think boards should demand that evidence before approving a critical AI renewal. By 2040, the machines may be extraordinary, but the leverage remains ordinary: a tested right to leave while the buyer can still negotiate it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An exit proof is a bounded handover with a failure condition. It turns supplier choice into evidence that a workflow can move.</p></div>
