Alaska settled an ownership question before its oil income became routine. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was being built to carry North Slope oil, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/) and reserved part of the US state's mineral revenue. The first resident dividend followed in 1982. The public claim came first.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a policy recommendation told through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and proposes ways to navigate it. It follows [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's April 2025 scenario, and four of that work's five authors also contributed to Plan A. The authors explicitly say Plan A is primarily a recommendation rather than their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my speculative book about AI's economic transition, has a [retail publication date of March 22, 2026](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GTK53C9H), and its PDF was public on my site by March 23. A [public link to Plan A appeared on July 9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). That sequence establishes publication order. It does not establish influence or predictive accuracy.

I think the narrower comparison is more useful. The two works approach the future from different directions, yet converge on an economic break and a public dividend before separating over power. Their disagreement exposes three questions often compressed into the word *governance*: can states detect hidden computing capacity, who holds a legal claim on AI-generated gains, and can an institution prove that a critical process will survive a change of supplier?

| Test | Failure it addresses | Evidence required | Decision owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Infrastructure verification | Undeclared large-scale computing capacity | Transaction records and physical inspection | States, regulators, or treaty bodies |
| Economic ownership | AI-generated gains accrue to a narrow group | A legally enforceable claim on output or revenue | Legislatures and the citizens or asset owners named in law |
| Supplier exit | A vital process cannot move away from one supplier | A replacement team completes a live handover against buyer-owned cases | The accountable business executive and chief information officer |

Their overlap is thematic rather than predictive: both connect automated knowledge work to a public dividend, while their differences separate the ability to inspect infrastructure, hold a claim on its gains, and leave a supplier.</p></div>

## The economic break is the real convergence

The public book page, which is authoritative for my taxonomy, names the First Crucible as [The Correction](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), the Second as The Transformation, and the Third as The Alignment. The Correction describes inflated AI expectations meeting uneven returns, followed by company failures and stronger pressure to automate. Plan A has no close equivalent because it begins with geopolitical rivalry and a race to improve advanced AI. The works start in different places.

They meet in The Transformation. My book imagines output recovering while many jobs do not and states the demand problem plainly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” In Plan A's scenario, AI performs [one third of all cognitive labor by mid-2031](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=13), meaning work based mainly on information, analysis, communication, and judgment. The scenario then projects about 50 percent real economic growth in 2032 even as automation erodes the tax base.

The mechanism matters because production and purchasing power can move in opposite directions. As wages disappear, governments collect less personal-income and payroll tax. Companies can also expense or depreciate spending on data centers, factories, and robots, which lowers taxable profit during the build-out. Output may rise quickly while household demand and public revenue weaken.

Both works answer with a Citizen's Dividend, a public payment tied to AI-driven productivity. [My book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37), based on what a person doing equivalent work would have contributed through taxes and social payments. [Plan A proposes tradable permits](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17) instead: the state would issue limited permissions to add robot or computing capacity, builders could buy and sell them, and part of the permit revenue would go to a corporation owned equally by US citizens. In its scenario, the resulting dividend rises from $45,000 per American adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035, measured in 2025 dollars.

The Alignment carries the shared economic question into politics: will citizens become stewards of abundance, or will a narrow group control the productive system? Plan A also tries to spread the gains rather than allow irreversible concentration. I think this convergence matters because it includes the broken income loop, the Alaska precedent, and the same policy name. Yet “Citizen's Dividend” predates both works, and Plan A's figures remain projections. The overlap strengthens the question; it does not prove either timetable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The canonical sequence in my book is The Correction, The Transformation, and The Alignment. Plan A has no matching market correction, but both works reach the same income-and-tax problem and propose a Citizen's Dividend. They agree on the economic break while choosing different ways to fund the response.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership govern different failures

Plan A is much stronger on a technical danger that my book largely brackets. Its scenario has the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029. Without that agreement, the authors model fully automated AI research and superintelligence by the end of 2030. Under the deal, development slows, pauses at top-human-expert ability in 2035, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. *Takeoff* is the period when AI automates enough AI research to accelerate its own improvement; *superintelligence* means AI significantly better than the best humans across fields while also working faster and more cheaply. These are scenario dates, not promised events.

That acceleration requires a physical build-out. Plan A projects growth from roughly 20 million H100-equivalents in 2026 to 60 billion in 2034. An H100-equivalent measures AI computing performance against Nvidia's H100 processor, so it is a unit of capacity rather than a literal chip count. To constrain secret capacity, major data-center owners and chip suppliers would report large transactions, rival powers would inspect physical infrastructure, and AI research would become almost entirely transparent.

Plan A stress-tests that design with [an alternate covert-project branch](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45). In this fictional branch, China diverts roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents into tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station, a remote Chinese hydroelectric project whose power supply and underground setting make it useful as a concealment site in the scenario. About 200 people know. The example reveals the limit from inside Plan A's own argument: even an ambitious inspection system must account for collusion, missing records, and physical evidence that arrives too late.

The Alignment in my book asks a different question: who owns AI infrastructure and receives its output? Plan A gives citizens shares in the corporation receiving permit revenue, creating an economic claim without giving them direct ownership of chips, models, or AI companies. My proposal distributes infrastructure among individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, including AI models that can run on local hardware.

I don't know which balance could survive geopolitical pressure. I think the disagreement is more informative than a forced match. Plan A tackles secret capacity and loss of control, where my book offers no comparable plan for containing superintelligence. My book goes further on distributed ownership, which inspections cannot create. Detecting a breach does not decide who holds economic power afterward, and broad ownership cannot by itself contain a system more capable than its owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses public reporting, inspections, and transparent research to constrain a race toward superintelligence. The Alignment asks who owns AI infrastructure and its output. Each addresses a gap left by the other because observing concentrated power and distributing economic power are different jobs.</p></div>

## The third question begins at the contract desk

These state-level choices become immediate when a hospital or insurer renews an AI contract. My book illustrates the pressure with [two fictional insurers](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21). One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. The other uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that carry out parts of the claims process, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The numbers are illustrative, but the mechanism is clear: adoption can outrun a buyer's ability to reverse it.

My take is that exit proof should become mandatory when a business-critical AI workflow handles regulated decisions or an operationally essential process. Exit proof is a live handover test showing that a replacement team can operate the process to an agreed standard. Low-risk tools may need only a tested data export and documented settings. High-impact workflows need a full test with accountable business and technology owners, and that requirement must enter the contract at signing, while the buyer still has leverage.

A practical sequence is to classify the workflow, put a supplier-funded test and budget cap in the initial contract, schedule the test before renewal, and escalate only failed critical workflows. One workflow, one replacement team, and a 30-day window make a useful starting point because they limit cost and disruption while still exposing hidden dependencies. This is a proposed baseline, not a universal standard. The existing AI portfolio forum, with business, technology, procurement, legal, data, and risk represented, should resolve disputes. The accountable business executive judges the result; the chief information officer owns renewal and restoration of the original service if the test fails.

The acceptance record can remain short, but it must be precise. It should name buyer-owned input cases and expected outcomes, record the current supplier's performance on those same cases as the baseline, and identify the agreed person or system that evaluates the results. It should also set limits for error, completion time, and cost, state how long evidence will be kept, and name the person responsible for restoring service. This turns a vendor demonstration into a result that can be accepted or rejected.

The handover package should include workflow settings and their version history, model instructions, connections to other business systems, delegated permissions, source records, and recovery guidance. It also needs data lineage, the record of where inputs came from and how they changed. A replacement should fail even after producing an acceptable decision if it cannot reconstruct the source trail, uses permissions that were never delegated, or leaves the buyer unable to restore the original service safely. One failed test would show that a workable exit remains unproven, not that migration will always be impossible. It should trigger funded transition help before a retest.

This test extends familiar portability controls. Data portability moves files, source-code escrow releases software code under agreed conditions, and normal workflow migration moves known components. Exit proof examines the larger business claim: can another team operate the process to the accepted standard without a hidden dependence on the current supplier?

The [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law, shows why regulatory disclosure remains separate. Rules for general-purpose AI models, which can perform many tasks and be integrated into other systems, have applied since August 2, 2025; most of the Act began applying on August 2, 2026. Its documentation and transparency duties do not demonstrate that a buyer can transfer a working process.

I think exit proof fails as a test of bargaining power if a replacement reproduces the workflow but exclusive data, licensing terms, or scarce computing capacity still let the original supplier dictate the price. A buyer could therefore complete the technical handover and remain economically dependent. That result would not make the test useless. It would show exactly where technical portability ends and control over scarce inputs begins.

Alaska secured a public claim before oil income became routine. We still have the same choice with AI infrastructure: establish ownership claims and tested exit rights while the rules are fluid, or negotiate them after dependence has hardened. The hardware may arrive under either arrangement. The public claim will not appear unless we make it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof is a proposed supplier-funded handover test for critical AI workflows, agreed at signing and run before renewal against buyer-owned cases, measurable limits, traceable sources, and safe restoration. A technically successful handover can still leave the buyer captive to exclusive data, licensing, or scarce capacity, which reveals a separate economic dependency. AI infrastructure may become routine; ownership claims and practical exit rights will not secure themselves.</p></div>
