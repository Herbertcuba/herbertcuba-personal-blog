[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) gives policy machinery to a chain I set out in [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf): automated work can expand output while reducing labor income and weakening demand. The 2026 governance scenario comes from the AI Futures Project, a forecasting group whose six authors include four from *AI 2027*, its 2025 scenario of AI surpassing top human experts. They call Plan A a recommendation, not their best guess. I think its mechanisms must stand without faith in its dates.

Both works call one response a Citizen's Dividend and cite the Alaska Permanent Fund, a public oil-wealth fund created in 1976 and paying residents since 1982. Plan A puts capacity fees into a citizen-owned corporation; the book transfers AI-infrastructure gains to the people whose spending sustains demand.

The public record supports only a sequence. A book PDF entered my author-controlled [public repository on March 23, 2026](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8); the current edition is dated June 24. A [public Plan A link appeared on July 9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). That proves the PDF preceded the link, not either work's original release or private development. The convergence matters more than priority.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both frameworks connect automated work to weaker labor income and use an Alaska-style public fund to return resource or capacity revenue to citizens. The verified artifacts establish a public sequence, not independent origin or certain timelines.</p></div>

## The Void is the economic match

The book's three crucibles are the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment ([printed p. 5](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6)). The Correction begins when inflated expectations collapse and job-protecting inefficiency ends. In the Void, production recovers but jobs do not. Its Transformation chapter states the demand problem plainly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” As labor income falls, purchasing power follows, so the chapter proposes a Citizen's Dividend. The Alignment asks who owns the machines and distributes their abundance.

Plan A reaches the same chain through policy. By mid-2031, its AI does one-third of cognitive labor, shrinking payroll and income-tax receipts. The United States caps new robot and computing capacity, then sells permits: transferable rights to add a set amount of that scarce capacity. Most fees fund a Citizen's Dividend projected to rise from $45,000 per adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035, in real 2025 dollars ([pp. 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17)). The book instead proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax on each human-equivalent unit of deployed AI labor. I think the genuine match is the diagnosis and dividend, not the speed or funding method.

The book's fictional insurer example makes the pressure concrete. One company keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; another uses AI, cuts staff by 40 percent and premiums by 20 percent, then wins within six months ([printed p. 20](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21)). Customers benefit while wage-supported demand weakens. That incentive does not depend on Plan A's takeoff timetable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Void and Plan A share a causal claim: automation can shrink labor-linked income and demand, creating a need for a Citizen's Dividend. They disagree on pace and on whether deployed AI labor or permission to build capacity should fund it.</p></div>

## Verification cannot settle ownership

Plan A answers a secret state project with verification: evidence that declared chips, datacenters, and frontier research match reality. Its appendix imagines 500,000 H100-equivalents, a measure of high-end AI computing power, hidden in tunnels beside China's proposed Medog Hydropower Station. The construction could mask a datacenter, but its demand for electricity and cooling would leave traces. Plan A estimates that, until 2043, the chance of such a project reaching Top-Expert-Dominating AI before detection remains below 10 percent. This level outperforms the best human specialists across cognitive work ([pp. 45–50](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45)).

Like the Death Star program in *Rogue One*, the 2016 *Star Wars* film, knowledge can be divided while the machine retains an industrial footprint. The book's Alignment asks who receives returns from visible, legal capacity. It favors distributed ownership and open models, whose weights can run on local or organizational hardware without a provider's hosted service ([printed p. 56](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57)). Inspection can succeed while wealth stays concentrated; broad access can coexist with covert danger.

I don't know which control will face the harder test, and Plan A's authors do not claim to know the future either. My take is that verification and ownership complement rather than validate each other. I think calling either sufficient confuses visibility with power.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification constrains hidden capacity by making physical and organizational claims testable. Ownership determines who benefits from capacity that is already visible and legal.</p></div>

## Exit proof belongs in the renewal decision

Ownership of model access or dividend rights does not transfer a buyer's data, integrations, operating knowledge, or contractual access. An institution can receive a transparent AI service and still be unable to change suppliers. That makes supplier mobility a separate control.

I would add an exit proof: a funded, timed handover of one working AI service. Test one workflow, one data extract, and one replacement team over 30 days. The chief information officer owns renewal; the supplier funds the test. To reproduce results, the buyer owns past cases, accepted outcomes, and scoring, while the supplier provides decision records and saved model, prompt, and tool settings. Transfer requires usable data, connection details, and permissions; reconstruction requires data history and a restore guide. The replacement may change the method if the result meets the error limit, deadline, and cost ceiling.

Renewal depends on passing. Missing access, delay, excess errors, or excess cost trigger transition help, continued access, and supplier-funded repair. I think a failed handover predicts supplier lock-in even when every required disclosure is present.

Most of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's AI regulation, has applied since August 2, 2026. Article 50 covers disclosure for certain AI interactions and AI-generated or manipulated content. Disclosure cannot prove a service will survive a supplier change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An exit proof makes supplier mobility a CIO-owned renewal condition with measurable consequences. A board that cannot prove exit has handed its bargaining power to the supplier.</p></div>
