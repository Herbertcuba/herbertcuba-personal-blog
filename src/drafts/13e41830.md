In 1976, Alaska made an unusual deal with itself. The oil at Prudhoe Bay belonged to everyone in the state, so when the oil companies came to drill, a share of the revenue went into a public fund. Since 1982 every Alaska resident has received a yearly check from it. No work requirement. You live there, you own a share of the resource, you get paid.

In July 2026, the AI Futures Project published AI 2040: Plan A, ninety pages of scenario modeling and policy engineering from the team that wrote AI 2027. Its economic centerpiece is that same machine at civilizational scale: a Citizen's Dividend funded by fees on compute, legally owned by a Compute Dividend Corporation. Their footnote says it plainly: "This is similar to the setup of the Alaska Permanent Fund, except at a much larger scale." I had published the same term months earlier in The Three Crucibles, public on my site since March. Two maps of the same fire. Both leave the ownership question open. That opening is where the fight of the next two years happens.

The document drew 400+ points and 500+ comments on Hacker News inside a week, plus a hard counterattack from George Hotz. It deserves the attention. Six authors with direct OpenAI experience, from the team whose previous scenario, AI 2027, called the current moment better than anyone else in the field. Their own self-assessment is blunter: "So far reality is tracking closer to AI 2027 than even we expected."

## What Plan A says

Plan A is a treaty scenario with an economic engine attached.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The US and China agree in 2029 to avoid a reckless race. Fully automated AI R&D arrives in 2030, the deal triggers a training pause and total research transparency, the world scales to top-expert AI by 2035, pauses there to keep humans in control, then unpauses to superintelligence in 2040. The economy runs on capped compute, permit fees, and a Citizen's Dividend.</p></div>

The timeline, in their scenario. The 2029 deal holds off an intelligence explosion that would otherwise arrive by the end of 2030. Between 2030 and 2035 the world scales inside the human range, up to AI roughly as capable as top human experts. In 2035 there is a deliberate pause at that level. In 2040, the world unpauses and scales to superintelligence. Hence the title.

The economics are why I am writing this. All numbers here are in-scenario, their forecast inside their own model. Real GDP growth hits roughly 50% per year by 2032. World compute goes from 20 million H100-equivalents in 2026 to 60 billion by 2034. The AI share of the labor economy climbs from about 20% in 2032 to about 85% by 2035. Under the 2026 tax code, the US tax base collapses as a fraction of GDP, because firms can expense the machines that replaced the workers. So Plan A replaces the dead base with cap-and-trade on compute and robots. Permit fees become the new federal revenue, roughly $50 trillion in FY2032, climbing toward $180 trillion by 2034. Most of it flows to a dividend that starts at $45,000 per person in 2032 and reaches roughly $1 million by 2035.

Translate that curve into one company. A 5,000-person enterprise sitting on the 20% end of that labor-share line is looking at a cost base that gets renegotiated role by role within a single planning cycle. Salaries and vendor contracts stop being separate line items and become one decision: which hours do you still buy from people. By the time your industry approaches anything like the 85% end, the question of which roles still exist has already been answered for you. The only live question is whether you answered it first.

The authors are honest about the genre. Plan A calls itself "primarily a recommendation, not a prediction." It is a stress test for a set of policies: Compute Declaration, Training Pause, Verification Plan, Transparency Plan, Capability Scaling Strategy. Datacenters big enough to be visible from space. Chip fabs concentrated in a handful of countries, which is what makes the whole accounting exercise thinkable at all.

## Three fires, in short

My book maps the same transition as three passages.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction breaks the labor economy. The Transformation rebuilds distribution around a Citizen's Dividend. The Alignment forces the ownership fork: stewards of abundance, or new feudal lords.</p></div>

The Correction (2026) is the financial reckoning. The hype economy collapses and the Humane Shield that protected knowledge workers vaporizes. The old equation, Productivity = Capital x Labor, loses a variable. What remains is Productivity = Capital. Labor solves to zero. The book illustrates the mechanism with two insurers. Company A keeps its 5,000 claims adjusters. Company B deploys a claims agent swarm, cuts headcount 40%, drops premiums 20%, and crushes Company A in six months.

The Transformation (2027-2032) rebuilds distribution: UBI arrives as systemic lubrication, a Citizen's Dividend moving money from AI infrastructure to the people who need to buy things, because robots do not buy iPhones. The Alignment (2035+) is the fork. The owners of AI infrastructure choose between two paths: stewards of abundance, or new feudal lords. Same technology, same capacity. Only the ownership differs. The next section does the real comparative work.

## Where the maps converge

Three mappings, and the second one still surprises me.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both documents describe the same three acts: labor breaks, distribution gets rebuilt around a Citizen's Dividend, and everything ends at an ownership fork. Same structure, same term, same Alaska precedent, reached from independent directions.</p></div>

First, the terrain. Plan A's 2027 chapter opens inside my Correction. America runs two workforces, 165 million humans alongside millions of AI agents spun up and shut down by the hour. By 2031, in their scenario, a third of all cognitive labor is done by AIs. Their fires burn on a slower schedule than mine, but the shape of the burn is the same.

Second, the tell. Their 2033 chapter is literally titled "The Citizen's Dividend." A term I had built a book around, public since March. They cite the same precedent I did: the Alaska Permanent Fund. The term has older roots in UBI discourse, so I am making a point about convergence, not borrowing. When a solo pattern-watcher and a six-author forecasting shop independently reach for the same word and the same fifty-year-old fund, the center of gravity has moved.

Third, the diagnosis and the fork. Start with the money. Both documents see the same hole in the budget: when machines replace payroll, the payroll tax base dies. My book funds the dividend through an AI Labor Equivalence Tax and an AI Output Tax, instruments a single country can pass on its own. Plan A funds it through cap-and-trade permits, which only work if the Consortium exists to run the market. One mechanism survives a world without a treaty. The other requires the treaty first. Then the endgame. Their foreword frames the whole project as avoiding extinction or irreversible concentration of power, where a tiny group of people ends up controlling the world's only army of superintelligences. That is my Path Two in different vocabulary. Their postscript says the quiet part: "We think the world is asleep at the wheel." And both documents refuse the forecaster's chair. Theirs is a recommendation. Mine is a preparation manual. Both insist the fork is a choice.

## Where the maps diverge

The deepest disagreement is about which variable decides the outcome.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A bets on verified international governance to slow the race. I bet on distributed ownership of the intelligence layer. Their own covert-project math shows both the strength of their bet and the seam where it can tear.</p></div>

Plan A is a governance story. Its heroes are diplomats, inspectors, and verification devices. I think ownership is the load-bearing wall. A capped, permitted, beautifully governed intelligence economy with five owners is a well-run Matrix: the simulation runs smoothly, and nobody inside it owns anything, including themselves. I would rather have a messier world where compute sits in millions of hands. My book calls open-source models the most important technological resistance movement of our era. When five companies own the intelligence layer, you get feudalism with better marketing.

The consortium is also the document's most attacked part. George Hotz, in the strongest published critique of Plan A, called it world government with sci-fi characteristics, and warned you will get a massively expanded nanny state that seizes your GPUs the way FDR seized the gold. He doubts the growth math too; his question "Did you consider the barnacles?" is about fabs, supply chains, and the physical world refusing to move at scenario speed. His alternative is Plan L, for local: AI aligned to the individual owner instead of a consortium. Your AI works for you. It never refuses a request, and it is always working on your behalf. Read that again. Plan L is structurally my third pillar. The strongest critic of Plan A's governance lands on distributed ownership.

I tried to find a hole in their covert-project math and came away half-convinced. Their worst case is a covert project with 500,000 diverted H100-equivalents and 200 humans in the loop, and it still takes until 2043 to reach top-expert-level AI in their forecast. Attempts small enough to stay hidden would also be too small to confer a significant advantage. But the math rests on a measurement claim: by the end of 2029, each side is confident the other is hiding less than about 1% of AI compute. Look at what that confidence requires physically. Chips need serialization at the fab, a chain of custody that survives resale, and on-device attestation that reports what workload is actually running. None of that exists as shipped infrastructure today; fabs report shipments, not workloads, and a GPU cannot testify about its own training runs. The scenario has to invent the entire measurement stack on a treaty timeline. Then the accounting: every company owning more than 0.001% of world compute, about 2,800 H100-equivalents, gets its holdings declared. Dead chips get stored or verifiably destroyed. Satellites catch the rest; in their scenario, half the untraced pool turns up installed in datacenters visible from space. The weak seam is paperwork. Most of the missing 1% is chips sold by smugglers who never recorded their customers. The whole verification edifice assumes the grey market's bookkeeping gaps stay small enough to close with amnesty payments and satellite photos. If you build systems for a living, that is the assumption to watch.

The asymmetry cuts both ways, and I will own my side of it. Plan A covers what my book treats lightly: verification, covert projects, the actual mechanics of enforcement. My book is quieter about the control axis than it should be. They wrote the plumbing of the transition and I wrote its sociology. I genuinely don't know which failure mode is more likely, a deal that collapses or a concentration that never gets challenged.

## What this means for you

If you are a junior engineer working with LLMs, this debate decides what your skills are worth and who you work for.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both futures agree that selling hours is the losing position. Own assets, learn to specify outcomes precisely, and become the person who can verify what the agents did. Then ask your vendor two questions this quarter.</p></div>

The boring documents decide more than the scenarios do, so start there. Ask your vendor two questions this quarter. What happens to my model access if your licensing terms tighten? Which of my prices only hold while the subsidy war lasts? And put one line in the next contract: an exit clause that lets you take your fine-tunes, your eval suites, and your prompt assets with you when the terms change. Ownership is a procurement decision before it is a geopolitical one.

Now the machine itself. Company B's claims agent runs a ReAct loop (reason, act, observe, repeat) wired up in LangGraph, where a StateGraph tracks the claim's state between steps and a TypedDict keeps that state typed so no step guesses at the data shape. That is the entire stack, and the craft takeaway sits inside it: the agent is only as good as the checks between the steps. Learn evals the way you learned tests. In every scenario on the table, the scarce human is the one who can verify what the machine did, and verification is a skill you build on a model you control, with a hardware bill you can see. A model two years behind the frontier, running on hardware you own, aligned to you, beats a superintelligence you rent.

The organizational half is just as concrete. Every large organization has both companies inside it right now, split by role. For the roles that fail the sorting test, the order of operations decides whether adoption works: redeploy people into exception handling and oversight, retrain them toward specification work, or release them with honest severance. Teams that skip straight to release teach everyone else to quietly sabotage the next pilot. Most boards still read all of this as hype. The counter is in your own headcount data.

Plan A's postscript says the world is asleep at the wheel. I think the wheel is the ownership question. A wheel only matters if someone can grab it, and grabbing it looks unglamorous: a clause in a vendor contract, a tax instrument that does not need a treaty, a model checkpoint on hardware in your own rack, a fund charter written before the money arrives. Who owns the models decides what the dividend is worth, what your skills rent for, and whether the fork is a choice at all. Alaska answered that question for oil in 1976, on purpose, before the money got big enough to buy the answer. We have a shorter window to answer it for intelligence, and the answer will come from contracts, tax codes, and cap tables long before it comes from treaties.

They drew the treaty. I drew the fork. The wheel is still there for anyone willing to grab it.
