[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) gives policy machinery to a chain I set out in [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/): automated work can expand output while reducing labor income and consumer demand. Four of Plan A's six AI Futures Project authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the forecasting group's 2025 study of AI surpassing top human experts. They call Plan A a recommendation, not their best guess. I think its mechanisms must stand without faith in its dates.

Both works propose a Citizen's Dividend, a universal cash payment funded by returns from AI infrastructure, and both point to the Alaska Permanent Fund, the public oil-wealth fund that has paid residents since 1982. This economic agreement has limits: verification governs hidden capacity, ownership governs who receives the gains, and exit proof governs an institution's bargaining power.

My book PDF entered its [public repository on March 23, 2026](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8); a [public Plan A link](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) followed on July 9, 2026. This proves sequence, not private origin; convergence matters more than priority.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both frameworks link automated work to weaker labor income and an Alaska-style dividend, but leave control unsettled.</p></div>

## The Void is the economic match

The [June 24, 2026 edition, printed page 5](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the three crucibles as the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment. The Correction starts when inflated expectations collapse. In the Void, production recovers but jobs do not; its Transformation chapter links falling labor income to weaker purchasing power and proposes the Citizen's Dividend ([printed page 35](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36)). The Alignment asks who owns the machines and distributes their abundance.

Plan A reaches the same chain. In its scenario, AI systems perform one-third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, meaning work based mainly on analysis, communication, and professional judgment. As automated work replaces paid jobs, payroll and income-tax receipts fall.

The United States therefore sells permits to add robot and computing capacity, then directs most fees to a Citizen's Dividend projected at $45,000 per adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035 ([PDF pages 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17)). The book instead proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax: a charge for each unit of deployed AI labor equal to one person's work ([printed page 36](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37)). I think the match lies in the diagnosis and dividend, not the pace or funding method.

The book's fictional insurer makes the pressure concrete. One company keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; another uses AI, cuts staff by 40 percent and premiums by 20 percent, then wins within six months ([printed page 20](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21)). Customers gain while wage-supported demand shrinks. That incentive does not depend on Plan A's timetable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Void and Plan A share a causal claim: replacing paid work can weaken both tax revenue and consumer demand. They converge on a Citizen's Dividend but fund it differently.</p></div>

## Verification cannot settle ownership

Plan A answers secret state projects with verification: evidence that declared chips, datacenters, and advanced AI research match reality. Its appendix imagines 500,000 H100-equivalents hidden beside China's proposed Medog Hydropower Station; the unit compares computing systems with the H100, a high-end AI chip used as its baseline. In the scenario, the chance that this project reaches Top-Expert-Dominating AI (TED-AI) without detection stays below 10 percent until 2043. TED-AI means systems that outperform the best human specialists across relevant cognitive work ([PDF pages 45–50](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45)).

The Death Star program in *Rogue One*, the 2016 *Star Wars* film, is a fictional project to build a planet-destroying battle station through compartmentalized work. Secrecy can hide roles, but not the electricity, cooling, equipment, and construction the machine requires. Verification looks for that footprint.

The book's Alignment asks a different question about visible, legal capacity: who receives its returns? It favors distributed ownership and open models, AI systems whose trained parameter files, called weights, are available to run on an owner's hardware rather than only through a provider ([printed page 56](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57)). That portability lets an owner keep using the model without the provider's permission.

I don't know which control will face the harder test, and Plan A's authors do not claim to know the future either. My take is that verification and ownership complement rather than validate each other. I think calling either sufficient confuses visibility with power.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification makes hidden capacity harder to conceal. Ownership determines who can use visible capacity and receive its gains.</p></div>

## Exit proof belongs in the renewal decision

Yet ownership does not prove that an institution can leave an AI provider. A buyer may control model access or dividend rights while the supplier holds the working setup, data connections, and permissions. Bargaining power remains untested.

I would add an exit proof: a funded, timed handover of one working AI service. Start with one workflow, one data export, one replacement team, and 30 days. The chief information officer owns the renewal decision, and the supplier funds the test.

The buyer should own past cases and the acceptable result for each; these become the replacement test. The supplier should document the model version, instructions, data connections, permissions, and any software the AI calls. Data export proves only that files can leave; reconstruction proves another team can restore the service. The replacement may change the model, instructions, or workflow if it meets the error limit, deadline, and maximum cost.

Renewal should depend on passing. A missed deadline, excessive errors, or excessive cost should trigger continued access, transition help, and supplier-funded repair. I think a failed handover predicts supplier lock-in even when every required disclosure is present.

That distinction matters now. Most of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's AI regulation, began applying on August 2, 2026. Article 50 requires disclosure for certain AI interactions and AI-generated or manipulated content, but disclosure cannot prove that a service will survive a supplier change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof makes changing suppliers a CIO-owned renewal condition. If a service cannot be handed over on time and within budget, the institution lacks the bargaining power it thinks it owns.</p></div>
