[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 2026 conditional policy scenario by the AI Futures Project, a forecasting group focused on advanced AI. It proposes how countries could govern superintelligence, AI far beyond the best human experts across most intellectual work. Its authors call it a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my book about the passage through advanced automation. Its PDF was public in my [author-controlled repository](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) on March 23, 2026, and the [current edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) is dated June 24. A [public Plan A link](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) followed on July 9. Those dates show sequence, not private origins.

Both call their proposed public payment from AI wealth a Citizen's Dividend and cite the Alaska Permanent Fund, created in 1976 to save oil wealth, with annual payments to residents since 1982. They are arguments about the future, not data validating one another. I think their divergence exposes three controls. Plan A stresses verification: checking whether declared chips, data centers, and frontier AI research, work on the most capable systems, match reality. The book asks who owns productive AI and receives its returns. I add exit proof: a funded, timed handover of one working AI service.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works reach the same economic fork, then address hidden capacity, ownership of the gains, and the practical ability to leave a supplier.</p></div>

## The maps meet in the Void

The [June 24 PDF edition's opening map](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment. In the Correction, AI makes capital more productive and human labor less valuable. In the Void, production can recover without jobs. Its [Transformation chapter](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36) links lost labor income to weaker demand and proposes the Citizen's Dividend. The Alignment asks who owns the machines and who distributes their abundance.

Plan A puts scenario numbers on that chain. By mid-2031, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, work based mainly on analysis, communication, and professional judgment. Lost jobs shrink income- and payroll-tax revenue, so the United States sells permits for new computing and robot capacity. Most permit revenue funds a Citizen's Dividend projected at $45,000 per adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035 ([PDF pages 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17)).

[The book would tax](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37) each human-equivalent unit of deployed AI labor, the work one person would otherwise perform; Plan A charges for new capacity. As the book puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones”: cheap production cannot sustain an economy when households lose the income to buy its output. I think this is meaningful convergence, not one-to-one validation, and it does not require accepting Plan A's timetable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Void and Plan A connect automated work to weaker household demand and a Citizen's Dividend, while one would charge deployed AI labor and the other would sell permits for new capacity.</p></div>

## Verification cannot settle ownership

Plan A proposes declarations for major chip and data-center transactions, chip tracking, reciprocal infrastructure inspections, and almost entirely transparent research on the most capable AI systems. Those declarations matter because advanced data centers need chips, electricity, cooling, and construction. Inspectors can compare what governments report with that physical footprint, making concealed capability harder to assemble.

Appendix D tests the idea with a fictional covert data center beside China's proposed Medog Hydropower Station, whose construction and power could provide cover. In the scenario, its chance of building AI better than the best human experts across domains without detection before 2043 remains below 10 percent. Like the Death Star program in *Rogue One*, the 2016 *Star Wars* film, a secret project can hide its purpose more easily than its industrial footprint.

The Alignment addresses a different risk: visible and legal AI capacity can still funnel income and control to a few owners. [The book calls](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57) for distributed ownership, open infrastructure and model software available to multiple operators, and laws against a private intelligence monopoly. Its Citizen's Dividend gives people a claim on returns from AI infrastructure. My take is that verification governs hidden capacity, while ownership governs distribution. I don't know which will face the harder test, but I think either one alone lets power concentrate again.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Inspection can expose undeclared AI capacity, but it cannot decide who controls legal capacity or who receives the wealth that capacity creates.</p></div>

## Exit proof belongs in the renewal decision

Even verified and broadly owned infrastructure can leave an organization trapped by one provider. The supplier may still control the working setup, data connections, permissions, and tools that keep a service alive. Exit proof tests that dependence before another renewal deepens it.

Test one workflow, one data export, one replacement team, and 30 days. The chief information officer owns renewal, and the supplier funds the handover. The buyer owns the evaluation set, past cases with accepted outcomes. The package identifies the model version; includes system instructions, the persistent rules directing the service; records data sources, permissions, and connected tools; and explains restoration. It lets the replacement team reproduce a working service rather than merely receive a data export.

The replacement team may change the model, instructions, way it fetches source information, or workflow if it preserves the agreed business result. Missing the deadline, error limit, or cost ceiling means failure. Renewal remains conditional on passing; failure triggers continued access, supplier-funded repair, and enforceable transition support.

Since August 2, 2026, Article 50 of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's AI regulation, has applied disclosure rules to certain AI interactions and AI-generated or manipulated content. Disclosure identifies AI involvement but cannot prove that a working service will survive a supplier change. I think a failed handover predicts dependence despite compliant disclosure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification constrains hidden capacity, ownership allocates gains, and exit proof tests bargaining power. If a buyer cannot reproduce one working service within agreed limits, its freedom to leave exists mainly on paper.</p></div>
