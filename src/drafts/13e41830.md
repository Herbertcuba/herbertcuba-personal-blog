[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) imagines a future in which humanity handles superintelligence reasonably well. Its successful outcome is still rough.

Before abundance, AI absorbs a large share of cognitive work. The tax base starts to fail as power gathers around the machines that produce intelligence.

My book [The Three Crucibles](/books/the-three-crucibles/) was written in 2025. Its downloadable edition was public on my site by March 23, 2026, before AI 2040 appeared in July. It traces a similar sequence inside a fictional insurer.

I think the overlap deserves attention without making it a prediction contest. AI 2040 starts with geopolitics and superintelligence. My book starts with Company B choosing agents over claims adjusters. Both arrive at the same question: how do people keep an income and a say when labor stops being the main source of power?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040's optimistic scenario still contains a labor shock and concentrated control. The Three Crucibles follows a similar company-level sequence. Their overlap gives us a reason to prepare while both timelines remain uncertain.</p></div>

## What AI 2040 actually proposes

AI 2040 is a policy recommendation written as future history.

Four of its six authors, Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Eli Lifland, and Daniel Kokotajlo, also wrote [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/). Plan A pressure-tests policy. The team separates it from its best guess.

In 2029, the United States and China agree to avoid a reckless race. The deal blocks fully automated AI research in 2030. Systems pause near top-human-expert level in 2035, then resume toward superintelligence in 2040.

Takeoff is the feedback loop: AI helps build better AI, speeding up the next cycle. Superintelligence means systems that beat the best humans across all tasks at lower cost.

Plan A tries to slow that loop through four principles: buy time, make major frontier research visible, spread frontier work among many countries and companies, and keep limits reversible. Its rules focus on large training runs, declared datacenters, chip flows, and inspections.

Compute means the chip capacity used to train and run models. In Plan A, it plays the role of spice in *Dune*. Datacenters need physical chips and power, which gives governments something they can declare, inspect, and limit.

I think this is the report's strongest idea. It turns an abstract intelligence race into an infrastructure problem.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A 2029 US-China agreement slows the AI research feedback loop. Plan A puts human control on a physical base: major training runs, chips, power, and datacenters that governments can inspect.</p></div>

## The first Crucible starts inside a company

The Three Crucibles makes the first economic shock small enough to see.

Company A keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. Company B deploys an agentic swarm, AI systems that can use tools toward a goal. It cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The example is fictional.

Company B enters The Correction. Productivity moves toward capital, and the humane choice becomes hard to defend in a market that rewards lower prices.

Plan A scales that tension across the economy. In its scenario, AI performs one third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, and real GDP growth reaches about 50 percent in 2032. Without reform, the tax base collapses as a share of GDP while firms pour revenue into machines.

The book's interior calls the second Crucible The Void. Output recovers while human purchasing power lags. The demand problem fits in one line: "Robots do not buy iPhones."

Company B can offer cheaper insurance, but its customers still need money to buy a policy. I think the first break begins once enough companies make that same calculation. Superintelligence can wait.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Company B wins by replacing adjusters with agents. Plan A projects that choice across an economy where output rises while wages weaken. A rational decision becomes a social problem at scale.</p></div>

## The same dividend, with two different meters

Both documents respond to the demand problem with the exact phrase "Citizen's Dividend."

The Three Crucibles defines it as a direct transfer from AI infrastructure to the people who need to consume its output. Plan A gives its 2033 chapter the same name. Both point to Alaska, which first paid residents an oil dividend in 1982.

The funding mechanics split. My book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax for each human-equivalent unit of AI work, plus a small levy on computational output. Plan A caps added robot and compute-production capacity, then auctions permits to build more.

Under the book's proposal, Company B's move from adjusters to agents creates a tax duty tied to labor and AI output. Under Plan A, the scarce capacity behind those agents carries the permit price.

The scenario projects $50 trillion in US permit fees for fiscal year 2032, in real 2025 dollars. Most compute and robot permit fees fund the dividend. Payments start at $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and reach about $1 million by 2035. These are scenario figures.

I think the shared economic sequence carries more weight than the shared name. Wage income weakens, the old tax base follows, and a public claim on machine wealth becomes necessary because Company B still needs paying customers.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works use the same response and cite the same Alaska precedent. The Three Crucibles taxes AI labor and output. Plan A auctions permits for scarce production capacity. Each method reconnects machine wealth with human demand.</p></div>

## The third Crucible is about control

A dividend can spread income while control stays concentrated.

Plan A proposes a Compute Dividend Corporation in which each US citizen owns one share. It also spreads frontier development among many actors and makes major research easier to inspect.

The book calls the third Crucible The Alignment, and it pushes ownership further down. It asks whether people and local institutions can own the intelligence infrastructure itself. A citizen share can pay income even while the datacenters, models, and operating rules remain somewhere else.

Plan A's covert-project appendix tests the limit of verification. China hides a datacenter near the Medog Hydropower Station. It uses about 500,000 H100-equivalents, a standard compute measure, with around 200 people aware. US intelligence in the scenario puts its chance of reaching Top-Expert-Dominating AI undetected below 10 percent until 2043. That means AI above top human experts.

Verification lowers the risk without making secrecy impossible.

Company B faces a smaller version. It can pass every inspection and remain tied to one model provider. If its claims workflow cannot run elsewhere under the same policy and evidence rules, visibility has exposed the dependency without removing it. That engineering test appears in [Reversibility is the new correctness](/posts/reversibility-is-the-new-correctness/), alongside the wider question of [who owns the agent layer](/posts/who-owns-the-agent-layer/).

For Company B, the ownership issue reaches code. A model reads the claim, reasons about the next step, calls a policy tool, and reads the result. That pattern is called ReAct. LangGraph is a Python library that arranges the loop as a `StateGraph`, connected steps that share state. A `TypedDict` tells type checkers which fields to expect; Python leaves them unenforced at runtime. This code controls one provider's workflow. It gives Company B no right to move it elsewhere.

AI 2040 studies loss of control in far more depth. The Three Crucibles spends more time on ownership after control succeeds. I think a durable settlement needs both layers.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A gives citizens an economic share and uses verification against hidden concentration. The Third Crucible asks who can own and move the intelligence layer. Income can spread while control remains centralized.</p></div>

## Preparation begins before certainty

Dates can fail while choices survive.

AI 2040's calendar and growth math may be wrong. The Three Crucibles may compress a shift that arrives unevenly. Company B will still decide where its agent stores state, when a person reviews a claim, and whether a fallback can run before the contract is signed.

That is the in-between. The old wage bargain weakens before abundance arrives. Small architecture choices become procurement facts. Across enough companies, those facts become political structure.

I think preparation means preserving choices while they are still cheap to preserve. For Company B, that means a tested fallback and the contractual right to move its data and workflow. For society, it means deciding how machine wealth reaches citizens and how ownership spreads before a crisis sets the terms.

I don't know which dates will survive contact with reality. If the AI Futures Project and I are even directionally right, the way there will be bumpy. That is why I wrote The Three Crucibles as a preparation document: the rules around income and ownership are easiest to change before Company B becomes the whole economy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Preparation can begin before 2040 is certain. Economic and technical choices are easier to preserve before automation makes them costly to recover. The Three Crucibles was written for that unsettled passage.</p></div>
