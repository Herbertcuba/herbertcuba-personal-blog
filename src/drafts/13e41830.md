[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) and my book [The Three Crucibles](/books/the-three-crucibles/) both answer automation with a Citizen's Dividend modeled on Alaska.

The book was public on my site on March 23, 2026. AI 2040 appeared in July. Four of its six authors also wrote [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/). Plan A calls itself "primarily a recommendation." Its dates and numbers belong to a scenario.

I think the timing is useful because the same structure appeared in two different documents. Their disagreement begins when each asks who can act after a control fails.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles both arrive at a Citizen's Dividend built on the Alaska precedent. The overlap supports the diagnosis. Their split over verification and ownership matters more than credit.</p></div>

## The same dividend appears twice

The book's Company B makes the economic chain easy to see.

Company B is a fictional insurer in a thought experiment. Company A keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. Company B deploys an agentic swarm, cuts headcount by 40 percent and lowers premiums by 20 percent. In the book, Company B wins within six months.

That change moves through the book's three Crucibles. The Correction breaks the old link between labor and output. The Void begins when machine output rises faster than household income. The Alignment decides whether abundance is shared or concentrated. The demand problem fits in one line: "Robots do not buy iPhones."

Plan A models the same pressure at national scale. "Takeoff" means capability growth after AI starts helping researchers build better AI. Its scenario has a US-China deal in 2029, a pause around top-human-expert level in 2035 and a move toward superintelligence in 2040. The deal avoids fully automated AI research in 2030.

The numbers are scenario projections. AI performs one-third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, while real GDP growth reaches about 50 percent in 2032. The US caps robot and compute-production capacity and auctions permits, collecting a projected $50 trillion that fiscal year.

The American version sends most permit fees to a Citizen's Dividend. Each US citizen owns one share in a Compute Dividend Corporation. Payments to American adults rise from $45,000 per person in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035.

The Three Crucibles proposes taxes on AI labor and output. Plan A sells permits for robot and compute-production capacity. I think the shared diagnosis survives if the numbers fail. When Company B removes payroll faster than new wages appear, public revenue and household demand need a claim on machine production.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works trace the same economic chain from automated labor to a weaker wage-tax base and a Citizen's Dividend. The book taxes AI labor and output. Plan A caps and auctions robot and compute-production capacity.</p></div>

## The split is verification versus ownership

Plan A deserves its strongest reading before testing its gap.

Its four principles are Buy Time, Total Research Transparency, Diffuse AI Broadly and Reversibility. Major datacenter owners and suppliers declare large transactions for inspection. Almost all AI research becomes public, and frontier work spreads across regulated actors. I think this is a serious attempt to prevent a quiet takeover.

Appendix D stress-tests that system. China diverts about 500,000 smuggled H100-equivalent chips to tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station. Around 200 people may know. The scenario keeps the chance of undetected Top-Expert-Dominating AI below 10 percent until 2043.

That supports the verification case and shows its boundary. Verification reveals what exists and whether rules were broken. Ownership determines who retains a claim, an exit and a voice when the rules fail. A dividend supplies the economic claim. Operational portability lets Company B change suppliers. Democratic control gives people a voice over frontier rules. Cash can spread widely while model supply remains concentrated.

This is the Special Order 937 problem in *Alien*. The Nostromo crew finds the hidden order after the company has set the mission and treated them as expendable. Better evidence changes their knowledge while leaving control elsewhere.

George Hotz attacks Plan A from another side. He argues that fabs and supply chains slow hard takeoff, then proposes "Plan L, for local," where AI serves its individual owner. I don't know whether hidden compute or concentrated control breaks first. I think Plan A handles detection better, while The Three Crucibles better defines the rights that survive failure.

This claim can be tested. Plan A can shrink the ownership gap. Its citizen shares, broad diffusion and transparency would need to give people a usable supplier exit and a real voice over the rules. Its economic claim is clearer than its exit and voice.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A combines transparency, diffusion and reversible limits in a serious governance design. Its covert-project scenario supports the verification case. The remaining gap is whether citizens and firms keep a practical claim, an exit and a voice when enforcement fails.</p></div>

## Company B turns the split into code

A junior engineer can make operational portability testable inside Company B's claims workflow.

Suppose its agent uses ReAct, short for reasoning and acting. The model calls a tool, reads the result and chooses its next action. It loops until it proposes an outcome or reaches a human boundary.

LangGraph is a Python library for workflows that branch or loop. Its `StateGraph` connects steps as nodes and routes between them with edges. The nodes share a state record. A `TypedDict` describes that record to a static type checker. Runtime enforcement needs other code.

This small routing skeleton starts after the agent has proposed a claim outcome:

```python
from typing import Literal, TypedDict
from langgraph.graph import END, StateGraph

class ClaimState(TypedDict):
    claim_id: str
    proposed_outcome: Literal["pay", "review", "deny"]
    requires_human: bool
    model_id: str
    trace: list[dict]

def checkpoint(state: ClaimState) -> dict:
    return {}

def route_after_checkpoint(
    state: ClaimState,
) -> Literal["human_review", "finish"]:
    if state["requires_human"]:
        return "human_review"
    return "finish"

flow = StateGraph(ClaimState)
flow.add_node("checkpoint", checkpoint)
flow.add_node("human_review", checkpoint)
flow.add_node("finish", checkpoint)
flow.set_entry_point("checkpoint")
flow.add_conditional_edges(
    "checkpoint",
    route_after_checkpoint,
    {"human_review": "human_review", "finish": "finish"},
)
flow.add_edge("human_review", END)
flow.add_edge("finish", END)
claim_flow = flow.compile()
```

The code implements conditional routing only. Damage estimation, setting `requires_human` and replay collection remain application work. Company B must apply a versioned claims policy, append tool inputs and results to `trace`, then validate the state at runtime.

Before changing models, the team runs legally reviewed claims through the current model and a named fallback. Each case has allowed outcomes and a human-review rule. An unsupported denial fails the migration.

I think this is testable operational ownership. ReAct and LangGraph provide workflow structure. The policy logic, evaluation set and replay evidence are the parts Company B can carry elsewhere.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>ReAct describes the claim agent's tool-use loop. LangGraph can expose its state and route a flagged case to human review, while TypedDict supplies static hints only. Portability exists when the same representative claims pass the same rules with a named fallback model.</p></div>

## The exit test belongs before the contract

Company B has the most choice before it signs or renews a provider agreement.

The AI engineering owner runs the fallback replay before signature and at renewal. Architecture verifies the export bundle. Procurement and legal record the approved EU or EEA data path, notice period and deletion terms. Failure blocks the deal until the defect is fixed or a named executive accepts the risk in writing.

The review must name the actual service and licence. Generic labels such as "closed" and "open" are too weak. For a hosted API, Company B lists the artifacts its signed terms allow it to export. It assumes no access to model weights. For a named open-weight model, the team reads that version's licence and tests whether it can operate the model.

The bulk of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng) began to apply on August 2, 2026. General-purpose model obligations had applied since August 2, 2025. Company B still needs proof that its provider exit works.

I think junior engineers should treat the replay as production work. A pilot proves that today's provider can handle a claim. The replay checks continuity after a provider change. That is [reversibility as an engineering constraint](/posts/when-software-becomes-disposable/) and gives [the agent layer a named owner](/posts/who-owns-the-agent-layer/).

A failed fallback gives Company B evidence of dependency before the contract turns that dependency into an operating fact.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Company B should run a funded fallback replay before signature and at renewal. The test needs named owners and a rule that blocks the deal after failure. A promise of portability has little value until the claims workflow passes the exit test.</p></div>
