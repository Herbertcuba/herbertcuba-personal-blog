[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) uses a thought experiment: an insurer with 5,000 claims adjusters faces a rival that automates, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months (p. 20). [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) applies the same pressure to an entire economy.

Four of Plan A's six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/): Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Eli Lifland, and Daniel Kokotajlo. They call Plan A a recommendation and say their best forecast may differ. I use the overlap as support for a mechanism; a scenario cannot confirm a prophecy with data.

A [March 23 repository commit](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) added the book PDF, and a [July 9 Hacker News submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) later linked Plan A. This dates those two public records only; the book's original publication date remains unverified.

I think the stronger case is the chain they share: automation weakens labor income and the tax base, creating the case for a Citizen's Dividend. Plan A asks states to verify dangerous development, while the book asks who owns the resulting wealth. My take is that this fork reaches every company buying AI.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Two maps reach the same economic pressure point: machines keep producing while wages stop carrying demand. Their split shows that inspection and ownership govern different forms of AI power.</p></div>

## Two routes to the same economic break

Plan A imagines a 2029 US-China agreement that slows takeoff, the speed at which capable AI improves. In its scenario, systems stay within human ability through 2035 and move toward superintelligence in 2040. By mid-2031, AI performs a third of work done mainly with the mind. The US later auctions permits for new robots and computing capacity, with most fees funding a Citizen's Dividend projected at $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million by 2035 (pp. 4, 13, 17–18).

The book maps this pressure across three stages. The Correction drives down the value of human labor, while the Transformation rebuilds demand because “robots do not buy iPhones.” The Alignment asks whether abundant production leads to broad stewardship or concentrated control. Its Citizen's Dividend gives people a claim on machine output, and its AI Labor Equivalence Tax shifts public revenue toward deployed AI labor. Separate proposals for distributed ownership, open infrastructure, and legal limits address concentration (pp. 35–36, 56).

Both also point to Alaska's oil dividend as a precedent (Plan A p. 18; book p. 37). I think the link between wages and demand survives errors in the timelines. The funding methods differ: Plan A prices new capacity through permits, while the book taxes deployed AI labor. Both move public revenue away from payroll and toward the machine economy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both maps expect automation to weaken labor income and public revenue. Plan A funds a dividend through capacity permits; The Three Crucibles proposes a dividend and a tax on deployed AI labor.</p></div>

## The maps split over power

Plan A relies on verification, meaning proof that declared chips, datacenters, and research on the most capable systems match reality. Major chip transactions are declared, rival powers inspect infrastructure, and that research becomes almost entirely transparent (pp. 7, 11). This targets unsafe development in a race between states, a problem enterprise portability cannot solve.

The report tests verification against a covert project. In one appendix branch, China hides roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents of AI computing power near the Medog Hydropower Station; around 200 people know. The setup resembles *Andor*: splitting knowledge lets a small cell hide a large operation. Plan A still puts the chance of reaching top-expert AI undetected before 2043 below 10 percent (Appendix D, pp. 45–50).

The book looks at another danger. Its dividend and AI-labor tax address distribution, while distributed ownership and open infrastructure address concentration in a few private hands. I don't know which failure will dominate. I think verification and ownership both belong in the answer because each covers ground the other leaves open.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification makes a secret frontier race harder. Distribution and ownership shape who benefits from the systems that pass inspection. A credible control system needs both.</p></div>

## The 2040 fork is already in the contract

Return to the insurer. Once an AI claims service shapes staffing and premiums, a supplier failure or licensing change threatens continuity. Plan A's chip verification works at another level and leaves buyer dependency unchanged. A supplier can meet transparency rules while its contracts, proprietary records, or migration costs make the service hard to move.

At company level, the ownership question becomes practical: can the buyer keep operating after leaving one supplier? I would add an exit proof, a timed handover of one service before renewal. Records and event logs preserve past decisions; model or prompt versions, tests, and tool interfaces let a replacement team reproduce the workflow. Permissions and transition rights provide legal and technical access. Only the drill shows whether the promised door opens.

Keep the scope narrow: one workflow, a named owner, a recovery target, and a cost limit. The replacement team restores the service and reproduces sampled decisions under a documented setup. A pass supports portability only for that workflow under those conditions. Other workflows and shared infrastructure remain outside the test.

[Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), applicable from August 2, 2026, requires disclosures for certain AI interactions and generated content. General export and portability rights sit outside it. Other duties may require documents or logs depending on the system and the buyer's role, while usable exports and transition support still need contract terms.

I think this will become Europe's next AI fault line. Transparency tells us what we can inspect; a tested exit tells us whether choice survives when a supplier changes the terms.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>At renewal, run a bounded handover with a named owner, recovery target, and cost limit. Success shows the specified workflow can move under those conditions. Power stays concentrated when exit is an untested clause.</p></div>
