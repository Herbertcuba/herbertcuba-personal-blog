Alaska voters created a public investment fund from mineral revenue in 1976; resident dividends began in 1982. Fifty years later, two AI futures use its precedent and name: the Citizen’s Dividend.

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), a 2026 conditional scenario from the nonprofit AI Futures Project, proposes a fictional corporation that receives part of robot and compute permit fees and gives every US citizen one equal share. Its authors call it a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast. Four of the five front-page authors of [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), the project’s April 2025 scenario of a rapid AI race, return.

My site records the first link and PDF for my book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), on March 23, 2026, and its June 24 update. AI 2040 added its PDF link on July 9. This proves only when the public files appeared, not influence, independent development, validation, or accuracy.

I think the value lies elsewhere. The later scenario quantifies the same possible break, then chooses another way to contain power. Read together, they point to infrastructure visibility, durable economic rights, and supplier exit.

## The convergence is an economic break

The Three Crucibles PDF opens with The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. The Void and The Transformation develop the middle stage; the landing page uses The Transformation. The argument moves from labor losing value to production outrunning wage-funded demand, then to ownership of abundance.

Plan A quantifies a similar break. Its scenario has AI performing one third of cognitive labor, work based on reasoning and judgment, by mid-2031. In 2032 it projects about 50 percent growth in real gross domestic product, or inflation-adjusted economic output. Yet job losses weaken personal-income and payroll-tax receipts, while deductions for new machines reduce corporate taxable income. Output rises as purchasing power and employment-linked revenue weaken.

Plan A sells capacity permits, tradable permissions to build limited robot or compute infrastructure; scarcity generates revenue. The scenario projects $50 trillion in US permit fees in fiscal 2032 and a $45,000 Citizen’s Dividend per American adult in real 2025 dollars. These are modeled, not observed, outcomes.

[The Three Crucibles PDF](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) pairs its dividend with an AI Labor Equivalence Tax, a levy tied to the public revenue from equivalent human work. Its demand argument is blunt: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” I think the overlap matters because both works connect lost demand and tax revenue to a public claim on automated output, although one uses capacity permits and the other AI labor.

That claim should be able to lose. [Statistics Norway](https://www.ssb.no/en), the official statistics agency, publishes industry data on [AI use](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/13265), [worker compensation](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/09170), [productivity](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/09174), and [taxes](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/10799). A 2032 test could take 2023–2025 as baseline, compare fast and slow AI adoption, and match industries on earlier productivity.

Reject the economic-break claim if high adopters record productivity growth ten percentage points above low adopters while labor’s share of value added, the part paid to workers, stays within two points of baseline and social-contribution receipts remain stable relative to the economy. A labor-share fall above five points with weaker receipts would support it. Tax changes, oil shocks, reclassification, or a result between thresholds would be inconclusive. I think a useful framework names the evidence that could defeat it.

## Where the maps split

Takeoff is AI automating AI research and speeding its own improvement. Plan A’s scenario reaches a US–China agreement in 2029, pauses in 2035 at the level of the best human experts, and resumes in 2040 toward superintelligence: AI better than the best humans at every task, as well as faster and cheaper.

Restraint depends on compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI. Major chip transactions would be declared, datacenters inspected, and AI research made transparent. Developers would submit safety cases, written arguments that a system will not cause irreversible catastrophe, for public and expert criticism.

A fictional branch stress-tests the design. China withholds the declaration of a hidden chip stockpile, then builds a covert datacenter beside the unfinished Medog Hydropower Station, a dam project whose construction offers cover and whose tunnels help conceal heat. The site still needs equipment, power, cooling, and people. I think industrial AI capacity leaves evidence beyond software, although inspection may arrive late.

Ownership addresses another failure. Plan A’s Compute Dividend Corporation would receive a fixed share of permit fees. That is a claim on fees, not direct ownership of robots, datacenters, models, or companies. My book distributes ownership of AI infrastructure among individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities.

Plan A’s 2040 storyline hands institutions and equipment to AI systems until humans could no longer shut everything down. The Alignment asks whether people remain “stewards of abundance” or depend on “the new feudal lords.” I do not know which of us is right about governance, and neither do they. My take is that verification and economic rights must be joined: evidence about machinery cannot decide who benefits, while ownership cannot establish safety.

## Practical exit is the third control

Control concentrates at three scales. States must see hidden infrastructure, citizens must claim automated wealth, and organizations must move critical workflows. Economic rights check ownership; exit checks supplier bargaining power. Both must exist before dependence hardens.

My book compares two fictional insurers. One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; the other uses coordinated AI agents, software that handles claims, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. Adoption outruns reversibility.

Reversibility capture occurs when a supplier retains data, licences, interfaces, permissions, certification, or computing capacity needed to run a workflow. Exit proof is a live handover by a replacement team before renewal, using historical cases controlled by the buyer. Require it when failure could stop a critical operation or harm people, or when two of three risks are high: sensitive data, supplier concentration, and replacement difficulty. Low-risk tools need a tested export and documented settings.

Disaster-recovery exercises rehearse restoration, while portability tests prove that data and settings can move. Exit proof adds operation against buyer-owned cases and a renewal consequence. I think the leverage only exists before you sign.

| Field | Contract rule |
| --- | --- |
| Test pack | Freeze model and environment versions, tools, data, cases, expected results, and the grader, the approved scoring method. |
| Pass | Run three times. Every run meets safety, approved permissions and decision rights, and evidence rules; the median meets error, time, and cost limits. |
| Retry | Allow one retry only for a recorded external outage. A changed version, missing log, or unavailable grader is indeterminate and requires a full rerun. |
| Owners and cost | Business accepts risk and downtime; technology restores service. The buyer funds its team and reviewer; supplier fees are fixed, and the supplier repairs and retests missing access or documentation. |
| Renewal | Failure triggers repair and rerun, then portability support, repricing, or non-renewal unless the risk forum accepts a time-limited exception. |

An illustrative claims-triage test allows 30 days and 15 days of buyer effort for 200 cases. The median error rate stays within two percentage points of the incumbent; each run finishes within an hour and meets a cost limit agreed before the pilot. Correct decisions without a source trail or model version are indeterminate. These are proposals, not an industry standard.

The [European Union’s 2024 AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), its risk-based AI law, became generally applicable on August 2, 2026. Rules for general-purpose models, which support many tasks, had applied since August 2, 2025; some high-risk rules transition later. Existing descriptions and logs can support exit proof, but cannot demonstrate a handover.

A passing handover can leave dependence on exclusive data, licences, or scarce compute. Technical exit does not settle ownership. I think Alaska’s lesson is timing: it secured the public claim before oil income became routine. The machines may be built with these rights or without them; who holds the durable claim is still open.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Exit proof is a risk-tiered handover test agreed at signing, then run before renewal against fixed versions, buyer cases, measurable limits, evidence, and allocated costs. A buyer can pass technically and remain economically dependent, which is why visibility, rights, and exit must be secured before concentrated control becomes ordinary.</p>
</div>
