Alaska gives the future-of-AI debate a practical starting point. Voters created the mineral-revenue-backed Alaska Permanent Fund in 1976, and dividends began in 1982. Fifty years later, two AI futures use the same precedent and the same label for a public payment funded by shared revenue: the Citizen’s Dividend.

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 2026 policy recommendation from the nonprofit AI Futures Project, told through a conditional scenario. Its outcomes depend on governments implementing the plan; they are not observed evidence or the authors’ best guess. Four of its six authors also wrote [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), their April 3, 2025 scenario about automated AI research racing toward superintelligence, systems better than the best humans across every task.

My book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), was on my site by March 23, 2026; the current PDF followed on June 24, and AI 2040 added its PDF link on July 9. This establishes public-artifact order, not influence, independent discovery, validation, or predictive success. I think structure matters more: both maps reach the same economic break, then protect different things.

## The maps meet at the economic break

The Three Crucibles opens with The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. The Correction marks labor’s loss of economic weight. The Void is where work and income detach; later chapters call that rebuilding The Transformation, the current landing-page label. The Alignment frames the choice between stewardship and feudal concentration.

In Plan A’s scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, work based on reasoning and judgment, by mid-2031. For 2032, it projects roughly 50 percent growth in real gross domestic product, or inflation-adjusted output, as job losses weaken income and payroll taxes while investment deductions reduce corporate taxes. The report says this figure cannot be translated directly into 2025 purchasing power, so it does not prove household demand will weaken.

Plan A responds with tradable permits to build scarce robot or computing capacity. In its scenario, United States permit fees reach $50 trillion in fiscal 2032. A fictional Compute Dividend Corporation receives a legally assigned share that rises from 25 percent of those fees in 2032 to 75 percent in 2035. Each citizen gets one share; the 2032 payment is $45,000 per American adult in inflation-adjusted 2025 dollars.

[The Three Crucibles PDF](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) proposes a Citizen’s Dividend and an AI Labor Equivalence Tax, charged for each human-equivalent unit of AI work according to what a person in that role would have generated for public revenue. Its demand warning is blunt: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” I think the diagnosis and distribution logic converge, but the financing differs. Plan A charges scarce capacity; my book taxes equivalent AI labor.

[Statistics Norway](https://www.ssb.no/en), the country’s official statistics agency, publishes AI-use, compensation, and productivity data by industry. In 2032, compare high- and low-adoption industries with similar 2023–2025 productivity and labor-share trends. Labor share is compensation divided by value added, or output minus inputs. The thesis weakens if the high-adoption group gains ten points more productivity while its relative labor share moves no more than two; a fall beyond five points supports it, with the middle inconclusive. I think a framework that can lose is more useful than one that resembles a later report.

<p>Both works reconnect automated production to citizens, but one uses capacity-permit revenue and the other equivalent-labor taxation. The economic thesis remains a hypothesis to test against productivity, labor share, and public revenue.</p>

## Verification and ownership solve different failures

Takeoff means AI automating AI research and accelerating its own improvement. Plan A’s conditional path reaches a United States–China agreement in 2029, pauses in 2035 at the level of a top human expert, and resumes in 2040 toward superintelligence. Its restraint system depends on compute, the computing capacity used to train and run AI.

Major chip transactions would be declared, datacenters inspected, and AI research made almost entirely transparent, while private use stayed private. Developers would publish safety cases, detailed arguments that a system will not cause irreversible catastrophe, for criticism by the public, experts, auditors, and rivals. The aim is to expose dangerous capacity before it outruns political control.

Plan A stress-tests that design with a fictional covert Chinese project. Smuggled chips feed tunnels beside the unfinished Medog Hydropower Station, where construction provides cover and another tunnel carries away heat. The project needs electricity, equipment, and roughly 200 informed people. I think that leaves traces, but inspectors may find them too late.

Ownership addresses another dependency. Plan A gives citizens a claim on permit fees; The Three Crucibles spreads ownership of AI infrastructure among individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities. One distributes revenue rights, the other productive assets. Plan A ends with humans handing institutions and equipment to AI until they could no longer shut all of it down. The Alignment asks whether owners become “stewards of abundance” or “the new feudal lords.” I don't know which path is right. My take is that verification and economic rights must work together: visibility cannot decide who benefits, while ownership cannot establish safety.

## The decision comes before dependence

I call the combined frame the visibility-claim-exit test. It asks three questions: Is powerful infrastructure visible? Do citizens have an enforceable claim on its output? Can organizations move critical workflows between suppliers? The first two concern states and assets; the third concerns contracts, so the analogy has a limit. Power can concentrate at one layer while another looks well governed. The leverage only exists before you sign.

My book illustrates the enterprise risk with two fictional insurers. One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; the other uses coordinated AI agents, cuts headcount 40 percent, lowers premiums 20 percent, and wins within six months. It is an illustration, not market evidence, of adoption outrunning reversibility.

Reversibility capture occurs when a supplier retains something a workflow needs elsewhere, such as data, permissions, or computing access. Exit proof is a live handover test agreed at signing and run before renewal with buyer-controlled cases. It records current and replacement technical environments, groups cases by risk, sets hard limits for safety, evidence, time, cost, and worst-case errors, then repeats the run. Correct output with a missing audit log fails; incomplete reruns are indeterminate.

The buyer’s procurement system should block preferred status and renewal after failure or an indeterminate result until a clean rerun, unless the business owner accepts a time-limited exception. For a first critical workflow, I would propose 30 days, at most 15 staff-days, and a contractual budget ceiling. A universal Swedish-krona or Norwegian-krone figure would be false precision because failure costs vary. These are proposals, not a Nordic standard.

Decide now which suppliers must prove exit, who owns the budget, and which system blocks renewal without evidence. Through 2032, monitor whether higher AI adoption brings productivity gains with a falling labor share, while tracking public revenue separately. The economic-rights case weakens if output remains broadly shared without a dedicated claim; the exit case weakens if critical workflows remain replaceable without live handovers.

The [European Union’s 2024 AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, provides legal context. The risk-based law generally applies from August 2, 2026; rules for general-purpose AI models, which support many tasks, have applied since August 2, 2025. Documents and logs aid an exit test but cannot prove handover. Legal sets risk, the process owner approves cases, security checks evidence, and procurement binds the result to renewal.

I think Alaska’s sequence carries the lesson. Voters created the claim before the revenue became enormous, then built the payment around that right. Concentration returns wherever one protection is missing: hidden infrastructure evades safety, unclaimed output concentrates wealth, and untested exit turns adoption into dependence.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Make advanced-AI capacity visible, give citizens enforceable claims on automated wealth, and prove organizational exit before concentrated power closes the choice.</p>
</div>
