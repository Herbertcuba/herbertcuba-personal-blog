Alaska settled an ownership question before its oil income became routine. In 1976, as Prudhoe Bay oil promised a flood of public money, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/) to reserve part of the US state's mineral revenue. The first resident dividend followed in 1982. Alaska did not predict the oil price; it secured a public claim before the money became ordinary.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a policy recommendation told through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that studies advanced AI and how governments might navigate it. It follows the group's April 2025 [*AI 2027* scenario](https://ai-2027.com/), and four of that work's five authors also wrote Plan A.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my speculative book about AI's economic transition, was public on my site by March 23, 2026; the current PDF edition followed on June 24. A public link to Plan A appeared on July 9. That chronology establishes public order, not influence or predictive accuracy.

I think the narrower comparison is more useful. The two works independently reach an economic break and a public dividend, then separate over how power should be controlled. Their disagreement exposes three questions that are often blurred together:

| Test | Failure it addresses | Evidence required | Decision owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Infrastructure verification | Undeclared large-scale computing capacity | Records plus physical inspection | States or treaty bodies |
| Economic ownership | AI-generated gains accrue to a narrow group | A legally enforceable claim on output or revenue | Legislatures and the citizens named in law |
| Supplier exit | A vital process cannot move to another supplier | A live handover against buyer-owned cases | Accountable business and technology owners |

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A is a conditional policy scenario, while The Three Crucibles is my speculative book. Their overlap does not confirm either chronology. It reveals that inspecting AI infrastructure, allocating its gains, and retaining the ability to leave a supplier are different tests of control.</p></div>

## The economic break is the real convergence

The public book page, which is authoritative for my taxonomy, names the First Crucible as The Correction, the Second as The Transformation, and the Third as The Alignment. The Correction describes inflated expectations meeting uneven returns, followed by company failures and stronger pressure to automate. Plan A has no close equivalent because it begins with geopolitical competition over advanced AI. The maps start in different places.

They meet in The Transformation. My book imagines output recovering while many jobs do not and states the demand problem plainly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” In Plan A's scenario, AI performs [one third of all cognitive labor by mid-2031](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=13), meaning work based mainly on information, analysis, communication, and judgment. It then projects about 50 percent real economic growth in 2032 while automation erodes the tax base.

The mechanism matters because production and purchasing power can move in opposite directions. When wages disappear, governments collect less personal-income and payroll tax. Companies can also deduct or depreciate investments in data centers, factories, and robots, which can reduce taxable profit during the build-out. Output may surge while household demand and public revenue weaken.

Both works answer with a Citizen's Dividend, a public payment tied to AI-driven productivity, and both point to Alaska. [My book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37), based on the taxes and social payments a person doing equivalent work would have contributed. [Plan A proposes tradable permits](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17): government limits new robot and computing capacity, lets builders buy and sell permissions, then directs part of the revenue to a corporation owned equally by US citizens. In the scenario, its dividend grows from $45,000 per American adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035, measured in 2025 dollars.

The Alignment carries the income problem into politics: will citizens become stewards of abundance, or will a narrow group control the productive system? Plan A also tries to spread gains rather than allow irreversible concentration. I think the convergence is unusually specific because it includes the broken income loop, the Alaska precedent, and the same policy name. Yet the term “Citizen's Dividend” predates both works, and Plan A's numbers remain scenario projections. A later scenario can stress-test my framework; it cannot turn speculation into observed evidence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The canonical sequence in my book is The Correction, The Transformation, and The Alignment. Plan A has no matching market correction, but both works reach the same income-and-tax problem and propose a Citizen's Dividend. They agree on the economic break while choosing different funding mechanisms.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership govern different failures

Plan A is much stronger on a technical danger that my book largely brackets. Its scenario has the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029, slow development, and pause in 2035 at AI matching top human experts before moving toward superintelligence in 2040. Without the deal, the authors model a rapid route to fully automated AI research and superintelligence by the end of 2030. *Takeoff* means AI automates enough AI research to speed up its own improvement; *superintelligence* means systems that outperform the best humans across fields while working faster and more cheaply. These are conditional dates, not promised events.

The proposed restraint depends on seeing the physical build-out. Plan A projects growth from roughly 20 million H100-equivalents in 2026 to 60 billion in 2034. An H100-equivalent measures AI computing performance against Nvidia's H100 processor, so it describes capacity rather than a literal chip count. Major data-center owners and chip suppliers would report large transactions, rival powers would inspect infrastructure, and AI research would become almost entirely transparent.

Plan A tests its own design with [a covert-project branch](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45). In this fictional branch, China diverts about 500,000 H100-equivalents into tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station, a remote Chinese hydroelectric project whose power and underground setting make it useful for concealment. Roughly 200 people know. The authors put the chance of this hidden project reaching top-expert AI undetected before 2043 below ten percent, but the branch still shows what verification must defeat: false records, official collusion, and delayed physical evidence.

The Alignment asks a different question: who owns AI infrastructure and receives its output? Plan A gives citizens shares in the corporation that receives permit revenue, creating an economic claim without distributing chips, AI models, or AI companies. My book proposes broader ownership among individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, including models that can run on local hardware.

I don't know which balance could survive geopolitical pressure, and neither do the Plan A authors. I think the disagreement is more informative than a forced match. Plan A tackles secret capacity and loss of control, where my book offers no comparable plan for containing superintelligence. My book goes further on distributed ownership, which an inspection regime cannot create. Detecting a breach does not decide who holds economic power afterward, while broad ownership cannot contain a system more capable than its owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses reporting, inspection, and transparent research to constrain a rush toward superintelligence. The Alignment asks who owns AI infrastructure and its output. Each addresses a gap left by the other because observing concentrated capacity and distributing economic power are different jobs.</p></div>

## A usable exit starts at signing

The first two tests sit mainly with governments and citizens. A third appears whenever an organization makes a critical process dependent on an AI supplier. My book illustrates the pressure with [two fictional insurers](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21). One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. The other uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that carry out parts of the claims process, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The numbers are illustrative, but the mechanism is clear: adoption can outrun a buyer's ability to reverse it.

My take is that exit proof should be mandatory at renewal for a business-critical AI workflow handling regulated decisions or an operationally essential process. Exit proof is a live handover test showing that a replacement team can run the process to an agreed standard. Low-risk tools may need only a tested data export and documented settings. High-impact workflows need a full handover test with named business and technology owners. The requirement belongs in the contract at signing, while the buyer still has leverage.

The operating sequence is short: classify the workflow, contract the test, run it before renewal, and escalate failed critical workflows. For a first pilot, I would choose one workflow, one replacement team, and a 30-day window. I would also require the supplier to reimburse test costs up to five percent of the annual contract value, with the buyer approving and funding any excess. That cost rule is a proposed starting point, not an industry standard.

The business owner should schedule the test and judge its outcome. The technology owner sets the technical baseline and owns restoration if the exercise fails. A cross-functional AI portfolio forum, the group responsible for AI investment and risk decisions, can settle disputes among the business, technology, procurement, legal, and the supplier. If no such forum exists, the contract should name an equivalent decision group.

The acceptance record can stay compact:

| Field | Required record |
| --- | --- |
| Inputs | Frozen, buyer-owned test cases |
| Baseline | The current supplier's results on those cases |
| Grader | The named person or system evaluating outcomes |
| Limits | Agreed error, completion-time, and cost thresholds |
| Evidence | Source trail, decision log, and retention period |
| Recovery | The owner and deadline for restoring the original service |

The handover package should include versioned workflow settings, model instructions, system connections, delegated permissions, and data lineage, the record of where inputs came from and how they changed. A replacement should fail even after producing an acceptable decision if its evidence cannot be reconstructed, its authority exceeds agreed permissions, or the original service cannot be restored safely. One failure shows that a workable exit remains unproven, not that migration will always be impossible; it should trigger funded repair and a retest.

This is a broader claim than data portability, which moves files, source-code escrow, which releases code under agreed conditions, or ordinary workflow migration, which moves known components. Exit proof asks whether another team can operate the whole process to the accepted business standard without hidden dependence on the current supplier.

The [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law, shows why disclosure remains separate. Its duties for general-purpose AI models, systems built to perform many tasks and support other applications, have applied since August 2, 2025. Most of the Act began applying on August 2, 2026. Documentation and transparency still do not prove that a buyer can transfer a working process.

I think exit proof also reveals where technical portability ends. A replacement may reproduce the workflow while exclusive data, licensing terms, or scarce computing capacity still let the original supplier dictate the price. The handover can succeed and the buyer can remain economically dependent. That result leads back to ownership.

Alaska secured a public claim before oil income became routine. AI infrastructure may also become routine, but neither ownership claims nor practical exit rights will appear unless we establish them while the rules are still fluid.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof is a proposed handover test for critical AI workflows, agreed at signing and run before renewal against buyer-owned cases, measurable limits, traceable sources, and safe restoration. A technically successful handover can still leave the buyer dependent on exclusive data, licensing, or scarce capacity. AI infrastructure may become routine; ownership claims and practical exit rights will not secure themselves.</p></div>
