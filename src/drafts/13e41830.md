Alaska settled an ownership question before its oil income became routine. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was being built to carry North Slope oil, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/) and reserved part of the state's mineral revenue. The first resident dividend followed in 1982. The public claim came first.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a policy recommendation expressed through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and proposes policies for navigating it. It follows [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's April 2025 scenario, and four of AI 2027's five authors also worked on Plan A. The authors say Plan A is primarily a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my speculative book about AI's economic transition, has a [retail publication date of March 22, 2026](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GTK53C9H), and its PDF was public on my site by March 23. I use the [June 24 edition](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) here. A [public link to Plan A appeared on July 9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). That order establishes timing, not influence or predictive accuracy.

I think the narrower comparison is more useful. The works converge on an economic break and a public dividend, then split over power. Together they suggest three tests: verification constrains hidden computing capacity, ownership allocates the gains, and exit proof, a live handover test, shows whether an institution retains bargaining power when an AI contract comes up for renewal.

| Test | Failure it addresses | Evidence required | Decision owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Verification | Undeclared large-scale computing capacity | Public transaction records and physical inspection | States, regulators, or treaty bodies |
| Ownership | AI-generated gains accrue to a narrow group | A legally enforceable claim on output or revenue | Legislatures and the citizens or asset owners named in law |
| Exit proof | A vital process cannot move away from one supplier | A replacement team completes a live handover against buyer-owned cases | The accountable business executive and chief information officer |

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 is a conditional policy scenario, while The Three Crucibles is my 2026 speculative book. Their overlap is thematic rather than predictive: both connect automated knowledge work to a public dividend, while their disagreement separates control into verification, ownership, and a tested ability to leave a supplier.</p></div>

## The closest overlap begins in the Void

My book's labels require care because two first-party versions differ. The opening map in the [June 24 PDF names the crucibles Correction, Void, and Alignment](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6). The current book page uses Transformation as the short label for the second, while the PDF places a Transformation chapter inside the Void. I follow the PDF's three-part map here.

The [Correction](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=19) describes inflated AI expectations meeting uneven returns, followed by company failures and pressure to automate. Plan A has no close equivalent because it starts from geopolitical rivalry and a race to improve advanced AI. The two maps begin in different places.

Their closest economic overlap appears in the Void. My book imagines output recovering while many jobs do not, and its Transformation chapter states the demand problem plainly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” In Plan A's scenario, AI performs [one third of all cognitive labor by mid-2031](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=13), meaning work based mainly on information, analysis, communication, and judgment. The scenario then projects about 50 percent real economic growth in 2032 even as automation erodes the tax base.

The tax mechanism matters. As wages disappear, governments collect less personal-income and payroll tax. At the same time, companies can expense or depreciate spending on data centers, factories, and robots, which lowers reported taxable profit while the build-out is under way. Production can rise quickly while household demand and public revenue weaken.

Both works answer with a Citizen's Dividend, a public payment tied to AI-driven productivity. [My book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37), based on what a person doing equivalent work would have contributed through taxes and social payments. [Plan A proposes tradable permits](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17) for new robot and computing capacity, then directs part of the revenue to a corporation owned equally by US citizens. In the scenario, the dividend rises from $45,000 per American adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035, measured in 2025 dollars.

I think this convergence is meaningful because it includes the broken income loop, the Alaska precedent, and the same policy name. Yet “Citizen's Dividend” predates both works, and Plan A's figures are scenario projections rather than observed evidence. The designs also differ: one taxes AI work that replaces human contributions, while the other charges for permission to expand the machines doing it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The June 24, 2026 edition of my book maps Correction, Void, and Alignment, with Transformation inside the Void. Plan A has no matching market Correction, but both works reach the same income-and-tax problem and propose a Citizen's Dividend. They agree on the break while choosing different ways to fund the response.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership govern different failures

Plan A is much stronger on the technical danger that my book largely brackets. Its scenario has the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029. Without that agreement, the authors model fully automated AI research and superintelligence by the end of 2030. Under the deal, development slows, pauses at top-human-expert ability in 2035, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. Takeoff is the period when AI automates enough AI research to accelerate its own improvement; superintelligence means AI significantly better than the best humans across fields while also working faster and more cheaply. These are scenario dates, not promised events.

The agreement depends on verification. Major data-center owners and chip suppliers report large transactions publicly, rival powers inspect physical infrastructure, and AI research becomes almost entirely transparent. Plan A stress-tests this design with [an alternate covert-project branch](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45) in which China diverts roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents to tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station, with about 200 people aware. An H100-equivalent is a measure of AI computing performance based on Nvidia's H100 processor, rather than a literal chip count.

The [Alignment passage in my book](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=56) asks a different question: who owns AI infrastructure and receives its output? Plan A gives citizens shares in the corporation receiving permit revenue, which creates an economic claim but not direct ownership of the chips, models, or companies. My proposal distributes infrastructure among individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, including AI models that can run on local hardware.

I don't know which balance could survive geopolitical pressure. I think the disagreement matters more than a forced match. Plan A tackles secret capacity and loss of control, where my book offers no comparable plan for containing superintelligence. My book goes further on distributed ownership, which inspections cannot create. Verification may expose a breach, but it cannot decide who holds economic power afterward.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses public reporting, inspections, and transparent research to constrain a race toward superintelligence. The Alignment passage in my book asks who owns AI infrastructure and its output. Verification can reveal hidden capacity but cannot distribute ownership; ownership does not by itself contain superintelligence.</p></div>

## The third test appears at renewal

These state-level questions become immediate when a hospital or insurer renews an AI contract. My book illustrates the pressure with [two fictional insurers](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21). One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. The other uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that carry out parts of the claims process, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The numbers are illustrative, but the mechanism is clear: adoption can outrun a buyer's ability to reverse it.

My take is that exit proof should become mandatory when a business-critical AI workflow handles regulated decisions or an operationally essential process. Low-risk tools may need only a tested data export and documented configuration. High-impact workflows need a full handover test, with the scope classified at signing rather than negotiated when the buyer is already dependent.

The sequence can stay simple. Contract the supplier-funded test at signing, including a fixed budget cap. Run it before renewal on one critical workflow, with one replacement team and a 30-day window. The existing AI portfolio review, with business, technology, procurement, legal, and risk represented, should resolve disputes. The executive accountable for the process judges the business result, while the chief information officer owns the renewal decision and rollback.

The acceptance contract should name the inputs, the incumbent's baseline on the same cases, the outcome grader, limits for error, time, and cost, how long evidence must be kept, and who restores the original service if the test fails. The handover package should include workflow settings saved with their version history, model instructions, connections to other business systems, permissions, source records, restoration guidance, and data lineage, the record of where inputs came from and how they changed. The buyer should own the historical cases and expected outcomes used for evaluation.

A replacement may change the model, instructions, search method, or workflow if the business result stays inside the agreed limits. It should still fail if it reaches an acceptable decision but cannot reconstruct the source trail, uses permissions that were never delegated, or leaves the buyer unable to roll back safely. One failure would not prove migration impossible forever. It would show that a workable exit remains unproven, stop automatic renewal, and trigger funded transition help before a retest.

This goes beyond familiar portability controls. Data portability moves files, source-code escrow releases code under agreed conditions, and normal workflow migration moves components. Exit proof tests the larger claim: can a replacement team operate the business process to an accepted standard without hidden dependence on the incumbent?

The [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law, shows why disclosure remains separate. Rules for general-purpose AI models, which can perform many tasks and be integrated into other systems, have applied since August 2, 2025; most of the Act began applying on August 2, 2026. Its documentation and transparency duties do not demonstrate that a buyer can transfer a working process.

I think exit proof is falsified as a bargaining-power test if a replacement can reproduce the workflow but exclusive data, licensing terms, or scarce computing capacity still let the incumbent dictate the price. Even verification, a public claim on AI-generated wealth, and a successful technical handover can coexist with economic dependence. The three tests therefore expose different failures; they do not guarantee freedom when a necessary input remains scarce.

Alaska secured the public claim before oil income became routine. With AI, institutions must decide who can inspect the infrastructure, who receives its output, and who can walk away before dependence hardens around them. The machines may be built either way. Those claims are still choices.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof is a proposed supplier-funded handover test for critical AI workflows, agreed at signing and run before renewal against buyer-owned cases, explicit limits, traceable sources, and a safe rollback. It fails as a bargaining-power test when a technically successful handover still leaves the buyer captive to exclusive data, licensing, or scarce capacity. AI infrastructure will become routine; the open choice is which claims we secure before it does.</p></div>
