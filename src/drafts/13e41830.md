In 1976, Alaska amended its constitution to reserve part of its mineral revenue. Prudhoe Bay, the oil field on Alaska's North Slope, was about to generate oil money. The Alaska Permanent Fund paid its first resident dividend in 1982, making public ownership concrete while the terms were negotiable.

Fifty years later, that design reappears in [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), a 2026 scenario from the AI Futures Project, a forecasting group. Four of the five authors of the 2025 rapid-AI scenario *AI 2027* also worked on Plan A. Plan A calls itself a recommendation rather than a best-guess forecast. It proposes permits for robots and AI computing capacity, the chips, data centers, power, and equipment used to train and run advanced AI systems, then returns most permit revenue to citizens.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my book on three stages of AI-driven economic change, reaches the same term and precedent: a Citizen's Dividend, meaning a public claim on wealth created by AI infrastructure. Its PDF was public in my site's repository on March 23, 2026; this edition is dated June 24; and a public post linked to Plan A on July 9. That establishes public sequence, not private origin or influence.

I think the comparison matters because it separates three controls. Verification uses inspectable evidence to reveal prohibited AI capacity, while ownership determines who may use the systems and receive their returns. Exit proof then tests whether a buyer can hand a working process to another supplier rather than trust a contract.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works independently connect automation, weaker labor income, and a public dividend. Their divergence reveals three questions: what can be inspected, who owns the gains, and who can still leave.</p></div>

## The closest match sits inside the Void

The [June 24 edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment. The Correction is competition driving firms to automate, while the Void is the period when machine output grows as jobs, labor income, and demand fall behind. The Alignment is the choice between broad stewardship and concentrated control. Transformation is a chapter within the Void, not the second crucible's name.

Plan A lacks a direct counterpart to the Correction's company-level pressure, but its economic scenario enters the same Void. By mid-2031, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, work based on analysis, communication, research, or judgment. As employment and labor income fall, the tax base shrinks as a share of the economy. Robot and computing permits then produce a projected [$50 trillion in US revenue in fiscal 2032 and $180 trillion in 2034](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17).

Plan A's proposed Compute Dividend Corporation makes that revenue claim precise. It receives a legally defined share of permit fees, starting at 25 percent in 2032 and rising to 75 percent in 2035. Every US citizen holds one share, and the corporation distributes the resulting profits as equal dividends. In the scenario, payments begin at [$45,000 per American adult in 2032 and approach $1 million in 2035](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18).

The book takes a different route. Companies pay an AI Labor Equivalence Tax for each human-equivalent unit of AI labor they deploy, in proportion to the public revenue a person in that role would have generated through income tax, payroll charges, and social-security contributions. The tax supports the Citizen's Dividend because falling wages also weaken demand. As the book puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.”

I think this independent overlap is meaningful, but it does not validate the book's dates. Both works follow automation through lost labor revenue to a citizen dividend and use Alaska to show how a public claim can work. One taxes human-equivalent machine work; the other raises public revenue from scarce permits.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The strongest convergence lies in the Void: automation weakens income and demand, so AI-created wealth returns to citizens. The book taxes human-equivalent AI labor; Plan A assigns permit fees to a citizen-owned corporation.</p></div>

## Verification can reveal capacity without deciding ownership

Plan A's governance problem begins with takeoff, a feedback loop in which AI automates research and speeds its own improvement. In its scenario, a 2029 US-China agreement blocks a route to superintelligence by the end of 2030, meaning systems that exceed humans across almost all intellectual work. Development pauses at top-expert capability in 2035, comparable to the best human specialists, then resumes toward superintelligence in 2040.

A pause works only if neither side can build in secret, so Plan A requires declared chip and data-center transactions, reciprocal inspections, and almost entirely public AI research. Its covert-project appendix tests this verification regime with computing power equal to roughly 500,000 high-end AI chips hidden in tunnels at the Medog Hydropower Station in Tibet. The construction site and abundant electricity could mask the facility; about 200 people know within the scenario.

Like *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film whose simulated world depends on physical machines, advanced AI cannot float free of chips, power lines, cooling systems, buildings, and supply chains. Verification becomes plausible because those traces are difficult to erase completely.

Visible capacity can still be privately controlled, so the book answers with distributed AI ownership. Open infrastructure means individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities own the computers on which AI runs. Open models are trained AI systems whose files can be downloaded and run on that local hardware. Legal limits on private monopoly protect access; local owners use the output directly, while the labor tax and dividend spread part of the return.

Plan A gives citizens permit revenue, but not ownership of the chips, models, or companies producing it. The book argues for broad ownership but supplies neither cross-border inspections nor technical safety cases for systems nearing superintelligence. I think neither work closes both problems. I don't know which approach would better survive geopolitical pressure, and Plan A's authors do not claim certainty either.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A makes secret national AI capacity harder to hide. The Three Crucibles asks who controls lawful, visible systems and receives their output. Inspection can reveal a machine without settling its ownership.</p></div>

## The same control problem reaches the contract desk

The scale changes inside a company, but the logic survives. State inspection tests for hidden national capacity; a buyer's handover test asks whether supplier dependence makes departure impractical. Both replace reassurance with evidence.

The book illustrates the pressure with two fictional insurers. One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; an AI-powered rival cuts staff by 40 percent, reduces premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months ([printed p. 20](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21)). That speed rewards adoption before anyone proves the claims process can be transferred.

My take is that renewal should depend on exit proof: a supplier-funded, 30-day handover of one workflow and one usable data extract to one replacement team. The chief information officer should own the decision. The supplier must provide data and decision history, versioned settings and model instructions, permissions, software connections, records of where source information came from and how it changed, plus operating and recovery guidance.

The replacement may change the AI model, system instructions, workflow, or the way it finds the source information it uses. The acceptance test remains fixed. On a buyer-owned evaluation set, an agreed sample of accepted historical cases, it must reproduce the business result within limits for errors, time, and cost.

Missing access, unusable data, information trapped inside the supplier's service, missing operating instructions, or a result outside the limits should block automatic renewal. The supplier then funds correction and transition support. A second failure lets the buyer leave without a termination fee.

The [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's main AI law, shows the limit of disclosure. Rules for general-purpose AI models, systems trained for many tasks, have applied since August 2, 2025; most of the law began applying on August 2, 2026. Article 50 requires disclosure in some AI interactions and software-readable markings for some AI-generated or manipulated content, meaning labels software can detect. None proves that another team can keep a business process running.

I think that is the failure beyond transparency. An institution may verify capacity and secure part of the gains, yet lose bargaining power if no replacement has tested the path out. Control becomes real when the right to leave survives contact with the system.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof is a supplier-funded handover test owned by the chief information officer and tied to renewal. If a replacement cannot reproduce one agreed result from buyer-owned cases within fixed limits, the buyer lacks practical control.</p></div>
