Alaska settled an ownership question before its oil income became routine. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was being built to carry North Slope oil, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/) and reserved part of the state's mineral revenue. The first resident dividend followed in 1982. The public claim came first.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a policy recommendation expressed through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and proposes ways to navigate it. It follows [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's April 2025 scenario, and four of AI 2027's five authors also worked on Plan A. The authors explicitly say Plan A is primarily a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my speculative book about AI's economic transition, has a [retail publication date of March 22, 2026](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GTK53C9H), and its PDF was public on my site by March 23. A [public link to Plan A appeared on July 9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). That chronology establishes publication order, not influence or predictive accuracy.

I think the narrower comparison is more useful. The two works approach the future from different directions, yet converge on an economic break and a public dividend before splitting over power. Their disagreement exposes three tests that are often compressed into the word *governance*: verification of hidden computing capacity, ownership of AI-generated gains, and exit proof, a proposed live handover test that shows whether an institution can leave a supplier when a critical AI contract comes up for renewal.

| Test | Failure it addresses | Evidence required | Decision owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Verification | Undeclared large-scale computing capacity | Transaction records and physical inspection | States, regulators, or treaty bodies |
| Ownership | AI-generated gains accrue to a narrow group | A legally enforceable claim on output or revenue | Legislatures and the citizens or asset owners named in law |
| Exit proof | A vital process cannot move away from one supplier | A replacement team completes a live handover against buyer-owned cases | The accountable business executive and chief information officer |

Their overlap is thematic rather than predictive: both connect automated knowledge work to a public dividend, while their disagreement separates the ability to inspect infrastructure, hold a claim on its gains, and leave a supplier.</p></div>

## The economic break is the real convergence

The public book page, which is authoritative for my taxonomy, names the First Crucible as [The Correction](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), the Second as The Transformation, and the Third as The Alignment. The Correction describes inflated AI expectations meeting uneven returns, followed by company failures and stronger pressure to automate. Plan A has no close equivalent because it begins with geopolitical rivalry and a race to improve advanced AI. The maps start in different places.

They meet in The Transformation. My book imagines output recovering while many jobs do not and states the demand problem plainly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” In Plan A's scenario, AI performs [one third of all cognitive labor by mid-2031](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=13), meaning work based mainly on information, analysis, communication, and judgment. The scenario then projects about 50 percent real economic growth in 2032 even as automation erodes the tax base.

The mechanism matters because production and purchasing power can move in opposite directions. As wages disappear, governments collect less personal-income and payroll tax. Companies can also expense or depreciate spending on data centers, factories, and robots, reducing taxable profit during the build-out. Output can rise quickly while household demand and public revenue weaken.

Both works answer with a Citizen's Dividend, a public payment tied to AI-driven productivity. [My book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37), based on what a person doing equivalent work would have contributed through taxes and social payments. [Plan A instead proposes tradable permits](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17) for new robot and computing capacity, then directs part of the revenue to a corporation owned equally by US citizens. In its scenario, the dividend rises from $45,000 per American adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035, measured in 2025 dollars.

The Alignment carries the shared question into politics: will citizens become stewards of abundance, or will a narrow group control the productive system? Plan A also tries to spread the gains rather than permit irreversible concentration. I think the convergence is meaningful because it includes the broken income loop, the Alaska precedent, and the same policy name. Yet “Citizen's Dividend” predates both works, and Plan A's figures are projections, not observed evidence. The overlap strengthens the structure of the question; it does not prove either timetable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The canonical sequence in my book is The Correction, The Transformation, and The Alignment. Plan A has no matching market correction, but both works reach the same income-and-tax problem and propose a Citizen's Dividend. They agree on the economic break while choosing different ways to fund the response.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership govern different failures

Plan A is much stronger on the technical danger that my book largely brackets. Its scenario has the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029. Without that agreement, the authors model fully automated AI research and superintelligence by the end of 2030. Under the deal, development slows, pauses at top-human-expert ability in 2035, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. *Takeoff* is the period when AI automates enough AI research to accelerate its own improvement; *superintelligence* means AI significantly better than the best humans across fields while also working faster and more cheaply. These are scenario dates, not promised events.

That acceleration requires a physical build-out. Plan A projects growth from roughly 20 million H100-equivalents in 2026 to 60 billion in 2034. An H100-equivalent is a measure of AI computing performance based on Nvidia's H100 processor, rather than a literal chip count. To constrain secret capacity, major data-center owners and chip suppliers would report large transactions, rival powers would inspect physical infrastructure, and AI research would become almost entirely transparent.

Plan A then stress-tests its own design with [an alternate covert-project branch](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45). In that branch, China diverts roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents into tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station, with about 200 people aware. The operation is fictional and conditional, but its purpose is revealing: even an ambitious verification system must account for concealment, collusion, and the possibility that physical evidence arrives too late.

The Alignment in my book asks a different question: who owns AI infrastructure and receives its output? Plan A gives citizens shares in the corporation receiving permit revenue, which creates an economic claim but not direct ownership of the chips, models, or companies. My proposal distributes infrastructure among individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, including AI models that can run on local hardware.

I don't know which balance could survive geopolitical pressure. I think the disagreement matters more than a forced match. Plan A tackles secret capacity and loss of control, where my book offers no comparable plan for containing superintelligence. My book goes further on distributed ownership, which inspections cannot create. Verification may expose a breach, but it cannot decide who holds economic power afterward; ownership alone cannot contain a system more capable than its owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses public reporting, inspections, and transparent research to constrain a race toward superintelligence. The Alignment asks who owns AI infrastructure and its output. Each closes a gap left by the other: inspection does not distribute power, while distributed ownership does not make superintelligence safe.</p></div>

## The third test begins at the contract desk

These state-level questions become immediate when a hospital or insurer renews an AI contract. My book illustrates the pressure with [two fictional insurers](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21). One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. The other uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that carry out parts of the claims process, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The numbers are illustrative, but the mechanism is clear: adoption can outrun a buyer's ability to reverse it.

My take is that exit proof should become mandatory when a business-critical AI workflow handles regulated decisions or an operationally essential process. Low-risk tools may need only a tested data export and documented configuration. High-impact workflows need a full handover test, with accountable business and technology owners. The scope must be classified at signing because the leverage exists before dependence does.

A practical sequence is to classify the workflow, contract a supplier-funded test with a fixed budget cap, run it before renewal, and escalate failed critical workflows. One workflow, one replacement team, and a 30-day window make a useful starting point because they bound cost and disruption while still exposing hidden dependencies. That is a proposed baseline, not a universal standard. The existing AI portfolio forum, with business, technology, procurement, legal, and risk represented, should settle disputes; the accountable business executive judges the outcome, while the chief information officer owns renewal and rollback.

The acceptance contract should first name the buyer-owned inputs and expected outcomes, then record the current supplier's baseline on the same cases. It should specify the outcome grader, limits for error, time, and cost, and how long the evidence must be kept. A separate recovery clause should name who restores the original service if the test fails.

The handover package should include workflow settings with their version history, model instructions, connections to other business systems, delegated permissions, source records, and restoration guidance. It also needs data lineage, the record of where inputs came from and how they changed. A replacement should fail even if it reaches an acceptable decision when it cannot reconstruct the source trail, uses permissions that were never delegated, or leaves the buyer unable to roll back safely. One failed test would not prove migration impossible forever. It would show that a workable exit remains unproven and trigger funded transition help before a retest.

This goes beyond familiar portability controls. Data portability moves files, source-code escrow releases code under agreed conditions, and normal workflow migration moves components. Exit proof tests the larger claim: can a replacement team operate the business process to an accepted standard without hidden dependence on the current supplier?

The [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law, shows why disclosure remains separate. Rules for general-purpose AI models, which can perform many tasks and be integrated into other systems, have applied since August 2, 2025; most of the Act began applying on August 2, 2026. Its documentation and transparency duties do not demonstrate that a buyer can transfer a working process.

I think exit proof fails as a bargaining-power test if a replacement reproduces the workflow but exclusive data, licensing terms, or scarce computing capacity still let the original supplier dictate the price. An institution could therefore pass inspection, hold a public claim on AI-generated wealth, and complete a technical handover while remaining economically dependent. The tests reveal different failures; they cannot make a scarce input abundant.

Alaska secured the public claim before oil income became routine. AI infrastructure can be built with public ownership claims and tested exit rights, or without them. The hardware may arrive under either arrangement. The claims will not appear unless we make them.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof is a proposed supplier-funded handover test for critical AI workflows, agreed at signing and run before renewal against buyer-owned cases, explicit limits, traceable sources, and safe rollback. It fails as a bargaining-power test when a technically successful handover still leaves the buyer captive to exclusive data, licensing, or scarce capacity. AI infrastructure may become routine; ownership claims and exit rights will not secure themselves.</p></div>
