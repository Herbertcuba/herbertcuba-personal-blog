One of Alaska's most consequential oil choices came before the windfall. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was being built to carry crude from Prudhoe Bay, the large oil field on Alaska's North Slope, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/). The fund would save part of the state's mineral income, and its first resident dividend followed in 1982. Alaskans secured a public claim while the terms were still open.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a 2026 policy recommendation told as a scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that studies advanced AI and how governments might navigate it. Four of the five authors of [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's 2025 scenario about a rapid path to superhuman AI, also worked on Plan A. Its authors are explicit that Plan A is primarily a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast.

My book [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) was [published on March 22, 2026](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GTK53C9H). A public link to Plan A appeared on July 9. That sequence proves neither influence nor accuracy, and a scenario cannot confirm my dates as external data. The book's canonical sequence is the Correction, the Transformation, and the Alignment. I think the stronger claim is more useful anyway: the two maps reach a remarkably similar economic fork, then reveal different requirements for control.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A does not validate The Three Crucibles as a forecast. It strengthens a specific economic diagnosis, while the differences between the two works expose the need to separate verification, ownership, and practical freedom to leave.</p></div>

## The economic sequence converges

The Correction is the book's financial reckoning: overheated AI valuations fall, weaker firms disappear, and surviving companies face sharper pressure to automate. Plan A starts from geopolitics rather than a market correction, but its scenario reaches a related labor shock. By mid-2031, it has AI performing a third of all cognitive labor, meaning work done mainly through information, analysis, communication, and judgment.

That shift creates a problem even if unemployment is temporary. In [Plan A's 2032 economy](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17), real output grows by roughly 50 percent while the labor-based tax system starts breaking. As paid human work falls, personal-income and payroll-tax receipts fall with it. Heavy investment in robots, factories, and data centers can also reduce taxable corporate profit through deductions and depreciation. Production can soar while public revenue and household demand weaken. As *The Three Crucibles* puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.”

The Transformation, the second crucible, asks what can replace wages as both household income and fuel for demand. The book proposes a Citizen's Dividend, a direct public claim on the productivity of AI infrastructure. Plan A uses the same name and the same Alaska precedent. In its scenario, the United States sells tradable permits to build robots or computing capacity, meaning the chips and facilities used to train and run AI. Part of those fees legally belongs to a Compute Dividend Corporation; every US citizen receives one share, and its profits are divided equally. The scenario projects an adult payment of $45,000 in 2032 and roughly $1 million in 2035, in 2025 dollars.

The funding methods differ. *The Three Crucibles* proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax tied to the tax and social contributions a person in an equivalent role would have generated. Plan A charges for permission to add physical capacity. I think the overlap matters because both works connect automation to shrinking labor income and the need to distribute machine-generated wealth. Their agreement is structural, not proof that either timeline is right or that either work invented the phrase Citizen's Dividend.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Correction and Transformation meet Plan A in the same economic chain: automation can raise output while weakening labor income, tax receipts, and consumer demand. Both works answer with a public claim on machine-generated wealth, although one would tax equivalent AI labor and the other would sell capacity permits.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership answer different questions

The Alignment, the third crucible, is the political test. The public book page describes a choice between owners who become “stewards of abundance” and owners who become “the new feudal lords.” Plan A sees the same danger of concentrated power, but approaches it through takeoff governance. Takeoff is the period when AI automates enough AI research to accelerate its own improvement, potentially turning a technology race into a much faster and harder-to-control process.

In [Plan A's proposed timeline](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=4), a 2029 agreement between the United States and China prevents fully automated AI research from producing superintelligence by the end of 2030. Superintelligence means systems that outperform the best humans across fields while also working faster and more cheaply. Development continues within the human range until a 2035 pause at top-human-expert AI, roughly the capability of the strongest specialists, before scaling resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. These are stages in the authors' preferred path, not observed milestones.

The plan therefore depends on verification, meaning evidence that countries are obeying agreed limits. Major chip and data-center transactions are declared, rival powers inspect infrastructure, and AI research becomes almost entirely transparent. Yet the report's own covert-project appendix imagines roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents installed in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station in China. An H100-equivalent is a standard way to compare computing power across different hardware, not a literal chip count. Ongoing dam construction provides cover, while small modular nuclear reactors and gas turbines provide power before the dam is complete. The authors estimate that only about 200 people would know.

This is where *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film about intelligent machines running a simulated reality, becomes a useful metaphor. Its green code looks weightless, but the machine world behind it still needs industrial hardware and energy. Advanced AI has the same physical shadow: chips, electricity, cooling systems, and buildings create traces that inspectors can seek even when the software remains hidden.

Ownership changes the question. Plan A gives each citizen a legal share in the dividend corporation, rather than in the chips, models, or companies producing the value. *The Three Crucibles* argues for distributed AI ownership through infrastructure held by individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, alongside models that can run locally or on an owner's premises. I think the distinction is fundamental. Verification asks whether actors follow limits; ownership determines who holds a claim on capacity, output, and bargaining power. I don't know which balance can survive geopolitical pressure, and Plan A's authors do not claim certainty. A system can be visible to inspectors while a small group still sets its terms of access.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses verification to constrain hidden computing capacity, while Alignment asks who controls visible infrastructure and receives its gains. Inspectability can expose rule-breaking, but ownership determines who can use the system and bargain over its output.</p></div>

## Control becomes real at the exit test

The comparison leaves one more boundary: whether an institution can change suppliers without losing the process that now carries out its work. *The Three Crucibles* imagines one insurer retaining 5,000 claims adjusters while a rival deploys coordinated AI agents, software systems that divide and execute the claims process. The rival cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. This is a thought experiment rather than a reported case, but it shows why adoption pressure can move faster than a buyer's ability to leave.

My take is that every critical AI contract should require exit proof before renewal: a supplier-funded handover test showing that a replacement team can run one working process. A useful first scope is one claims workflow, one portable data extract, one replacement team, and a 30-day test. The chief information officer should own the renewal decision, while the buyer should own the past cases used to evaluate the replacement.

The handover package should include the current workflow configuration, model instructions, tool connections, permissions, source records, and restoration guidance. It also needs data lineage, a record of where each input came from and how it changed. The replacement team may change the model, instructions, the way relevant records are supplied to the model, or the workflow design. What must remain stable is the agreed business result on the buyer's evaluation cases, within fixed limits for error, time, and cost.

Failure needs a contractual meaning. Missing records, blocked access, results outside the agreed limits, or decisions that cannot be traced to their sources should fail the test. That should stop automatic renewal and trigger funded transition assistance; a second failed test should let the buyer leave without a termination fee. The leverage exists before the contract is signed.

Regulation covers a related boundary. Since August 2, 2025, the [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law, has applied rules for general-purpose AI models. These are models with significant generality that can perform a wide range of distinct tasks and be integrated into downstream systems. Most of the law began applying on August 2, 2026, including transparency duties for specified AI interactions and AI-generated or manipulated content. Those rules can improve visibility, but they do not demonstrate that a buyer can transfer a working process to another supplier.

I think this is the second-order failure both maps leave exposed. Governments may verify computing capacity and citizens may receive part of its return, yet a hospital, insurer, or public agency can remain trapped inside the systems carrying out its work. Alaska secured a public claim before oil money became routine. A durable claim on AI power should include the ability to inspect the system, share in its gains, and transfer the work when the terms change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof turns supplier choice into a funded acceptance test with a named owner, an evaluation set, a deadline, and contractual consequences for failure. Verification governs hidden capacity, ownership governs economic claims, and exit proof preserves an institution's ability to move a working process.</p></div>
