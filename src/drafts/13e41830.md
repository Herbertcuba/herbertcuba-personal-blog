In 1976, Alaska voters put a rule between a resource boom and the state budget. Their constitutional amendment directed at least a quarter of certain mineral revenues into the Alaska Permanent Fund. The fund invests its principal, and dividend payments to eligible residents began in 1982. It is a practical answer to a philosophical question: when a place becomes rich because of a shared resource, who should hold a claim on that wealth?

Fifty years later, two documents use that answer to think about artificial intelligence. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) models a national dividend funded by permits for computing capacity and robots. My speculative book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), uses Alaska to explain a Citizen's Dividend, a direct public claim on wealth produced by AI infrastructure. The book has been public on my site since March 2026, months before Plan A appeared in July.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska turned shared resource wealth into a lasting public claim. Both AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles apply that principle to AI.</p></div>

## What Plan A is trying to prevent

Published in July 2026, Plan A is a scenario and policy proposal from the AI Futures Project, the forecasting nonprofit behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its 2025 scenario about rapid progress toward superhuman AI. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” It describes a path that its authors want governments to choose.

The proposed path starts with a 2029 agreement between the United States and China, intended to prevent a 2030 race driven by almost automated AI research. Development then stays within human capability until a 2035 pause at systems that match or surpass top human experts. In 2040, after further safeguards, scaling resumes toward superintelligence, meaning AI far beyond human ability across fields.

Its economic story is just as important. As AI performs more cognitive labor, wages and labor-tax revenue shrink relative to output, while households lose purchasing power. In the scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031 and real GDP, meaning inflation-adjusted economic output, grows about 50 percent in 2032. Plan A responds with tradable permits for compute, the chips and datacenters used to build and run AI, and for robots. Part of the permit income flows to citizens as dividends.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A proposes a controlled route to superintelligence while using permits and dividends to keep an automated economy connected to public income.</p></div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

The Three Crucibles approaches the same shift through three social pressures. **The Correction** is the moment AI productivity breaks the familiar link between human work and economic output. **The Void** is the unstable middle, when that old bargain is fading but replacement institutions are not yet secure. **The Alignment** is the eventual choice between broad stewardship of abundance and concentrated control by a small group of owners.

That middle period creates an immediate economic problem. Machines can produce, but they do not create consumer demand of their own. My book therefore proposes a Citizen's Dividend and a tax tied to productive work transferred from people to AI systems. The aim is to replace part of the income and public revenue that disappear when fewer wages are paid.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The three crucibles describe a broken labor bargain, a difficult transition, and a final choice over who controls and benefits from AI abundance.</p></div>

## Where the two maps meet and separate

The strongest overlap is not a date. It is the causal chain. Both maps expect AI production to weaken wage income, the existing tax base, and household demand. Both then reach for a Citizen's Dividend and the Alaska precedent. They also end at the same political fork: gains can spread broadly, or ownership can become more concentrated.

Their remedies differ. Plan A charges for scarce capacity before new compute or robots are built; The Three Crucibles taxes value after AI systems take on productive work. Plan A also puts more weight on verification, which means tracking chips, inspecting datacenters, and making research activity visible enough to check. Its own analysis of covert projects shows why secrecy remains a serious problem.

My book puts more weight on distributed ownership, so citizens hold an economic stake in the output itself. That does not make dangerous AI safe, just as inspections alone do not give the public a share of the gains. The two safeguards address different failures. We do not need to accept either timetable to see why capability controls and economic ownership belong in the same conversation.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The maps share a diagnosis but protect against different risks: Plan A makes AI activity more visible, while The Three Crucibles spreads the economic claim.</p></div>

## Why uncertainty still calls for preparation

There is no honest way to know whether either map will be right. AI progress may slow, the economic effects may arrive unevenly, and political institutions may respond better than expected. Plan A openly presents itself as a recommended path. The Three Crucibles exists for a similar reason: it is a preparation document, not a promise about the future.

But if these two maps are even directionally right, the road ahead will be bumpy. Productivity could move faster than income, law, and public legitimacy can adjust. The useful response is neither certainty nor panic. It is to prepare for the ownership, distribution, and control questions before events answer them for us.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>No one knows what waits on the road to 2040, but shared warning signs make preparation more responsible than confident prediction.</p></div>
