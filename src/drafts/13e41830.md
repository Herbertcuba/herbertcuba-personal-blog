In 1976, Alaska's voters created the Alaska Permanent Fund, a state-owned investment fund that saves part of the public revenue from a finite resource: oil. The first dividend was paid in 1982, and eligible residents have received yearly cash payments from the fund's earnings ever since.

That decision matters to the AI debate because it answered a question before the wealth became overwhelming. [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), a 2026 governance scenario from the US nonprofit AI Futures Project, and my 2026 speculative book, [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), both return to the same issue: if a resource produces extraordinary value, who should hold a claim on it? Their answers are remarkably close, although their safeguards are not.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Alaska turned shared resource wealth into a lasting public asset and a citizen dividend. Both future maps return to that model when asking who should benefit from AI.</p>
</div>

## What AI 2040 actually proposes

At 90 pages, *Plan A* combines a scenario with a policy recommendation. Four of the five authors credited on [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), an April 2025 scenario about rapid AI development, also appear on its byline. Yet the new document calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Its dates describe a preferred route through danger, not a timetable we can rely on.

In that route, a US–China agreement in 2029 prevents fully automated AI research and development in 2030. Development continues within the range of human ability, pauses around leading-expert level in 2035, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. The report uses *superintelligence* for AI that is broadly better than the best humans while also faster and cheaper.

The economic mechanism is as important as the capability jump. By mid-2031, AI performs a third of cognitive labor, meaning work based mainly on reasoning and information, in the scenario. Wage income and its tax revenue then weaken. Governments cap new robots and *compute*, the processing capacity used to train and run AI, and sell permits that grant the right to add more. Part of the fees goes to a proposed Compute Dividend Corporation, with one share and an equal dividend for every US citizen.

Plan A would enforce its limits through inspections, chip tracking, and research transparency. Its appendix tests that system against a hypothetical covert Chinese project, estimating below a 10% chance that it reaches top-expert-dominating AI, a level beyond leading human experts, while remaining undetected by 2043. *Verification* means producing evidence that the declared limits are actually being followed, and it is central to the proposal.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A imagines a managed pause, enforceable limits on AI capacity, and citizen dividends funded by permits when automation erodes wage-based income.</p>
</div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

*The Three Crucibles* is a preparation document rather than a calendar that must come true. By August 2026, its manuscript was more than six months old. A downloadable PDF and homepage link were added to my site on March 23, 2026, before AI 2040 was publicly available on July 9, 2026.

**The Correction** begins when productive intelligence moves from human labor into machines owned as capital. Work does not disappear overnight, but wages start losing their old role as the main route for income to reach households.

**The Void** is the unstable passage that follows. Production may rise while purchasing power and social confidence fall, because AI systems can make goods and services but do not create consumer demand by themselves.

**The Alignment** is the political choice at the end of that passage. AI-generated wealth can make many people owners and beneficiaries, or it can leave control with a small group of companies and states.

The book proposes spreading ownership of AI systems, creating an income floor that grows with displacement, and taxing AI labor. That tax would be a levy on firms when productive work moves from taxed human wages to AI systems, replacing part of the public revenue that disappears with those wages.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The three crucibles describe a correction in labor's value, a dangerous gap before new institutions stabilize, and a final choice over who owns and benefits from AI-generated wealth.</p>
</div>

## The agreement is deeper than the dates

Both works argue that automation can weaken wages, consumer demand, and the tax base even while total production rises. Both arrive at the name “Citizen's Dividend,” and both use the Alaska Permanent Fund to show how citizens might hold a direct claim on income from a productive asset. They also end at the same fork: broad benefit or concentrated power.

Their differences show what each map leaves exposed. AI 2040 would fund dividends through permits for scarce robots and compute; my book emphasizes taxes on AI labor and wider ownership. Plan A relies on verification to reveal dangerous capacity and enforce limits, while my book asks who owns the systems and their output. A successful secret project could evade verification, but perfect visibility could still leave the gains concentrated. Wider ownership, in turn, cannot make an uncontrolled superintelligence safe.

AI 2040 does not prove that my book is right. A scenario is an argument, not economic evidence. Yet a forecasting team focused on AI safety and a book grounded in economics and ownership reached the same structural problem within months of each other. That convergence makes the question harder to dismiss.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The maps converge on economic disruption, citizen dividends, and a power struggle. They diverge on the main safeguard, revealing why verification and ownership solve different problems.</p>
</div>

## Preparation is the point

Nobody knows whether Plan A's dates will hold, whether the crucibles will arrive in sequence, or whether physical and political limits will bend both maps beyond recognition. If the AI 2040 authors and I are even directionally right, the road ahead will be bumpy because rapid capability gains would collide with tax systems, labor markets, and ownership rules built for another economy.

That is why my book exists as a preparation document. Alaska acted before perfect knowledge was available and gave citizens a claim on resource wealth. We can still decide who may limit advanced AI and who holds a claim on what it produces, but those choices become harder once the wealth and power have settled.

<p>No forecast can remove uncertainty. Preparation means settling the rules for control and shared benefit before advanced AI makes those decisions much harder to reverse.</p>
