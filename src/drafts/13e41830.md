Two works about advanced AI use the same unusual policy label: the “Citizen's Dividend.” Both also point to Alaska's oil fund as a model for giving the public a claim on wealth produced by a scarce asset. That resemblance deserves attention, although it proves less than a validation story would suggest.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) is a 2026 policy recommendation told through a conditional future scenario. The AI Futures Project, a nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and proposes policy, created it. Its six credited authors include four of the five authors of [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), their April 2025 scenario about AI automating the work of designing better AI. [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my economic and political framework for the shift from human labor toward AI-dominated production.

The chronology is narrow. On March 23, 2026, the version history of the cubagarcia.com code repository records the addition of the book PDF and a site link. The current book PDF carries June 24, 2026 creation metadata; the current Plan A PDF carries July 11, 2026 creation metadata. These are repository and file-build milestones, not evidence of first publication, influence, who conceived the ideas first, or correctness.

Plan A explicitly says it is primarily a recommendation, not a prediction, so it cannot serve as data confirming my framework. I think the disagreement offers more value. Advanced-AI policy must prove two things separately: that dangerous computing capacity is visible and stoppable, and that the public has an enforceable claim on the value it creates. If one control always secures both, this Two-Key Test is redundant; if either can fail alone, we need both.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The artifact record proves neither validation nor influence. The comparison instead yields a falsifiable test: technical control and public economic rights matter separately only if one can fail while the other succeeds.</p></div>

## The agreement is an economic sequence

In Plan A's conditional path, a 2029 agreement between the United States and China prevents fully automated AI research and development in 2030, when AI would otherwise do most of the work of designing better AI. The scenario keeps AI systems within the human range and pauses their development in 2035 when they roughly match top human experts. In 2040, it resumes development toward superintelligence, defined as systems significantly better than the best humans at every task while also faster and cheaper ([AI 2040, pp. 4–5](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

*The Three Crucibles* uses another clock. Its public page labels the stages The Correction in 2026, The Transformation from 2027 to 2032, and The Alignment from 2035 onward. The PDF's opening map calls the middle Crucible “The Void,” then later introduces The Transformation as the movement into it. Across both surfaces, the argument moves from labor losing economic weight to redistribution and a choice between shared abundance and concentrated ownership.

The economic mechanism is nevertheless close. In Plan A's United States scenario, personal-income and payroll-tax revenue collapses as machines replace paid work, while corporate tax does not automatically close the gap because firms can deduct investments in datacenters, factories, and robots immediately or over time. Inflation-adjusted output still grows by about 50 percent in 2032 ([AI 2040, pp. 16–17](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). These are scenario outputs, not economic evidence.

Both works answer that break with a Citizen's Dividend, a direct public payment backed by productive assets or their revenue. Both invoke the Alaska Permanent Fund, a state investment fund established by a 1976 constitutional amendment to preserve part of Alaska's oil wealth; the first resident dividend checks followed in 1982. I think this is more specific than generic support for basic income, but it does not make the term new or prove that either work arrived at it independently. Both maps put the public claim at the same point in the causal chain.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The timelines differ, but both works trace the same sequence: automation reduces labor's economic role, wage-based public revenue weakens, and citizens need another claim on machine-created value.</p></div>

## The same dividend conceals different rights

Plan A would cap robots and AI computing capacity, the processing power supplied by chips and datacenters. Under cap-and-trade, the government limits total capacity and auctions permissions that companies may trade. A fixed share of permit fees goes to a Compute Dividend Corporation in which every United States citizen holds one share. Citizens own a claim on permit revenue, not the chips, datacenters, or models.

*The Three Crucibles* proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax at another point in the system. A company would pay for each human-equivalent unit of AI labor it deploys, in proportion to the public revenue a person doing that work would have generated. The book also argues for open infrastructure, open models, and legal limits on private monopoly, seeking broader access to the productive layer rather than only a share of state-collected revenue. Its demand-side warning is blunt: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Production can rise while displaced workers lose the income needed to buy what machines produce.

These collection points create different weaknesses. A capacity permit can collect before useful work begins, but hidden or offshore facilities may escape the cap. A labor-equivalence tax follows deployment, where officials must decide what human work the system performs and how to measure it. My take is that Plan A distributes income from government-limited capacity, while the book reaches toward wider power over the productive layer. Similar dividends can therefore sit on top of different ownership arrangements.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A gives citizens a share of permit revenue. The Three Crucibles taxes deployed AI labor and seeks broader access to infrastructure and models. The payment may look similar while the underlying rights remain different.</p></div>

## The two keys fail in different ways

Plan A builds technical control through declarations by major datacenter owners and chip suppliers, inspections, monitoring of new model-training runs, research transparency, and company safety cases. A safety case is an evidence-backed argument that a system will not cause irreversible catastrophe, exposed to criticism from the public, scientists, government auditors, and rival AI companies ([AI 2040, pp. 7–8 and 13](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

Its fictional alternate branch stress-tests that architecture. China hides an AI datacenter in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station project, where construction provides cover and underground heat is harder for infrared satellites to detect ([AI 2040, Appendix D, pp. 45–47](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). This is not a real covert project. Within the scenario, it shows how a verification regime can govern what it sees and miss what it cannot.

The obvious objection is that Plan A already combines compute controls with a citizen dividend, so two familiar policies do not automatically deserve a new framework. I agree. The Two-Key Test adds one testable question: can the outcomes separate under pressure? A declared facility could pass inspection while the dividend claim is diverted or becomes legally unenforceable. In reverse, registered facilities could fund a dividend while an off-record training run escapes the stop system.

Neither work supplies an observed Nordic case in which one national key passes and the other fails. That absence should remain a research question, not be filled with a convenient anecdote. I don't know which mechanisms will survive real political and technical pressure. I think the frame earns its keep only when each key has its own owner, evidence, pass condition, and transition it must reject. If no real failure can separate them, discard the distinction.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Visibility can succeed while distribution fails, and a dividend can be paid while hidden capacity escapes control. The Two-Key Test remains a hypothesis until those asymmetric failures can be tested against observed cases.</p></div>

## Put both tests into the decision record

The distinction becomes useful before a law, license, or supplier contract is difficult to unwind. A public technical record should name the regulated unit, evidence window, detection standard, maximum share of prohibited activity that monitoring may miss, and pause authority. A separate economic record needs the revenue base, required public share, beneficiaries, payment date, audit route, and recovery authority. An undeclared run fails the first even if dividends arrive; diverted revenue fails the second even if every datacenter is visible.

A company board controls a narrower boundary. *The Three Crucibles* imagines one insurer keeping 5,000 claims adjusters while another uses AI agents to cut headcount by 40 percent and premiums by 20 percent, then overwhelms its rival within six months. This is not operating evidence. An unvalidated pre-contract template still needs the exact supplier and model version, a baseline period, and the affected decisions and roles. It must also specify how often evidence arrives, monitoring and sampling rules, a cost limit, a review deadline, and named pause and restart owners.

The supplier contract makes that record enforceable. It should grant audit access, require notice before a model-version change, set evidence deadlines, and preserve pause, termination, and exit rights. Restart requires proof that the failed condition is repaired. An unannounced change, withheld evidence, missed worker consultation, or a system that cannot be paused must trigger rejection rather than another meeting.

Sweden's Co-Determination Act of 1976 clarifies the institutional boundary. Section 11 generally requires an employer bound by a collective agreement to negotiate with the relevant worker organization before an important change to the business or working conditions. Exceptional reasons can permit a decision first. The law gives workers a formal place in a company decision, but creates neither a national register of AI computing capacity nor a Citizen's Dividend. A board cannot pretend that consultation settles the public ownership question.

I think this is where the comparison becomes practical. Visible, stoppable capacity can still produce orderly extraction when the public claim is weak, while a well-funded dividend can coexist with hidden systems nobody can reliably stop. Advanced-AI strategy becomes credible only when neither apparent success conceals the other failure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Record technical control and economic rights separately before approval. Each needs measurable evidence, a named authority, a rejection condition, and a real remedy, because one successful key can never excuse failure of the other.</p></div>
