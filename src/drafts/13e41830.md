[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) cannot validate *The Three Crucibles*, because it is a policy scenario rather than observed data. Its value lies elsewhere: two works starting from different problems reach the same economic break, then expose each other's missing safeguard.

The AI Futures Project, a nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and proposes policy, released Plan A in 2026. Four of the five authors of its April 2025 [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/) scenario about AI automating research on better AI are among Plan A's six authors. [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my economic and political framework for the same transition. A March 23 repository commit added its PDF and link to my site; the current PDFs are dated June 24 and July 11. This records the artifacts' order, not influence, intellectual priority, or accuracy.

I think the useful result is a Two-Key Test. One key makes dangerous AI computing capacity visible and stoppable. The other gives citizens an enforceable claim on the value that capacity creates. If one control always secures both outcomes, the distinction is redundant. If either outcome can fail alone, advanced-AI strategy needs both.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The documents' order does not prove validation or influence. Their structural overlap matters because their disagreement reveals two safeguards that can fail independently: technical control and economic rights.</p></div>

## The agreement is economic

In Plan A's conditional path, a 2029 United States-China agreement averts fully automated AI research in 2030, when AI would do most of the work of designing better AI. Development remains within human ability until a 2035 pause at top-expert level, then resumes toward superintelligence in 2040: systems significantly better than the best humans at everything, while faster and cheaper ([AI 2040, pp. 4–5](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

*The Three Crucibles* runs on another clock. The Correction, dated 2026, moves productivity from labor toward capital. The public page calls the 2027–2032 middle stage The Transformation; the PDF's opening map calls it “The Void” and develops The Transformation within it. The Alignment, dated 2035 onward, asks whether AI's owners become stewards of abundance or new feudal lords.

The dates differ, but the economic mechanism is close. In Plan A's United States scenario, inflation-adjusted output rises by about 50 percent in 2032 while personal-income and payroll-tax revenue weakens as AI systems and robots replace paid work. Corporate tax does not automatically fill the gap because firms reinvest in datacenters, factories, and robots, then expense or depreciate those investments ([AI 2040, pp. 16–17](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). These are scenario outputs, not economic evidence.

Both works answer that break with a “Citizen's Dividend,” a direct public payment backed by productive assets or their revenue. Both point to the Alaska Permanent Fund, a state investment fund created by a 1976 constitutional amendment to preserve part of Alaska's oil wealth. Investment returns support resident dividends, first paid in 1982. I think the precedent matters because it establishes the public claim before the asset income becomes politically untouchable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The clocks do not match, but the causal sequence does: paid work loses economic weight, wage-based public revenue weakens, and a new claim on machine-created value becomes necessary.</p></div>

## The funding mechanisms disagree

Plan A would cap robots and AI computing capacity, the processing power supplied by chips and datacenters, then auction permits to expand either. A defined fee share goes to its Compute Dividend Corporation, a public dividend vehicle giving every United States citizen one share. That share is a claim on permit revenue, not the AI infrastructure.

*The Three Crucibles* instead proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax. A company would pay for each human-equivalent unit of AI labor it deploys, in proportion to the public revenue a person doing that work would have generated. The book also calls for distributed ownership through open infrastructure and models, plus legal limits on private monopoly. Its demand-side warning is blunt: “robots do not buy iPhones.” Production can soar while workers lose the wages needed to buy it.

A capacity permit collects before useful work, but hidden or offshore facilities can escape it. A labor-equivalence tax follows deployment, where arguments begin over which human task was replaced and how much work the system performed. My take is that Plan A distributes revenue from regulated scarcity, while the book also tries to distribute power over the productive layer.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both routes can fund a Citizen's Dividend, but they collect at different points and confer different rights. Permit revenue gives citizens an economic claim; distributed infrastructure reaches further into who can control production.</p></div>

## The failures can separate

Plan A requires major datacenter owners and chip suppliers to declare large transactions, inspectors to check the record, monitors to watch for new model-training runs, researchers to share findings, and companies to submit safety cases. A safety case is an evidence-backed argument that a system will not cause irreversible catastrophe, tested by auditors, scientists, rivals, and the public ([AI 2040, pp. 7–8 and 13](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

Its fictional alternate branch shows the weakness. China hides a large AI datacenter in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station project, where construction provides cover and underground heat is harder to detect from satellites ([AI 2040, Appendix D, pp. 45–47](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). As a scenario stress test rather than a real covert project, it shows how a capacity regime can govern everything it sees and fail at what it misses.

Ownership has a different blind spot. A declared facility could pass inspection while its public share is diverted, delayed, or unenforceable. The reverse can also happen: citizens receive money from registered facilities while an off-record training run escapes the stop system. I think these asymmetric failures are the actual finding. Neither document supplies an observed Nordic case in which one national key passes and the other fails, so that remains a research test rather than implied proof.

I don't know which of these mechanisms will survive real pressure. The Two-Key Test earns its keep only when each safeguard has its own owner, evidence, pass condition, and rejected transition. If the same enforceable control always moves both outcomes together, we should discard the distinction.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification can succeed while distribution fails, and a dividend can be paid while hidden capacity escapes control. The two-key idea is useful only if it detects one of those failures before outcomes are locked in.</p></div>

## Put both keys in the record

The test belongs before a law, license, or supplier contract becomes difficult to unwind. A public-capacity record must name the covered unit, evidence window, maximum undetected share, and pause authority. An undeclared run or a breach that cannot be halted is a rejection.

The public claim needs a separate record: revenue base, required share, beneficiaries, payment date, audit and appeal routes, and recovery authority. Underpayment or diversion fails even when every datacenter is visible. I think this prevents the actor controlling capacity from grading its own promise to share.

A company board has narrower powers. The book's thought experiment contrasts one insurer keeping 5,000 claims adjusters with another using AI agents to cut headcount by 40 percent, lower premiums by 20 percent, and overwhelm its rival within six months. This is not case evidence. An unvalidated pre-contract template would record a twelve-month baseline, exact system version, customer-appeal and cost limits, affected roles, and review date. The contract would require audit access, evidence delivery, version-change notice, a pause or exit right, and proof for restart.

Sweden's Co-Determination Act of 1976 shows the boundary between company process and public settlement. Section 11 generally requires an employer bound by a collective agreement to negotiate with the relevant worker organization before an important change to the business or working conditions, although exceptional reasons can permit a decision first. This gives workers a formal place in deployment decisions but creates neither a national AI-capacity register nor a Citizen's Dividend.

My take is that convergence gives us a question, not permission to declare victory. When the technical key passes and the economic key fails, advanced AI becomes orderly extraction. When the economic key passes and the technical key fails, society shares income from a system it cannot reliably see or stop. A credible settlement has to turn both keys.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Record technical control and economic rights separately before approval. Each needs a named authority, auditable evidence, a failure condition, and a real power to reject or reverse the transition; otherwise one apparent success will conceal the other failure.</p></div>
