In 1976, Alaska voters made an unusual decision about the state's mineral wealth. They amended the constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenue would enter the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a publicly owned investment fund. A related annual payment to eligible residents began in 1982. The first payment came from surplus oil revenue; fund earnings supported later dividends.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) uses Alaska as a model for giving citizens a claim on wealth created by AI infrastructure. The AI Futures Project, a US nonprofit studying advanced AI and policy, published Plan A on July 9, 2026. Four of its six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's 2025 scenario about a rapid AI race.

The framework behind my 2026 speculative book, [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), was in writing by February 3, more than six months before this article. The book was public on my site by March 23, 108 days before Plan A appeared. That timing proves neither influence nor accuracy. It makes the overlap worth examining.

The book has three crucibles. **The Correction** is the shock as AI makes more human labor optional. **The Void** is the unstable interval after the old bargain between work, income and consumption starts failing, but before a replacement is trusted. The public book page describes this 2027–2032 passage as **The Transformation**, while the current downloadable book names the crucible **The Void**. **The Alignment** is the settlement that follows: AI-created abundance is either broadly stewarded or concentrated among a few owners.

Placed together, the documents suggest that the decisive AI conflict may begin before superintelligence. Institutions can lose their economic balance while the technology is still within the human range.

## Plan A begins with a deal

Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Its preferred path starts with a US-China agreement in 2029 that prevents fully automated AI research in 2030, meaning AI does not take over almost all the work needed to build more capable successors. Capability grows within the broad human range until a 2035 pause around top-expert AI, systems able to outperform leading specialists across important fields. The scenario moves toward superintelligence only in 2040, after further safety work and international oversight.

The agreement depends on tracking advanced chips, inspecting datacenters and sharing research information. It also uses permits for compute, the processing capacity supplied by chips and datacenters. This control system supports an economic one. Plan A projects that AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031 and that real output grows by about 50 percent in 2032. As taxable human work shrinks, permit fees flow partly to a Compute Dividend Corporation in which every US citizen owns one share. Its allocation rises from 25 percent of those fees in 2032 to 75 percent in 2035.

<p>Plan A combines a verifiable slowdown with a citizen-owned fund designed to replace some income lost as human labor declines.</p>

## The overlap begins before superintelligence

The dates differ, but the sequence is close. My Correction anticipates pressure on wages and the tax base. The Void is the passage in which productivity rises while the old way of distributing purchasing power stops working. Both documents answer with a Citizen's Dividend and point to Alaska, although Plan A uses permit fees while my book proposes taxing value created by AI-performed work.

Ownership is explicit in both. Plan A includes “Diffuse AI Broadly” among its principles and gives every citizen one share in its dividend corporation. The Three Crucibles argues for distributed ownership of AI infrastructure and claims on its returns. Plan A goes much further into international controls; my framework puts more weight on the legitimacy of the economic settlement.

This gives us a testable near-term claim. If the shared sequence is right, disputes over who owns compute, robots and their returns will become central before anyone verifies superintelligence. For a Nordic strategist, the question is whether a welfare state built around taxable human work has identified what could support it when more value comes from machines.

## The difference is what each document prepares us for

Plan A designs a route: negotiate, verify, pause, distribute and eventually proceed. Its appendix on a possible covert Chinese project asks whether enough chips, power, facilities and people could be hidden to break the agreement. That analysis matters because a pause is only as credible as its verification.

The Three Crucibles has a different job. It is a preparatory document for the social passage, not a treaty design or a forecast of model capability. Its stages remain useful if Plan A's dates move or no clean international pause appears, because work, demand, ownership and legitimacy can become urgent without superintelligence.

No one knows what awaits us between now and 2040. Plan A's authors do not claim to know, and neither do I. But if their scenario and my framework are broadly right, we have a bumpy transition ahead. That uncertainty is why preparation matters: institutions are easier to redesign before economic disruption hardens into political crisis.
