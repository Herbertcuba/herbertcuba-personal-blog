Two documents about advanced AI arrive at the same unusual name for a public payment funded by the machine economy: the “Citizen’s Dividend.” Both also point to the Alaska Permanent Fund, the state investment fund established by constitutional amendment in 1976, whose first resident dividends were paid in 1982. That is a specific convergence. It is not proof.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) is a 2026 policy proposal told through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, the research nonprofit behind the April 2025 [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/) scenario. Plan A says it is “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my framework for an economy in which AI makes production less dependent on human labor.

I think the comparison matters because the works agree on the economic break and then separate at the question of power. Plan A concentrates on verification and human control during a staged slowdown, before its scenario hands more authority to AI in 2040. *The Three Crucibles* concentrates on who owns the productive system and who receives its gains. Any serious strategy for advanced AI must answer where technical control ends and who shares the gains.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The shared Citizen’s Dividend and Alaska precedent reveal a real structural overlap, but a conditional scenario cannot validate another future map. The useful comparison is between technical control and public economic rights.</p></div>

## The convergence is economic, not chronological

Plan A’s conditional timeline begins with a 2029 agreement between the United States and China. Without that deal, its authors say fully automated AI research and development would arrive in 2030. This means AI systems would do almost all the work of building better AI systems, which the scenario says would lead by year-end to superintelligence: systems significantly better than the best humans at every task while also faster and cheaper. With the deal, capability stays within the human range through 2035 and pauses near top-human-expert level. In 2040, the scenario resumes progress toward that level ([AI 2040, p. 4](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). The endpoint matters because institutions and equipment are then gradually handed to AIs until humans can no longer shut the whole system down ([AI 2040, pp. 31–32](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

The economic mechanism does not require the reader to accept that timetable. In the scenario, real gross domestic product, an inflation-adjusted measure of economic output, grows by about 50 percent in 2032. Yet personal-income and payroll-tax revenue falls as more paid work disappears. Companies also deduct heavy investment in datacenters, factories, and robots, which reduces taxable profit. Output rises while the tax system loses its usual route into that output.

*The Three Crucibles* frames the transition in three public stages: the Correction in 2026, when automation’s productive advantage breaks the protection around human work; the Transformation from 2027 to 2032, when the problem shifts from making enough to distributing purchasing power; and the Alignment from 2035 onward, when society chooses between broad stewardship and concentrated control. Its blunt economic line is “Robots do not buy iPhones.” If labor income approaches zero, production can work brilliantly while demand collapses.

I think this is the genuine overlap. Both works move from labor displacement to a weaker wage-based tax base, then to a direct public claim on machine-created value. They do not need the same timetable for rapid AI progress to share that causal chain.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The timelines differ, but the works share an economic sequence: automation expands output, weakens labor income and wage-based revenue, and makes a new route from machine production to household income necessary.</p></div>

## The real split is verification versus ownership

Plan A would cap new robot production and AI computing capacity, the processing power supplied by chips and datacenters. Its cap-and-trade system sets a total allowance, auctions permits, and lets companies trade them. In the United States part of the scenario, a legally specified share of permit fees belongs to a Compute Dividend Corporation. Each US citizen owns one share, profits are divided equally, and the compute-fee share rises from 25 percent in 2032 to 75 percent in 2035 ([AI 2040, pp. 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

That distribution system depends on visibility. Plan A uses chip tracking, datacenter declarations, monitoring of large training runs, research transparency, and safety cases. A safety case is an evidence-backed argument that a new AI system will not cause an irreversible catastrophe, and Plan A requires it to withstand criticism from scientists, public reviewers, government auditors, and rival companies.

The document also attacks its own assumption through a fictional Covert Branch. In that imagined path, China hides an AI datacenter in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station project, using legitimate construction as cover and underground heat dissipation to make infrared detection harder ([AI 2040, Appendix D, pp. 45–47](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). The event did not happen. Its purpose is to show that a verification regime governs what enters the record and remains vulnerable to capacity kept outside it.

*The Three Crucibles* places the economic claim closer to labor and ownership. Its proposed AI Labor Equivalence Tax charges for each human-equivalent unit of AI work in proportion to the taxes and payroll contributions a person in that role would have generated. The book pairs that tax with universal basic income, a recurring public payment made regardless of employment, and distributed AI ownership, including open infrastructure, open models, and legal rules meant to prevent the shared AI systems beneath the economy from becoming a private monopoly.

My take is that each work is strongest where the other is thin. Plan A gives much more attention to inspection, pause rules, and catastrophic risk during the transition; at its endpoint, it trusts aligned AIs, systems whose goals the scenario treats as reliably matched to public values, enough to give up universal human shutdown power. Its Citizen’s Dividend mainly distributes a scheduled share of permit revenue rather than ownership in the productive systems themselves. *The Three Crucibles* reaches further into ownership and demand, but it does not provide Plan A’s detailed system for detecting and stopping hidden technical capacity. I think either protection can fail while the other still looks healthy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A proposes regulating scarce technical capacity and distributing part of its permit revenue. The Three Crucibles proposes a broader claim on AI labor and productive ownership. Verification and ownership address different failure modes.</p></div>

## A later echo is not validation

The chronology supports a narrow claim. A cubagarcia.com repository commit on March 23, 2026 added the book PDF and a homepage download link; the dedicated book-page source was added on June 1. Plan A’s official changelog records revisions in July 2026. These records establish file chronology, not public deployment time, influence, novelty, or correctness.

So I will not say that Plan A’s “data” confirmed my framework. There is no observed data here. The defensible claim is that the current documents contain the same unusual economic architecture. I think Plan A becomes more valuable as a challenge because it exposes technical controls that my framework treats less fully.

A nearby critique sharpens the split. George Hotz, the founder of autonomous-driving company comma.ai and machine-learning project tinygrad, argued in his July 2026 essay [“AI 2040 and the Cult of Intelligence”](https://geohot.github.io/blog/jekyll/update/2026/07/11/ai-2040.html) that Plan A centralizes too much authority. His alternative, “Plan L, for local,” calls for AI aligned with and working for its user. That is a serious answer to centralized control, but it does not explain how society should detect covert capacity or replace lost public revenue. It is also an argument, not empirical evidence.

The synthesis should therefore be tested, not branded as a victory. Ask separately whether dangerous computing capacity can be found and stopped, and whether citizens hold an enforceable claim on the value it creates. Each answer needs a measurable object, a fixed evidence period, an authority able to act, and a change that authority must reject. If one enforceable mechanism always guarantees both outcomes, the split is redundant. If observed cases never separate them, it may be only a neat idea.

Neither Plan A nor *The Three Crucibles* supplies a Nordic case in which one protection passes and the other fails. I don’t know which mechanism would survive sustained technical and political pressure. That missing case is the research agenda, because a society can account for every declared chip while ownership still concentrates, or pay a dividend while hidden capacity escapes its stop system.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The honest claim is structural convergence, not confirmation or priority. The combined frame earns its value only if technical control and public economic rights can be measured separately and shown to fail independently.</p></div>

<p class="scifi__mapping">In Bong Joon Ho’s 2013 science-fiction film, the survivors of a climate disaster live on a train whose engine keeps everyone alive while its carriages enforce a brutal class order. The mapping is direct: Plan A asks how the engine can be inspected and restrained before authority is handed over, while The Three Crucibles asks who controls the carriages and receives what the system produces. A future is governable only when the power to stop the machine and the right to share its output are both real.</p>
