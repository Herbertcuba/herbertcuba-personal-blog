Who should own wealth once machines need less human work? Alaska faced an earlier version of that question in 1976. Voters changed the state constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenue would enter the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a publicly owned investment fund. An annual payment to eligible residents began in 1982, with later dividends supported by the fund's investment earnings.

The principle matters more than the oil: residents kept a claim on wealth drawn from a shared resource. Fifty years later, [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) uses Alaska as a model for sharing wealth from AI infrastructure. So does my speculative book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), under the same phrase: a Citizen's Dividend. Its framework was in writing more than six months before this article.

The overlap is striking, but it is not proof. AI 2040 is a scenario, not economic data. It sharpens a question: what if machine output separates from human wages before our institutions are ready?

## What AI 2040 actually proposes

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 90-page scenario and policy proposal published on July 9, 2026, by the AI Futures Project, a US nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and studies policy. Four of the five authors of [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its 2025 rapid-AI scenario, are among Plan A's six authors. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.”

Its preferred path begins with a US-China agreement in 2029. Chip tracking, data-center inspections, and shared research keep AI systems from automating most of the work needed to improve their successors in 2030. Development pauses in 2035 around “top-expert AI,” systems that outperform leading human specialists. Controls are relaxed in 2040 for a move toward superintelligence, capability far beyond humans across most intellectual work.

The economic break comes sooner. Plan A imagines AI doing a third of cognitive labor, including analysis, writing, and planning, by mid-2031. Governments then limit robot deployment and compute, the chip and data-center capacity used to run AI, and sell permits under those limits. The resulting dividend rises from a projected $45,000 per person in 2032 to around $1 million in 2035. These are conditional figures, not promises.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses international controls to slow the AI race and permit revenue to fund a citizen dividend.</p></div>

## The three crucibles, in plain language

My book is a preparation document built around three stages. Each has a distinct role:

- **The Correction** is the economic reckoning. AI weakens the assumption that human labor will remain the main source of value, income, and tax revenue.
- **The Void** is the unstable interval after that bargain weakens but before a trusted replacement exists. A Citizen's Dividend can preserve purchasing power, but money alone does not restore purpose or legitimacy.
- **The Alignment** is the ownership choice. Society decides whether productive intelligence is broadly held and governed, or whether abundance concentrates power among a few owners.

The sequence matters more than the dates. Ordinary firms can pull society into The Correction long before anyone settles the technical argument about superintelligence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction breaks the labor bargain, The Void exposes the missing replacement, and The Alignment decides who holds power.</p></div>

## Where the two maps meet and separate

The closest match is economic. Plan A says labor taxes shrink as machines do more work; The Correction says productivity becomes less dependent on labor. Both then reach for a Citizen's Dividend and the Alaska precedent. Neither document invented resource dividends. The meaningful overlap is the sequence: machine output rises, wage-based distribution weakens, and citizens need a direct claim on the result.

Their funding mechanisms differ. Plan A caps robots and compute, then uses permit revenue for its dividend. My map leans toward taxes on AI labor and distributed ownership of AI infrastructure. One collects value at the point of permission; the other seeks a lasting public claim on the productive asset.

Both end at the same political fork. Plan A warns about an irreversible concentration of power. The Alignment asks whether we become stewards of abundance or new feudal lords. That shared structure suggests ownership may become urgent before superintelligence arrives, without validating either timeline.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works share an economic sequence and a concern about concentrated power, but propose different ways to carry the public claim.</p></div>

## The safeguard depends on what fails

Plan A places considerable weight on verification through tracked chips, inspected facilities, and transparent research. My book places more weight on ownership: who retains a claim on output when rules fail? Plan A's covert-project appendix estimates a below 10% chance that an undetected project reaches top-expert AI before 2043. The probability is low, but its consequence keeps the failure path relevant.

The safeguards cover different weaknesses. Verification may restrain a race while leaving compliant infrastructure concentrated. Broad ownership may distribute power but cannot reveal a hidden data center. Preparation has to hold both questions at once.

The economic thesis can also fail visibly. From a 2026 baseline, compare output per worker, real median labor income, and labor-tax receipts as a share of total economic output each year through 2030. A sustained split, with output rising while income and tax share fall, supports the sequence. If all three rise together, the transition is milder than these maps imply.

No one knows what waits ahead. The international agreement may never happen, physical deployment may slow automation, and new work may absorb more people than either document expects. But if AI 2040 and I are directionally right, the road will be bumpy before any promised abundance arrives. That is why The Three Crucibles exists: not as a calendar, but as a document for preparing while the outcome remains open.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification and ownership protect against different failures. Nobody knows the outcome, but the possibility of a rough transition is reason enough to prepare.</p></div>
