What happens when a society becomes much richer, but paid work stops being the main way people receive money? That question sits beneath both [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/), a 2026 policy scenario about governing advanced AI, and [my book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), a 2026 framework for the economic stages of the same transition. It is also older than AI.

Alaska faced a smaller version of it after oil revenue surged from Prudhoe Bay, a large oil field on the state's North Slope. Voters created the Alaska Permanent Fund in 1976: a state-owned investment fund that turns part of Alaska's oil wealth into a lasting public asset. The first dividend was paid to eligible residents in 1982. The fund does not make every Alaskan rich, but it established a powerful idea: citizens can hold a direct financial claim on wealth drawn from a shared resource.

Both works return to that example. If AI systems produce more while wages carry less of the economy, society needs another way to connect productivity, household income, and political legitimacy.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Alaska's oil fund shows the underlying question: when a shared resource creates exceptional wealth, citizens can receive a direct claim on part of it.</p>

## What AI 2040 proposes

[AI 2040](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 90-page scenario and policy proposal released in July 2026 by the AI Futures Project, the research nonprofit behind the April 2025 scenario *AI 2027*. Four of that earlier work's five listed authors also contributed to Plan A. The report is explicit about its status: “Plan A is primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.”

Its scenario imagines a US-China agreement in 2029 and a pause in 2030 before AI research becomes fully automated. AI then scales through the range of human ability, pauses again around 2035 at the level of leading experts across almost every field, and moves in 2040 toward superintelligence. The report defines superintelligence as AI that is significantly better than the best humans at everything, while also being faster and cheaper.

The economic mechanism matters more than the dramatic dates. In the scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, weakening a tax system built mainly on human work. Plan A caps how much compute and robot capacity can be built, then sells tradable permits to add that capacity. Compute means the chips and data centers used to train and run AI. A fixed share of compute-permit fees goes to a US Compute Dividend Corporation, where every citizen owns one share and receives dividends. That is why the report invokes Alaska.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A tries to control AI's pace through verified capacity limits, then gives citizens a share of the resulting permit-funded wealth.</p>
</div>

## The three crucibles, plainly

The framework in *The Three Crucibles* begins with the economic transition rather than an international agreement. Each crucible names a different pressure:

- **The Correction:** AI makes cognitive labor cheaper, forcing companies, wages, and labor-based taxation to adjust.
- **The Void:** production can keep rising while the institutions that once distributed income through jobs no longer work well enough. A Citizen's Dividend becomes a bridge through that unstable period.
- **The Alignment:** society decides whether AI abundance is broadly owned and governed or concentrated in the hands of a few.

A repository commit dated March 23, 2026 added the book PDF and a homepage download link; AI 2040 appeared in July. That sequence does not establish influence or prove predictive accuracy. It simply makes the specific overlap worth examining.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Correction breaks the old labor equation, the Void exposes the missing distribution system, and the Alignment decides who holds power in what follows.</p>
</div>

## Where the maps meet, and where they do not

The first point of agreement is causal. When machine labor replaces more human labor, productivity may rise while wages and labor-tax revenue weaken. More output cannot repair that gap by itself because households still need purchasing power.

The second overlap is unusually concrete. Both works use the term “Citizen's Dividend,” and both point to the Alaska Permanent Fund. My book connects that dividend to AI-infrastructure productivity, AI labor taxation, and distributed AI ownership. Plan A instead caps scarce capacity, sells permits, and places one share of its US dividend corporation with each citizen. The funding differs, but each gives people a claim that does not depend entirely on employment.

The deeper disagreement concerns the safeguard. Plan A relies heavily on verification, meaning credible evidence that countries and companies obey agreed limits through measures such as chip tracking and data-center inspection. Its own appendix stress-tests that reliance with a covert project, a secret state program designed to hide computing capacity from inspectors. The report gives such an undetected project a below-10% chance of reaching AI that exceeds leading human experts across almost every field before 2043.

My book places more weight on ownership and distribution. Verification without broad ownership could contain some technical risk while leaving the gains concentrated. Broad ownership without verification could spread financial claims on systems that governments cannot reliably restrain. Each map therefore contains part of the preparation that the other treats less fully.

This is convergence, not confirmation. AI 2040 is another scenario, not external data proving my dates. What the overlap does show is that a separately authored policy exercise encountered the same structural questions.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Both maps connect weaker wage distribution to a citizen claim on AI wealth; they split over whether verified control or broad ownership carries the heavier burden.</p>
</div>

The mapping is about access, not spacecraft. Technical abundance does not become shared prosperity on its own; institutions must decide who has an enforceable claim on what the technology produces.

## Preparation without prophecy

I do not know what waits for us, and Plan A's authors do not claim to know either. Its timetable may be wrong, a treaty may never arrive, and physical limits may slow the transition. Yet a slower transition would not remove the basic problem if machine productivity grows faster than wages and public institutions can adapt.

The purpose of my book is preparation, not prophecy. The useful questions are therefore practical even when the dates are uncertain: what capacity can be verified, who owns the productive systems, and who receives a claim on their output? If AI 2040 and the three crucibles are even roughly right, the bumpy road is this gap between rapidly growing production and the much slower work of rebuilding income, ownership, and trust.
