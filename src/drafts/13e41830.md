In 1976, after Prudhoe Bay oil created a temporary revenue stream, Alaska amended its constitution to save part of it in the Alaska Permanent Fund. The first dividend in 1982 gave eligible residents a cash claim on returns from a shared resource.

That design reappears in [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), a 2026 policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, the group behind the 2025 rapid-AI scenario *AI 2027*. Its authors call Plan A a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast. Permit fees for building robots and AI computing capacity fund a Citizen's Dividend explicitly modeled on Alaska.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my book about three stages of AI-driven economic change, reaches the same term and precedent. Its PDF entered my site's repository on March 23, 2026; this edition is dated June 24; and a public post linked to Plan A on July 9. They establish order, not origin or influence.

I think the useful claim is narrower than validation. The maps share part of the economic chain, then answer different questions about control. Verification governs hidden capacity, ownership allocates gains, and exit proof, a funded handover before renewal, tests an institution's bargaining power.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Two 2026 works connect AI infrastructure to a Citizen's Dividend. Their public order permits comparison; their different ideas of control carry the real value.</p></div>

## The overlap begins inside the Void

The [June 24 book edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment as its three crucibles. Transformation is a chapter within the Void. The Correction describes an AI market crash and energy shock stripping labor-heavy companies of their financial cushion. Plan A has no match for that opening mechanism.

The Void begins when AI creates value but many jobs do not return. Its Transformation chapter explains the demand problem: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Falling labor income weakens spending, so the book proposes a [Citizen's Dividend funded by AI infrastructure](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36).

In Plan A's scenario, AI does one-third of cognitive labor, work based on analysis, communication, research, or judgment, by mid-2031. Shrinking employment weakens labor-tax revenue. The government sells permits to build robots and computing capacity, directing most fees to a dividend projected at [$45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18). The book instead taxes machine work measured against comparable human work.

The Alignment, the book's final stage, asks whether control of AI infrastructure and models becomes broadly distributed or concentrated. Plan A gives US citizens a revenue claim but focuses on treaties and safety, not machinery ownership. I think the thematic convergence is meaningful, even with different beginnings and policies.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction has no Plan A equivalent. The Void carries the dividend overlap; the Alignment asks who controls the machines.</p></div>

## Plan A's pauses depend on physical proof

Plan A's treaty system is designed to slow a takeoff, the loop in which AI automates research and speeds its own improvement. In the scenario, a 2029 US-China agreement averts superintelligence by the end of 2030, meaning systems exceeding humans across almost all intellectual work. Capability grows within the human range, pauses at the level of leading human experts in 2035, then resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. Each pause depends on both governments seeing what the other is building.

Plan A requires declarations of major chip and data-center deals, reciprocal inspections, and almost entirely public AI research. Its covert-project appendix tests those controls with computing power equal to 500,000 high-end AI chips in tunnels beside Tibet's proposed Medog Hydropower Station, where the dam would supply power and the tunnels would hide the equipment. Like *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film whose virtual world depends on physical machines, advanced AI leaves a trail of chips, power, cooling, construction, and people.

I think that trail makes inspection plausible. The Alignment calls for distributed ownership because seeing a machine cannot determine who may use it or claim its output. I don't know whether Plan A's system could survive sustained US-China rivalry, and its authors do not claim certainty.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A's pauses rely on physical evidence and inspection. The book asks who may use a governed system and receive its output.</p></div>

## Put bargaining power into the renewal

At company level, a buyer may own its documents yet depend on one AI supplier for data, permissions, settings, connected tools, and operating knowledge. If leaving becomes impractical, the supplier can change price or terms without facing a credible refusal.

The book imagines one insurer keeping 5,000 people processing claims while an AI-powered rival cuts staff by 40 percent, premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months ([printed p. 20](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21)). That rush postpones the exit question.

My take is that renewal should depend on a supplier-funded, 30-day handover of one workflow and one data extract to one replacement team. The chief information officer judges the result. The team receives data history, settings and model instructions, permissions, tool connections, and recovery guidance. It may change the model or workflow, but must reproduce the outcome on a buyer-owned evaluation set, a sample of accepted past cases, within error, time, and cost limits.

Missing material or a result outside those limits fails the test and blocks automatic renewal. The supplier funds correction and transition; after a second failure, the buyer can leave without a termination fee. Since August 2, 2026, [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's main AI law, has required disclosure in certain AI interactions and software-readable labels for some generated content. These reveal AI use; the handover tests whether the buyer can act.

I think the Alaska analogy works because both rights must be fixed early. Alaska acted before oil revenue entered ordinary politics; a buyer must act before departure becomes too costly. Machines will be built; buyers must remain free to leave.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A funded handover makes renewal depend on demonstrated transfer. If another team cannot run the workflow despite clean disclosures, changing suppliers is already impractical, which is why the right to leave must precede dependence.</p></div>
