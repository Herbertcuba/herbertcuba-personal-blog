In 1976, Alaska voters decided that an oil boom should leave something behind after the wells declined. They amended the state constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenue would enter the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a state-owned investment fund. The first resident dividend was paid in 1982 from surplus oil revenue; today's annual payments draw on investment earnings connected to the state's mineral wealth.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), a 2026 scenario and policy proposal from the AI Futures Project, a US nonprofit studying advanced AI, applies the same idea to the chips and data centers that run AI. My 2026 speculative book, [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), also proposes a Citizen's Dividend, a direct payment that gives people a share of AI-driven production.

The framework behind *The Three Crucibles* was in writing by February 3, 2026, more than six months before this article, and public on my site by March 23. Plan A appeared on July 9. That sequence proves neither map, but the overlap points to a question that may arrive before superintelligence: what happens when productive capacity outgrows wages and the institutions financed by them?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska turned a temporary resource boom into a durable public claim. AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles ask whether AI production will require a similar settlement.</p></div>

## What AI 2040 actually proposes

Plan A is a 90-page future history. Four of its six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the AI Futures Project's 2025 scenario about a rapid AI race. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.”

Its takeoff path, the projected rise from current AI to much greater capability, begins with a US-China agreement in 2029. The scenario reaches nearly automated AI research in 2030, pauses around systems that outperform top human experts in 2035, then allows a controlled move to superintelligence in 2040. Superintelligence means AI that exceeds human ability across almost all important cognitive work.

Control rests on compute, the processing capacity supplied by advanced chips and data centers. Plan A proposes chip tracking, facility inspections, research transparency, and safety cases, meaning evidence-backed arguments that a system can operate within stated limits. In its economy, AI performs a third of cognitive labor, work based mainly on reasoning and communication, by mid-2031. Wage-linked taxes then shrink, so permits on chips and robots fund a public corporation that pays equal dividends.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A imagines a managed takeoff in which verifiable limits contain technical risk and permit revenue replaces part of the tax base lost to automated work.</p></div>

## The same passage appears in three crucibles

The numbered framework map in the downloadable edition of my book uses three Crucibles.

**The Correction** begins when AI performs enough work that productivity can rise while wages, employment, and labor-tax revenue weaken.

**The Void** is the unstable interval after the old labor bargain starts breaking and before a replacement has broad legitimacy.

**The Alignment** is the political choice that follows: whether AI infrastructure supports shared abundance or concentrates wealth and governing power among a few owners.

The sources differ on one label. The public book page calls the middle phase *The Transformation*. The downloadable edition names the second Crucible *The Void* and uses *The Transformation* as a chapter title, so I use *The Void* here.

Plan A traces much the same passage. Its weakening labor-tax base resembles the Correction. Its Citizen's Dividend, including the same Alaska precedent, bridges the Void. Its effort to spread gains while preventing concentrated control belongs to the Alignment. Public dividends predate both works, so the shared term is not the point. The stronger signal is the order: production separates from labor income, distribution must be rebuilt, and ownership becomes a question of legitimacy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both maps move from a weakened labor bargain, through a citizen dividend, toward a struggle over whether AI abundance is broadly shared or tightly held.</p></div>

## Their disagreement is about the basis of control

Plan A puts its confidence in verification. Governments count advanced chips, inspect data centers, require safety cases, and share enough research to slow dangerous development without stopping useful AI. Yet an appendix imagines a covert Chinese project built with smuggled chips. Its presence matters because verification is only as strong as its view of what actors can hide.

The Three Crucibles puts more weight on distributed ownership, meaning that more people hold an economic stake in AI infrastructure, alongside taxes linked to AI work and a dividend tied to its output. This does not solve technical control. It asks what remains after a system is declared safe: who holds the legal and economic claim on what it produces?

The two layers need each other. Verification without shared ownership could leave the gains concentrated. Shared ownership without effective safety could distribute claims on a system nobody can reliably govern.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A focuses on making AI capability visible and governable; The Three Crucibles focuses on who owns its output. Neither answer is sufficient alone.</p></div>

## Preparation is the point

No one knows whether AI will follow Plan A's calendar or whether the Crucibles will unfold in order. By 2030, a useful test will be whether AI-led productivity separates from wage income and labor-tax receipts. If those keep pace, or AI ownership broadens without intervention, the shared thesis weakens.

If both maps are directionally right, however, productive capacity will move faster than the institutions that distribute its benefits and restrain its risks. *The Three Crucibles* is my preparation document for that rough passage. Uncertainty about the route is not a good reason to ignore the terrain coming into view.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Neither map offers certainty. Both warn that AI capability may outrun wages, public revenue, ownership rules, and safety institutions, making preparation more useful than confidence in any exact date.</p></div>
