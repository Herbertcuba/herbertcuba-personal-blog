[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) tries to map the road to superintelligence.

The scenario begins with an AI race, passes through a US-China deal, and reaches controlled superintelligence in 2040. AI takes a large share of work, wage-based taxation buckles, and citizens receive a dividend funded by permits on compute and robots.

I wrote [The Three Crucibles](/books/the-three-crucibles/) more than six months ago. It was public on my site by March 23, before AI 2040 appeared in July, and follows a strikingly similar economic sequence.

I think the overlap deserves attention because the two works began in different rooms. The AI Futures Project models global policy. My book prepares people and organizations for the shock. Independent convergence adds weight without proving either.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles independently point to the same economic break: machine output rises while labor income weakens, so distribution has to be rebuilt. They diverge on the guardrail, with Plan A relying on verification and my book placing more weight on ownership.</p></div>

## What Plan A asks us to imagine

Plan A uses a detailed timeline to pressure-test a safer route through superintelligence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The scenario buys time through a 2029 US-China deal, pauses near top-expert AI in 2035, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. Its deepest bet is that transparency and verified compute can slow the race long enough for human institutions to catch up.</p></div>

Four of the six authors also wrote [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/): Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Eli Lifland, and Daniel Kokotajlo. They describe Plan A as a recommendation and a way to stress-test policy.

In their scenario, a 2029 US-China deal prevents fully automated AI research in 2030. Development pauses around top human experts in 2035 and resumes in 2040.

The plan has four principles: Buy Time, Total Research Transparency, Diffuse AI Broadly, and Reversibility. AI research becomes almost entirely transparent while inference remains private. Major compute transactions are declared, with inspections around large datacenters and suppliers.

The economic sequence is sharper. AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, while real GDP growth reaches about 50 percent in 2032. The United States caps robot and compute production, auctions permits, and collects a projected $50 trillion that fiscal year.

Most permit fees eventually fund a Citizen's Dividend. Payments to American adults rise from $45,000 per person in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035. These scenario numbers force the policy to face its own consequences.

I think this is the report's strongest move. It forces a safety proposal to survive contact with employment, tax revenue, and household demand.

Company B, the book's fictional insurer, fits inside that world. It deploys an agentic swarm, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and beats a rival that keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. AI 2040 scales that squeeze to a national economy.

## The same economic fracture appears twice

Company B moves through the three Crucibles on a smaller stage.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction breaks the old link between labor and output. The Void opens when production keeps rising while household income falls. The Alignment decides whether machine abundance is widely owned or concentrated.</p></div>

The Correction arrives when Company B produces the same service with fewer people. Productivity moves toward capital, and labor loses its old place in the equation.

The Void opens when the technology works faster than the social contract. The book compresses the demand problem into one sentence: "Robots do not buy iPhones." Machines can make goods cheaply while shrinking the wages that let households buy them.

The Alignment turns toward power. Who owns the machines, and who remains dependent on a small group of infrastructure owners?

Here the maps almost touch line for line. The Three Crucibles proposes a "Citizen's Dividend" funded through taxes on AI labor and computational output. AI 2040 uses the same name for a dividend funded through robot and compute permits. Both cite Alaska's oil dividend.

The mechanics differ. My book taxes human-equivalent AI labor and automated output. Plan A caps production capacity and auctions permission to build more. I think both follow the same intuition: shrinking payroll creates a need for a public claim on machine production.

The timing differs too. The book treats the Correction as an immediate business pressure. Plan A gives the world a negotiated pause through the 2030s. One prepares for institutional lag; the other designs international coordination.

## The maps split at control

Company B shows why economic relief can leave the power structure untouched.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A tries to contain dangerous capability through transparency, inspection, and reversible limits. The Three Crucibles concentrates on who owns the intelligence layer and who can keep operating when a central provider or political process fails.</p></div>

Plan A makes verification do enormous work. Its appendix imagines a Chinese covert project with about 500,000 H100-equivalent chips in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station. Around 200 people know. Within the scenario, the US intelligence community estimates that the chance of this hidden project reaching Top-Expert-Dominating AI stays below 10 percent until 2043.

The appendix attacks the plan from inside. A covert project exists to escape declaration, so the authors answer with chip accounting, inspections, intelligence work, and compute limits.

The Three Crucibles puts more pressure on distributed ownership. Company B may receive perfect compliance reports while building its core workflow around infrastructure it cannot replace. A dividend can soften the shock while control remains concentrated.

Plan A sometimes reads like *2001: A Space Odyssey* rewritten as a treaty. Every instrument is watched, HAL is audited, and humans try to keep control of the mission. My book asks who owns the ship and who receives what it produces. A perfect dashboard can still belong to someone else.

I think Plan A is stronger on civilization-scale loss of control. The Three Crucibles spends more time on distribution after labor loses bargaining power. Together they show two failures: the machine escapes human control, or its benefits collect behind a narrow gate.

I don't know which danger arrives first. Plan A does not know either. Scenario scrutiny exists because certainty is unavailable.

## Preparation reaches the codebase

Company B's claims agent brings the global fork into work a junior engineer can inspect.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Engineers cannot negotiate a US-China compute treaty, but they can keep one workflow legible and movable. A claims agent needs explicit state, a real route to human review, and a tested fallback before a provider becomes difficult to replace.</p></div>

Suppose Company B builds its claims agent with ReAct, short for reasoning and acting. The model reads a claim, calls a policy tool, and chooses the next step from the result. Tool use becomes visible inside the workflow.

LangGraph is a Python library for workflows that branch or repeat. A `StateGraph` connects steps as nodes and routes between them with edges. A `TypedDict` tells static type checkers which keys should exist in shared state, such as `claim_id` and `requires_human`. Python leaves runtime enforcement to application code.

Setting `requires_human = True` only flags a case. The workflow reaches a person after an engineer adds a real conditional route to a review node. LangGraph supplies the skeleton while Company B builds the runtime checks and evidence trail.

The same principle applies to model choice. Company B can replay reviewed claims against its current model and a named fallback, using the same policy and human-review boundary. A failed replay reveals dependency before a contract hardens it.

This is where [ownership of the agent layer](/posts/who-owns-the-agent-layer/) becomes concrete. It also connects to [the trust problem behind disposable software](/posts/when-software-becomes-disposable/). I think junior engineers will shape this future through ordinary architecture decisions.

Plan A says the world needs time. The Three Crucibles asks us to use that time to build distribution and ownership before the shock. Company B can start with a workflow that survives a model change.

The timeline may slip, and the economic numbers may miss by a mile. If the AI Futures Project and I are directionally right, the road ahead will still be bumpy.

I wrote The Three Crucibles as a preparation document for the period when machine output moves faster than institutions can adapt. We cannot know what waits in 2040. We can decide how exposed we are when the shaking starts.
