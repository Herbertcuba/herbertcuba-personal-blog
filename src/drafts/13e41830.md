Two maps can reach the same fork and still be wrong because they may share a mechanism or merely share assumptions. What matters is what each had to believe before arriving there.

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 2026 policy recommendation expressed as a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and proposes policy. The same group produced [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its April 2025 scenario about AI accelerating research on better AI. [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my book about the economic and political stages of the AI transition.

A [repository commit](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) dated March 23, 2026 added the book PDF and its download link. The current book PDF's metadata is dated June 24, 2026, while the Plan A PDF is dated July 11, 2026. Those records establish artifact order, not public deployment, influence, or empirical validation. Plan A also calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction” and says the scenario is not the authors' best guess.

I think the useful signal is narrower. International-security analysis and an economic-ownership argument reach the same structural problem, then expose why one safeguard will not solve it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The artifact chronology supports comparison, not validation. Different starting points lead to the same economic break and expose different missing safeguards.</p></div>

## The agreement is narrower than it looks

In Plan A's scenario, the United States and China make a deal in 2029 that averts fully automated AI research in 2030, meaning AI would otherwise do most of the work needed to design better AI. Development stays within the human range until a pause at top-human-expert level in 2035, then resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. The document defines superintelligence as systems significantly better than the best humans at everything while also faster and cheaper ([AI 2040, pp. 4–5](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

*The Three Crucibles* uses a different clock and two labels for its middle stage. Its public page names The Correction in 2026, The Transformation from 2027 to 2032, and The Alignment from 2035 onward. The PDF opening map instead calls the second Crucible “The Void,” then develops The Transformation within it. The argument moves from labor losing economic weight, through a break in wage-backed demand, to a choice between shared abundance and concentrated control.

The clocks do not line up, and neither work has earned the right to treat its dates as a timetable. I think that mismatch helps separate the economic claim from one theory of rapid AI progress. Both still move from weaker labor income to a rebuilt distribution system and then a political fight over control. Yet citizen payments and public ownership funds predate both works, so matching language establishes structural overlap rather than independent discovery.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works converge on labor disruption, redistribution, and concentrated power while disagreeing on timing. The overlap sharpens a structural question without proving either forecast.</p></div>

## The same dividend uses different collection gates

In Plan A's United States scenario, inflation-adjusted output grows by about 50 percent in 2032 while personal-income and payroll-tax revenue falls as AI systems and robots replace paid human work. Corporate tax does not automatically fill the gap because firms can reinvest in datacenters, factories, and robots, then expense or depreciate those investments before tax ([AI 2040, pp. 16–17](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). Output can rise while the public claim on it weakens.

Both works call their response a “Citizen's Dividend,” a direct payment backed by revenue or ownership from productive assets. Both cite the Alaska Permanent Fund, created through a 1976 constitutional amendment to preserve oil wealth and first paying a dividend in 1982. Alaska attached a public claim to a scarce asset before distributing later income.

Plan A caps robot and AI computing capacity, then auctions tradable permits to build more. Computing capacity is the processing power, supplied by chips and datacenters, used to train and run AI. In the scenario, permit fees reach $50 trillion in real 2025 dollars in fiscal year 2032 and $180 trillion in 2034; the modeled payment to American adults rises from $45,000 in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035 ([AI 2040, pp. 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). These are uncertain scenario outputs, not economic evidence.

*The Three Crucibles* reaches the dividend through an AI Labor Equivalence Tax. A company would pay for each human-equivalent unit of AI labor it deploys, in proportion to the public revenue a person doing that work would have generated. The tax follows deployed work, while Plan A's permits charge for scarce capacity before the work occurs.

I think this divergence matters more than the shared label. Permits can count large, declared facilities but miss hidden or offshore capacity. A labor-equivalence tax can follow deployment but invites arguments over equivalent human work and whether a task was replaced. The diagnosis converges while the collection system remains open.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A charges at the capacity gate; The Three Crucibles taxes human-equivalent AI labor after deployment. Each route has a different measurement problem and path for avoidance.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership fail separately

Plan A starts with major datacenter owners and upstream suppliers, including chip manufacturers, publicly declaring major purchases and sales so inspectors can account for holdings. It adds facility inspections, monitoring for new model-training runs, broad sharing of advanced-AI research, and safety cases. A safety case is an evidence-backed argument that a new AI will not cause an irreversible catastrophe, tested by outside criticism ([AI 2040, pp. 7–8 and 13](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

The document tests that premise in a fictional alternate branch. China diverts 500,000 H100-equivalents into a covert datacenter in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station project. An H100-equivalent normalizes capacity against the 16-bit computing performance of the H100 AI accelerator from chip designer Nvidia; it does not count identical chips. Construction provides cover, while underground heat dissipation makes infrared detection harder ([AI 2040, Appendix D, pp. 45–47](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). Verification therefore allows some unseen capacity rather than promising perfect visibility.

*The Three Crucibles* gives more weight to distributed ownership: AI infrastructure and models held beyond a small group of private firms, with legal limits on private monopoly. Plan A is not blind to ownership. Its citizen-owned Compute Dividend Corporation receives a legally defined share of permit fees, and every United States citizen owns one share. But capacity that escapes the permit record can also escape the dividend.

Verification can constrain dangerous, undeclared scaling without deciding who receives the gain. Distributed ownership can spread that gain without detecting a hidden research project. I don't know which mix of permits, taxes, citizen shares, and distributed systems would survive this pressure. A strategy unable to detect and act on both failures is incomplete before the crisis begins.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification protects the boundary around dangerous AI capacity; ownership protects the public claim on its output. Either can work while the other fails.</p></div>

## The two-key test belongs at two levels

Call the comparison the Two-Key Test. The capacity key asks whether dangerous AI computing, research, and deployment can be declared, inspected, authorized, and stopped. The claim key asks whether citizens have an enforceable share of machine-created value. I think it should be a design audit published before outcomes are known, not a prediction graded afterward.

At national level, the capacity record belongs to a regulator with legal inspection and stop powers. It should name the asset, owner and operator, authorized capacity, latest inspection, accepted amount of undetected capacity, and breach response. Plan A imagines each side becoming confident that the other hides no more than about 1 percent of AI computing capacity. That is a scenario tolerance, not a real-world standard. A restricted run outside the record, or a regulator unable to halt it, fails the capacity key.

The public claim record belongs to lawmakers, the treasury, or a legally constituted fund. It should name the revenue base, payer, required share, beneficiaries, payment date, auditor, and appeal process. The distribution ratio is audited money paid to named beneficiaries divided by permit or AI-labor-tax revenue assessed in the same period. A missed payment or a transfer below the required share fails the claim key. A company chief financial officer cannot create that right.

A company board has a narrower mandate. Before an automation deployment, it can fix a baseline period, unit of output, minimum improvement, maximum error rate, cost limit, system version, affected roles, and evidence owner. It can set a workforce-consultation trigger: a stated number or share of affected roles that requires consultation with named worker representatives. Legal and procurement teams can secure a right to pause or terminate, while the business owner defines the evidence required for restart. These controls do not substitute for a Citizen's Dividend; they stop the company from claiming powers it does not hold.

My take is that the convergence becomes useful only at this level of precision. Capacity control and economic rights need separate owners, records, thresholds, and stop conditions because one can look healthy while the other is bypassed. Advanced-AI strategy is incomplete until the people who can stop the machine and the people entitled to its gains both hold rights that survive pressure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Two-Key Test separates public authority from company control. Governments need stoppable capacity and enforceable citizen claims; boards need evidence, consultation triggers, and contractual pause rights.</p></div>
