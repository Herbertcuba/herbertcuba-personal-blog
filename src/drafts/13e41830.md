[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), a 2026 policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit, follows a clear economic chain: AI automates work, labor income and taxes fall, and a Citizen's Dividend returns some AI-created wealth to the public. Four of the five authors of *AI 2027*, the group's 2025 rapid-AI scenario, also worked on Plan A.

My book [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) traces a similar route. Its PDF entered my site's repository on [March 23, 2026](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8); this edition is dated June 24; and a public submission linked Plan A on [July 9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). The book comes first in that record. The dates establish neither influence nor separate origins, and validate neither timeline.

I think the comparison matters because the overlap is specific and the disagreement useful. Verification constrains hidden computing capacity, ownership decides who receives the gains, and exit proof tests whether an institution has real bargaining power.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both works connect automation to a citizen dividend, then expose different forms of control.</p></div>

## The closest match is inside the Void

The [June 24 edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment. The Correction is market pressure that makes AI adoption a survival move. The Void begins when machine output rises but jobs, wages, and demand do not. The Transformation is a chapter inside the Void, not its name.

Plan A starts further along. In its scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, such as analysis, research, and judgment, by mid-2031. Falling employment shrinks income and payroll taxes. Permits to build robots or computing capacity, the chips and facilities used to train and run AI, then produce a projected [$50 trillion in US revenue in fiscal 2032 and $180 trillion in 2034](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17).

Both works return some machine-created wealth to people. Plan A's proposed [Compute Dividend Corporation](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=19) legally owns part of the permit fees. Every US citizen owns one corporate share and receives an equal dividend. Its scenario moves from [$45,000 per adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18). Both cite the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=38), a state mineral-revenue fund created in 1976 that has paid resident dividends since 1982.

[The Transformation chapter](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36) puts the demand problem plainly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Its [AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37) charges each human-equivalent unit of AI work according to the public revenue a person in that role would generate.

I think this is a strong thematic match, not empirical validation. The diagnosis and dividend converge; funding and speed do not.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Void is the closest match: both link automated output to weaker labor income and a citizen dividend, funded differently.</p></div>

## Verification does not settle ownership

Plan A's takeoff mechanism is AI automating AI research, so improvement speeds itself up. In the [scenario](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=4), a 2029 US-China deal avoids superintelligence by the end of 2030, meaning systems better, faster, and cheaper than the best humans across fields. Development then stays within the human range until a 2035 pause at top-expert AI, roughly equal to the best human specialists, before scaling to superintelligence in 2040. The deal stops the early race; the pause controls later scaling.

The plan depends on verification: public declarations of major chip and data-center transactions, reciprocal inspections, and almost entirely public AI research. Its [covert-project appendix](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=47) tests the weak point. Roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents, a unit of computing power benchmarked to the H100 AI processor, sit in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station. Dam construction provides cover; small nuclear reactors and gas turbines supply power before completion. About 200 people know.

The physical lesson from *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film about a machine-built simulated world, is useful here. Advanced AI may feel like software, but it still needs chips, power, cooling, buildings, and supply chains. Those traces make inspection possible.

The Alignment asks who owns the machines and distributes the abundance. [The book argues](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57) for AI infrastructure held by individuals, cooperatives, or municipalities, with models running on local hardware. Plan A gives citizens shares in a dividend corporation, not in the chips, models, or AI companies. I think inspection and ownership are complements. I don't know which arrangement survives geopolitical pressure, but confusing them leaves one form of power untouched.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification can expose prohibited computing capacity. Ownership determines who controls lawful systems and receives their output, so inspection alone cannot resolve the Alignment.</p></div>

## Control also needs a tested way out

Inside a company, the book offers a [fictional comparison](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21) between an insurer with 5,000 claims adjusters and a rival using AI agents to cut headcount by 40 percent, lower premiums by 20 percent, and win within six months. Adoption pressure can move faster than a buyer's ability to leave its supplier.

My take: renew a critical AI contract only after exit proof, a supplier-funded, 30-day handover of one workflow and one usable data extract to a replacement team. The chief information officer owns the decision. The supplier provides decision history, saved settings and model instructions, access rights, software connections, records of where source information came from and how it changed, plus operating and recovery guidance.

The replacement may change the model, its instructions, how it finds source information, or the workflow itself. The business test stays fixed: using a buyer-owned sample of past cases, the replacement must reproduce the agreed outcome within limits for errors, time, and cost. Failure should block automatic renewal and require the supplier to fund correction and transition support. A second failure should let the buyer leave without a termination fee.

A supplier can explain its system and still fail the handover; that failure predicts practical lock-in despite formal transparency. I think institutions can test this control now. The machines may be visible and the gains shared, but bargaining power belongs to the party that can still walk away.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof makes supplier control pass or fail at renewal. If a replacement cannot reproduce the agreed business result within fixed limits, the buyer lacks a practical right to leave.</p></div>
