In 1976, Alaska changed its constitution so that part of its mineral wealth would be saved. The Alaska Permanent Fund is the state-owned investment fund created from that decision, following the Prudhoe Bay oil discovery. Since 1982, part of its earnings has funded cash dividends for eligible Alaska residents.

That old decision reappears in two works about advanced AI. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) proposes a much larger fund for AI wealth, while [my book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), describes the same mechanism under the same name: the Citizen's Dividend. This overlap proves neither account correct. It does, however, expose a shared problem: if machines produce more while human labor earns less, someone must still own the output and sustain demand.

## What AI 2040 is trying to do

The AI Futures Project is the research nonprofit behind *AI 2027*, an April 2025 scenario about rapid AI progress and the risks of extinction or concentrated power. Four of that work's five authors contributed to *AI 2040*, released in July 2026. They call Plan A [“primarily a recommendation, not a prediction”](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf).

Its scenario begins with a 2029 agreement between the United States and China. The countries avoid fully automated AI research in 2030, expand systems within the human range, pause at top-expert capability in 2035, and resume in 2040 toward superintelligence. The report defines that as AI better than the best humans across essentially every task, while also faster and cheaper.

The plan relies on verification, meaning shared evidence that countries obey the limits. States would track chips, inspect data centers, and count compute, the chip capacity used to build and run AI. As AI performs a projected third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, wage income and labor taxes shrink. Charges on scarce chips and robots then support a Compute Dividend Corporation, with one share for each US citizen and profits paid as a Citizen's Dividend.

<p>Plan A combines verifiable limits on AI capacity with a public claim on part of the wealth that capacity creates.</p>

## The three crucibles describe the same pressure

The composition of my book's core framework predates AI 2040 by more than six months, while its publicly verifiable trail begins with a March 23, 2026 repository record. This chronology supports comparison, not a claim of influence.

The three crucibles are simple to state. The Correction begins when markets price human cognitive labor against cheaper machine output. The Void is the unstable interval when wages and demand weaken before a replacement is ready. The Alignment is the political choice between shared abundance and control by a small group.

Plan A follows the same pressure from another direction. Its shrinking tax base resembles the Correction. Its Citizen's Dividend answers the Void by giving people a claim on machine productivity, although the funding differs: my book proposes taxation tied to AI labor and distributed AI ownership, while Plan A charges for deploying chips and robots. Both arrive at the Alignment question of whether AI-created wealth spreads or concentrates.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The works converge on labor losing value, distribution being rebuilt, and ownership deciding who benefits.</p>
</div>

## Their deepest disagreement is about trust

Plan A places much of its trust in visibility. If countries can count compute, inspect facilities, and detect hidden projects, they can enforce a pause. My book places more weight on ownership: an enforceable claim on the systems and the value they create.

Plan A tests its own assumption. One appendix estimates a chance below 10 percent that a covert project could reach top-expert-dominating AI, meaning systems exceeding leading human experts across almost every field, before 2043 without detection. The estimate is low, but the weakness is real because verification works only while important activity remains visible.

Ownership cannot solve loss of control, and technical control cannot guarantee fair distribution. A durable settlement has to answer both questions.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Verification limits hidden capability; ownership distributes economic power. Neither protection can replace the other.</p>
</div>

The mapping is narrow: Plan A focuses on preserving human command, while *The Three Crucibles* asks what kind of society remains if command holds. Solving one problem does not settle the other.

## Preparation before certainty

I do not know what waits for us, and Plan A's authors avoid claiming that they do. Capability growth may slow, physical limits may bite, and politics may scramble every date. Similar structure is evidence of a problem, not proof of a timeline.

Still, if AI 2040 and *The Three Crucibles* are directionally right, the road ahead will be bumpy. We will renegotiate income, ownership, state power, and the boundary between human and machine decisions while the technology keeps moving. My book exists as a preparation document for that passage, making those choices visible before they harden into infrastructure and law.
