Who gets paid when intelligence becomes infrastructure? That question sits beneath two documents that begin in different places and arrive at a similar institutional problem.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), released on July 9, 2026 by the AI Futures Project, is a policy recommendation told through a conditional scenario. The forecasting group also produced [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), an April 2025 scenario in which AI systems conduct the research needed to build better AI systems, creating a rapid feedback loop. Four of that work's five authors are among the six authors of *AI 2040*.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my book about AI-driven changes to work, taxation, ownership, and social power, was publicly available on my site by March 23, 2026; the current PDF edition is dated June 24. That order establishes public chronology. It does not establish influence, predictive success, or empirical validation.

I think the useful claim is narrower and more consequential. A later team worked forward from AI safety and geopolitics, while my framework worked forward from the economic break, yet both reached the need for a public claim on machine-created wealth. Their disagreement shows what each approach leaves exposed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p><em>AI 2040</em> cannot validate the dates in <em>The Three Crucibles</em>, but its later scenario reaches the same institutional pressure: once wages stop distributing enough income and tax revenue, citizens need a direct claim on automated production.</p></div>

## The sequence survives different clocks

The book's public page names The Correction (2026), The Transformation (2027–2032), and The Alignment (2035 onward). Its PDF map instead calls the second stage The Void, while a later page places both The Void and The Transformation within the Second Crucible. The two official sources disagree, so I will call it the middle crucible here.

The Correction describes AI expectations meeting uneven returns, followed by a shock that weakens protection around human work. The middle crucible separates recovering production from recovering jobs, breaking the path from employment to demand and public revenue. The Alignment asks who owns the machines and distributes abundance: broad stewards or new feudal lords. The book compresses this wager into “Productivity = Capital,” with labor solving to zero.

Plan A skips the market correction. In its scenario, the United States and China make a deal in 2029. This averts fully automated AI research in 2030, meaning AI systems do not fully take over the process of improving AI, and leads to a 2035 pause near the capability of the best human researchers. In 2040, the scenario resumes toward superintelligence, which the authors define as systems significantly better than the best humans at everything while also faster and cheaper.

The calendars diverge, but the economic chain meets. Plan A projects the share of economically valuable work done by AI systems and robots rising from about 20% in 2032 to about 85% in 2035. Its authors say wage earners would become a small part of the economy and the old labor-tax base would become unsustainable. I think this is the first real convergence: production can keep rising after wages stop carrying purchasing power and public finance.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works use different timelines and disagree on the speed of AI improvement, but both reach the same economic break: machine output grows while wages lose their role as the main bridge to household demand and government revenue.</p></div>

## A public claim needs a funding rule

A Citizen's Dividend is a direct payment to people financed by a public claim on AI-related revenue or productive assets. Both works use that name and point to the Alaska Permanent Fund, a state fund created in 1976 from mineral royalties that began paying resident dividends in 1982. The precedent matters because Alaska attached a public claim to a scarce resource before distributing the proceeds.

Plan A attaches that claim to capacity. Compute is the processing capacity used to train and run AI systems. Governments cap how much compute and robot capacity may be built, sell freely tradable permits to build it, and collect permit fees. In the scenario's American mechanism, the share of those fees assigned to a Compute Dividend Corporation rises from 25% in 2032 to 75% in 2035. Each United States citizen owns one share, and the corporation divides its profits equally among citizens.

*The Three Crucibles* attaches public revenue to work instead. Its proposed AI Labor Equivalence Tax would charge for each human-equivalent unit of AI labor in proportion to what a person in that role would have generated for public revenue. One mechanism prices permission to build capped capacity; the other charges for economically equivalent AI work. I think the distinction decides what must be observed: hardware and construction on one side, changing tasks and job value on the other.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The shared dividend hides a real policy choice. Plan A funds a public share by pricing permission to build capped compute and robot capacity, while <em>The Three Crucibles</em> tries to preserve public revenue when AI systems perform work once done by people.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership cover different failures

Plan A's international bargain depends on public AI research, declared and monitored datacenters, tracked chips, inspections, and checks for prohibited model-training runs, the compute-intensive processes that produce or improve models. It also requires safety cases: evidence-based arguments that a system can operate within stated risk limits and remain under human control. Verification is therefore not paperwork around the plan. It is the mechanism holding the plan together.

An appendix stress-tests that mechanism with a possible covert Chinese project using diverted chips and an underground datacenter. This is a scenario branch, not a claim about an existing facility. Hidden compute could let one participant improve systems outside the agreement, so the pause depends on making secret capacity difficult to assemble and use.

My book concentrates on another exposure. Distributed AI ownership combines open models with AI infrastructure owned by individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities rather than a few providers. That can spread decision rights and economic returns, but it does not prove that powerful systems are safe. Visible and well-controlled infrastructure can still remain narrowly owned.

I don't know whether international inspection could survive intense strategic pressure, or whether distributed ownership could remain governable as capabilities rise. I think the honest answer is to stop treating either safeguard as a substitute for the other. Verification addresses secret races and loss of control; ownership addresses dependency and the concentration of gains.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification asks whether advanced AI capacity is visible, constrained, and under human control. Distributed ownership asks who can direct that capacity and receive its returns. A system can pass one test while failing the other.</p></div>

## The decision arrives before abundance

Together, the works create a stricter test for AI strategy. Can institutions measure and stop dangerous capacity? Which source of public revenue survives if wages shrink? What legal right gives citizens a share of machine-created value? The answers must work together because the bargain hardens after infrastructure, ownership, and state revenue have settled around it.

My take is that *AI 2040* strengthens the case for the sequence, not the calendar. It gives technical and geopolitical detail to a transition that *The Three Crucibles* describes mainly through economics and ownership. The book, in turn, makes the distribution question harder to evade: controlled abundance can still produce permanent dependency.

I think preparation becomes credible when safety limits and economic rights are designed as one system. Abundance becomes public prosperity only when the public claim exists before the machines produce it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The combined framework requires two promises to hold at once: advanced AI remains visible and under human control, and citizens hold an enforceable economic claim on what it produces. Designing either promise later leaves the other kind of power already concentrated.</p></div>
