[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) gives every US citizen one share in a Compute Dividend Corporation. It calls the payout "The Citizen's Dividend." My book [The Three Crucibles](/books/the-three-crucibles/) uses the same term and the same Alaska oil-dividend precedent. Its route begins with an AI agent handling a damaged-car claim.

The phrase predates both documents. The Three Crucibles has been public on my site since March 23. AI 2040 arrived in July, with four of the five [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/) authors returning.

I think the independent agreement tells us more than the timing. An enterprise thought experiment and a national policy scenario hit the same economic problem. Their disagreement is useful too. Plan A tries to make concentrated power visible. The Crucibles asks who keeps a usable claim on the output when visibility fails.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An enterprise thought experiment and a government scenario both reach a Citizen's Dividend from different starting points. Their agreement strengthens the economic diagnosis. The damaged-car claim then exposes what cash distribution cannot settle: who can still operate after a model provider changes the deal.</p></div>

## Company B wins, then demand starts to break

Take one covered €2,400 repair inside the book's insurer thought experiment.

Company A keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. Company B deploys an agentic system, cuts headcount by 40 percent and lowers premiums by 20 percent. In the scenario, Company B wins within six months.

The First Crucible is the Correction. Competition forces other insurers to follow Company B. The Second is the Void, where machine output rises while wage income weakens. "Robots do not buy iPhones." The Third is the Alignment, which asks who owns the machines.

Plan A reaches the same pressure from the federal budget. It says personal income and payroll taxes produced roughly ten times as much US federal revenue as corporate taxes in 2026. In its scenario, machines take more paid work, so the tax base collapses as a share of GDP unless policy changes.

I think this overlap survives a slower AI timeline. Company B only needs an agent that settles enough claims at a lower cost. Once that works, price pressure carries the Correction through the market. The date can change while the demand problem remains.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Company B's lower price forces the market to move before policy is ready. As payroll shrinks, household spending and the tax system both lose support. That is the Correction widening into the Void.</p></div>

## Plan A turns the output gap into a permit bill

Takeoff means AI helps improve AI research, which can make capability grow much faster.

Plan A imagines a US-China deal in 2029 that prevents a 2030 jump to fully automated AI research. The parties pause at top-expert AI in 2035, then resume toward superintelligence in 2040. The authors call this a recommendation carried by an uncertain economic scenario.

Inside that scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor by mid-2031. Real GDP grows by about 50 percent in 2032. The US caps advanced compute and robot production, then sells permits worth a projected $50 trillion that year.

Some of the money goes into the Compute Dividend Corporation. One share goes to each citizen. The projected annual payout starts at $45,000 per person in 2032 and reaches about $1 million in 2035.

Alaska established its Permanent Fund in 1976 and paid its first dividend in 1982. Both documents use it. Plan A charges for scarce machine capacity before production. The Three Crucibles proposes taxes on AI labor or output as machines enter the economy.

I think the dollar values will age faster than the structure. Company B still needs a way to return part of its machine output to people whose wages once supported demand. The collection point can change, but the claim on the output has to exist.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A sells permits for scarce machine capacity and puts part of the proceeds into a citizen-owned corporation. The Three Crucibles taxes AI work closer to the point of use. Both try to keep machine abundance connected to human demand.</p></div>

## The Medog tunnels are Plan A's own warning

Plan A asks major data-center owners and upstream suppliers to declare large purchases and sales.

Inspectors validate those numbers, and almost all AI research becomes public. The plan also keeps pauses reversible. It spreads access and gives citizens an economic interest in the upside. This is far broader than a chip registry.

The report then tries to break it. In a covert branch, China diverts about 500,000 smuggled H100-equivalents into tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station. An H100-equivalent is a rough unit based on the computing power of Nvidia's H100 chip. Around 200 people may know about the project. In the scenario, its chance of reaching top-expert AI while hidden stays below 10 percent until 2043.

An inspector can confirm how many chips Company B's provider declares. Company B still has a problem if that provider raises prices or changes its license. A citizen share distributes cash. Inside the insurer, ownership means keeping its policy logic and the evidence needed to change suppliers. The weights may remain with a closed provider.

In *Alien*, the Nostromo crew eventually discovers Ash's secret orders. They gain evidence after Weyland-Yutani has already decided whose interests the mission serves. Visibility arrives. Control stays elsewhere.

[George Hotz's critique](https://geohot.github.io/blog/jekyll/update/2026/07/11/ai-2040.html) attacks Plan A from another side. He doubts hard takeoff because chip fabs and physical supply chains move slowly. His alternative puts AI closer to the individual owner.

I don't know which pressure breaks the scenario first. I think a clean provider switch would weaken my argument. Company B should be able to replay representative claims and preserve allowed outcomes. The covered €2,400 repair gives us that test.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A combines inspection with public research and a citizen share. Its covert branch shows how verification can still miss hidden activity. The insurer faces a smaller version of the problem when a closed provider can change the terms but the company cannot move the claims process.</p></div>

## Build the ownership test into the claim graph

Company B can turn the policy dispute into one failed claim replay.

Suppose its claim agent uses ReAct, short for reasoning and acting. The model reads the policy, calls a repair-estimate tool and checks the result before choosing its next step. It repeats that loop until it can propose payment or send the claim to a person.

LangGraph is a Python library for building a workflow that can branch or loop. A `StateGraph` is the graph object that connects the steps. Each step reads and updates one shared claim record. A `TypedDict` tells Python's type checker which fields belong in that record. It does not check live values while the program runs.

```python
from typing import Literal, TypedDict
from langgraph.graph import StateGraph

class ClaimState(TypedDict):
    claim_id: str
    repair_estimate_eur: float
    outcome: Literal["pay", "review", "deny"]
    model_id: str
    tool_trace: list[dict]

def needs_human(state: ClaimState) -> bool:
    return (
        state["outcome"] == "deny"
        or state["repair_estimate_eur"] > 5_000
    )

claim_graph = StateGraph(ClaimState)
```

The state records which model proposed the outcome. `tool_trace` stores the calls and results needed for replay. The human gate catches every denial and any estimate above €5,000.

An eval is a repeatable test of agent behavior. The covered repair has an expected result: `pay` or `review` is allowed. `deny` fails.

| Replay | Model | Outcome | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current | Provider A | `pay` | Pass |
| Fallback | Model B | `deny` | Fail |

The graph alone provides no portability. Company B keeps the state schema with its policy documents and stores prompts and tool traces in an exportable format. Its contract preserves data rights and names the fallback model. A closed provider is unlikely to transfer its weights. An open-weight model may allow self-hosting under its license.

Engineering runs the replay before signature and at renewal. Architecture owns the export format. Procurement and legal write the exit terms, including the EU or EEA data path. A failed replay blocks the deal until the defect is fixed or the executive owner accepts the risk.

On August 2, 2026, the bulk of the EU AI Act started applying. The obligations for general-purpose models have applied since August 2, 2025.

I think junior AI engineers should treat this replay as production work. A pilot proves that today's provider can settle the claim. The replay shows whether Company B can still settle it after the provider changes the deal. That is [reversibility as an engineering constraint](/posts/reversibility-is-the-new-correctness/), and it gives [the agent layer a named owner](/posts/who-owns-the-agent-layer/).

AI 2040 gives government a way to price machine output, while the replay checks Company B's operating rights. For the engineer, ownership has a boring proof: the covered claim still settles after the provider changes the terms.

Saving the claim with its trace lets a fallback eval turn provider exit into evidence before Company B signs the contract.</p></div>
