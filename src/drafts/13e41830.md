In 1976, Alaska voters amended their state constitution to create the Alaska Permanent Fund, a public investment fund for part of the state's mineral wealth. At least a quarter of certain mineral revenues goes into the fund instead of the annual budget. Dividend payments to residents began in 1982, giving Alaskans a financial claim on returns from a resource they collectively own.

Fifty years later, this old institution appears in two accounts of an AI-shaped economy. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/), published in July 2026, uses Alaska as the model for sharing wealth created by advanced AI. My 2026 speculative book, [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), has been public on my site since March 23, 2026 and reaches the same mechanism under the same name: the Citizen's Dividend.

My framework describes three passages. **The Correction** begins when AI raises output while reducing the need for human labor, weakening the link between productivity and wages. **The Void** is the transformation period in which the economy may grow while jobs, incomes, and consumer demand fail to recover together. **The Alignment** is the political choice that follows: broad stewardship of AI-created abundance or concentrated ownership of the systems producing it.

Agreement does not make either timeline true. It does show which institutional questions keep returning, even when the routes to them differ.

## What Plan A actually proposes

AI 2040 is a 90-page scenario and policy proposal from the AI Futures Project, the forecasting group behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its April 2025 scenario about rapid progress toward superhuman AI. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” It asks how the United States, China, and other countries could manage a fast rise in AI capability without losing political stability or human control.

Its preferred path begins with a US-China agreement in 2029. As automated AI research brings superintelligence close in 2030, participants pause and inspect compute, the chips and datacenter capacity used to train and run AI. Scaling stays within the range of human ability until another pause at top-expert AI in 2035, meaning systems that outperform the best human specialists across cognitive work. Scaling toward superintelligence, systems beyond human intellectual ability across almost every field, resumes in 2040 after years of transparency and safety work.

Plan A's economy changes just as quickly. In the scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor by mid-2031 and real output grows by roughly 50 percent in 2032. Labor-tax revenue shrinks as machines replace wage-paying jobs, so governments sell permits to build compute and robots. A scheduled share of the fees rises from 25 percent in 2032 to 75 percent in 2035 and funds a Compute Dividend Corporation, with one share for every US citizen and profits paid as dividends.

<p>Plan A proposes pauses, inspected computing capacity, international coordination, and a citizen-owned dividend fund to manage a fast transition toward superintelligence.</p>

## Where the forecasts meet and part ways

The clearest overlap is economic. The Correction and Plan A both describe productive capacity separating from human earning power. My book puts the demand problem plainly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Greater output cannot preserve household demand or employment-based taxes if fewer people earn wages.

Both accounts then reach for a Citizen's Dividend and the Alaska precedent. They fund it differently: my framework proposes an AI labor tax and distributed ownership, while Plan A uses permits for scarce computing capacity and robot production. The shared purpose is to return part of AI infrastructure's value to citizens so the consumer economy can keep functioning.

The deeper difference is control. Plan A emphasizes verification through declared computing capacity, inspected facilities, shared research, and evidence before scaling. Its appendix estimates a below-10-percent chance that a covert project could reach top-expert AI undetected before 2043. My framework puts more weight on who owns productive systems and holds a lasting claim on their output. Verification asks whether agreed limits are being followed; ownership decides who benefits when the machines operate. Preparation requires both answers.

## A preparation document, not a prophecy

No one knows what comes next, and precise dates can create a false sense of certainty. AI progress may slow because energy, chips, institutions, or the physical world refuse to move at software speed. Even then, the questions about demand, safe scaling, and ownership remain.

That is the purpose of my book. It is a preparation document for choices that become much harder once disruption is under way. If the AI Futures Project and I are even roughly right, the road will be bumpy because economic change, safety decisions, and political bargaining will arrive together. Alaska cannot tell us how to control superintelligence, but it shows the value of deciding who shares in a resource before its wealth reshapes society.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Neither forecast can know the future. Their practical value is preparation: decide how control, distribution, and ownership should work before rapid AI change forces those choices under pressure.</p>
