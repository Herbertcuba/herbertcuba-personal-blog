Buried inside [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a fictional company owned equally by every US citizen and funded by fees for robot and computing capacity. An AI safety plan becomes a draft economic constitution.

Plan A is the AI Futures Project’s 2026 policy recommendation, a conditional scenario rather than a best-guess forecast. The research nonprofit published [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/) on April 3, 2025; four of its five front-page authors return.

My site records the first PDF and link for my speculative book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), on March 23, 2026, and the update on June 24. AI 2040 added its PDF link on July 9. This proves artifact order only, not influence, independent discovery, validation, or accuracy.

I think both works map a break between machine output and wages, then separate over infrastructure visibility, durable economic rights, and a buyer’s ability to leave.

<p>The dates allow comparison, not victory. The overlap identifies an economic risk; the divergence separates visibility, economic rights, and supplier exit.</p>

## The economic break is the real convergence

The PDF of The Three Crucibles opens with The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. Chapters called The Void and The Transformation develop the middle stage; the landing page calls it The Transformation. The sequence moves from labor losing value to production outrunning demand, then to ownership and distribution.

Plan A quantifies the same problem. Its scenario has AI performing one third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, meaning work based mainly on reasoning and judgment. In 2032, it models roughly 50 percent inflation-adjusted growth while lost work cuts personal-income and payroll receipts and deductions for machine investment cut taxable income. Output rises while purchasing power and public funding weaken.

The remedies expose agreement and disagreement. Plan A sells capacity permits, tradable permissions to build limited robot or computing infrastructure. Its scenario projects $50 trillion in US permit revenue in fiscal 2032 and a $45,000 Citizen’s Dividend per American adult in 2025 dollars. These are conditional outputs, not observed results.

[The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) pairs its Citizen’s Dividend with an AI Labor Equivalence Tax, a levy on human-equivalent AI work tied to what a person in that role would contribute to public revenue. Its demand argument is blunt: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Machine abundance can grow while its customers lose income.

Both works point to the Alaska Permanent Fund, created by a 1976 constitutional amendment to invest mineral revenue and support resident dividends, first paid in 1982. I think it reveals shared logic without proving a shared path: citizens need an enforceable claim when a new resource displaces the old income base.

Norway can pressure-test that logic. [Statistics Norway](https://www.ssb.no/en), the official statistics agency, publishes [AI use by industry](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/13265), [value added and employee compensation](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/09170), [employment and productivity](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/09174), and [taxes and social contributions](https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/10799). Use 2023–2025 as a baseline and compare the fastest and slowest AI-use growth through 2032 after matching prior productivity trends. The strong thesis loses if high-adoption industries record productivity growth ten percentage points above the low-adoption group while labor’s share of value added, the part paid to workers, stays within two points of baseline and social-contribution receipts stay stable as a share of the economy. A labor-share fall of more than five points alongside weaker receipts supports it. Tax reform, oil shocks, reclassification, or a result between those bounds is inconclusive. This cannot prove causation, but it can make the claim lose.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Both works reconnect machine production to citizens through different dividend mechanisms. Norway’s official series test whether productivity can rise without weakening labor income and employment-linked public revenue.</p>

## Visibility and ownership solve different failures

Plan A begins with takeoff, a feedback loop in which AI automates AI research and speeds its improvement. Its scenario reaches a US-China agreement in 2029, pauses in 2035 at AI matching the best human experts, and resumes in 2040 toward superintelligence, AI better than the best humans across tasks while also faster and cheaper.

Restraint depends on compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI. Chip transactions would be declared, datacenters inspected, and research disclosed. Developers would publish safety cases, written arguments against irreversible catastrophe, for public and expert criticism. A fictional branch then hides processors beside China’s unfinished Medog Hydropower Station project and falsifies records, yet still needs construction, power, and cooling. I think verification turns restraint into an evidence problem because industrial capacity leaves physical traces.

Ownership addresses another failure. Plan A’s Compute Dividend Corporation would hold a fixed part of the permit fees, with one equal company share per US citizen. That is not direct ownership of robots, datacenters, AI models, or companies. My book proposes distributed ownership: individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities holding AI infrastructure and its income.

Plan A’s 2040 storyline hands institutions and equipment to AI systems until humans could no longer shut everything down. The Three Crucibles asks whether people remain stewards of abundance or depend on a narrow owner class. I don’t know which mix survives geopolitical pressure. My take is that safety rules and economic rights must be joined because each leaves the other’s failure untouched.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A uses inspection and safety cases against hidden capacity and dangerous systems. The Three Crucibles pushes ownership outward. Visibility cannot distribute gains; ownership cannot prove safety.</p>
</div>

## Practical exit is the third control

The controls address power at different scales. States need evidence about hidden infrastructure, citizens need rights to automated wealth, and organizations need a tested right to move a vital workflow. Both economic rights and exit rights must exist before dependence hardens.

My book compares two fictional insurers. One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; the other uses coordinated AI agents, software that handles claims work, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. Adoption can outrun the buyer’s ability to reverse it.

That is reversibility capture: a supplier retains the data, licences, interfaces, certification, or computing capacity that makes departure expensive. Exit proof is a live handover in which an independent team runs one critical workflow before renewal. I think it should be mandatory when failure can stop a critical operation or harm people, or when two other risks are high: sensitive data, supplier concentration, and replacement difficulty. Low-risk tools need only a tested export and documented settings.

The method borrows from disaster-recovery drills that rehearse restoration and portability tests that move data and settings. Exit proof adds operation against buyer-owned cases, frozen examples controlled by the buyer, and a result that changes renewal.

| Record | Decision rule |
| --- | --- |
| Scope | Freeze model and environment versions, cases and expected results, permitted tools and data, and the named grader that scores each run. |
| Pass | Run three times. Every run meets safety, authority, and evidence gates; the median meets error, time, and cost limits. |
| Retry | Retry only a recorded external outage. A changed version, missing log, or unavailable grader is indeterminate and requires a full rerun. |
| Owners and cost | Business accepts risk and downtime; technology restores service. The buyer funds its team and reviewer. Supplier fees are fixed; the supplier repairs and retests failures caused by missing access or documentation. |

An illustrative claims-triage pilot allows 30 days and 15 people-days for 200 historical cases. The median error rate stays within two percentage points of the incumbent baseline, with completion inside one hour and a cost cap. A correct result without reconstructable sources, versions, and permitted tools still fails. These are proposals, not an industry standard.

The [European Union’s 2024 AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), its risk-based AI law, became generally applicable on August 2, 2026. Rules for general-purpose models, which support many tasks, had applied since August 2, 2025; some high-risk rules transition later. Existing descriptions and logs support exit proof, but cannot prove a handover.

A failed or indeterminate test triggers repair and a rerun before renewal. Continued failure means repricing or non-renewal unless the portfolio risk forum, the group accountable for AI investment and risk, accepts a time-limited exception. Even a pass may leave dependence on exclusive data, licences, or scarce computing capacity, so technical exit does not settle ownership.

I think the common lesson is timing. Alaska secured a public claim before oil income became routine; buyers must secure exit before an AI workflow becomes indispensable. The machines may be built either way; who holds the durable claim is still open.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Exit proof is a risk-tiered handover test agreed at signing and run before renewal against fixed versions, buyer cases, measurable limits, and allocated costs. It protects a buyer without solving society’s ownership problem. Visibility, economic rights, and exit must be secured before concentrated control becomes ordinary.</p>
</div>
