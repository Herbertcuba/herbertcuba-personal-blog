In 1976, after oil from Alaska's Prudhoe Bay field created a large but temporary source of public revenue, the state amended its constitution to save part of that income in the Alaska Permanent Fund. The first dividend in 1982 gave eligible residents a direct cash claim on returns from a shared resource.

That design returns in [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), a 2026 policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, the research group behind both this report and its predecessor, *AI 2027*. Four of Plan A's six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), a 2025 scenario about a rapid race to advanced AI. The connection matters because Plan A is their positive policy response to the dangers described in the earlier work, although they call it a recommendation rather than their best-guess forecast. It proposes a Citizen's Dividend, a recurring cash payment funded by fees on AI infrastructure, and explicitly models the mechanism on Alaska.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my book about three stages of AI-driven economic change, reaches the same mechanism under the same name. The [public GitHub repository that stores my website's source files](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) shows its PDF on the site on March 23, 2026, and the edition cited here is dated June 24. A [Hacker News submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425), a post on a public technology forum, linked to *AI 2040* on July 9. Those records establish the order of the public artifacts. They cannot prove when either idea began or whether one influenced the other.

I think the useful claim is narrower than validation. The two works converge on part of the economic chain, then divide over control. Plan A emphasizes verification, the declarations and inspections used to expose hidden computing capacity such as chips, data centers, and power. The book asks who owns that infrastructure and its output. My extension is exit proof, a supplier-funded handover that tests whether another team can run a critical workflow before renewal. Verification governs hidden capacity, ownership allocates gains, and exit proof tests whether an institution still has bargaining power.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works reach the same Citizen's Dividend through overlapping economic logic, but their public dates establish sequence rather than influence. Their different answers to control make the comparison useful.</p></div>

## The three crucibles meet Plan A

The [June 24 PDF edition used here](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment as the three crucibles. Transformation is a chapter within the Void.

The Correction begins with two pressures: an AI market crash makes cash and financing harder to obtain, while an energy shock squeezes operating margins. Together they remove the financial room that had allowed companies to carry labor-heavy operations while AI stayed in pilot projects. This is the book's “Human Shield”: the ability to protect less efficient human workflows by absorbing their higher cost. Once that room disappears, a faster-automating rival can charge less, and delay becomes difficult to sustain.

The Void follows when AI stops being mainly a promise and starts producing real value without requiring as much human work. The economy recovers in the book, but many jobs do not. Inside the Void, the Transformation chapter explains the demand problem: when labor income falls, consumers lose spending power. “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Its response is a [Citizen's Dividend that transfers part of the income from AI infrastructure to consumers](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36).

In Plan A's scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, meaning work based on analysis, research, communication, or judgment. As paid human work shrinks, personal-income and payroll-tax revenue weakens. The US government in the scenario sells tradable permits, which grant the right to build new robots and AI computing capacity, and directs most permit fees to a Citizen's Dividend projected at [$45,000 per American adult in 2032](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18) and about $1 million in 2035.

The map is close, but it is not one-to-one. Plan A has no equivalent of the book's combined hype crash and energy shock, so the Correction is a different opening mechanism. The strongest convergence sits in the Void and its Transformation chapter: automation weakens labor income, taxes, and consumer demand, which creates pressure for a dividend. The Alignment then meets Plan A's concern about concentrated power. Funding still differs. The book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax on each unit of machine work measured against comparable human work, while Plan A charges for permission to build robots and computing capacity. I think this is meaningful thematic convergence, not proof that either timeline will happen.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction has no direct Plan A counterpart. The clearest overlap comes later: both works connect automated work to weaker income and demand, then propose a Citizen's Dividend, although one would tax machine labor and the other would charge for new capacity.</p></div>

## Verification does not settle ownership

Plan A centers on takeoff, a feedback loop in which AI automates AI research and speeds the creation of more capable systems. In its scenario, a 2029 agreement between the United States and China avoids a route to superintelligence by the end of 2030. Superintelligence means systems that exceed humans across almost all intellectual work. Development pauses in 2035 at top-expert capability, where AI can match leading human specialists on the relevant intellectual tasks, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040.

The agreement makes major chip and data-center transactions visible, opens infrastructure to reciprocal inspection, and makes almost all AI research public while keeping the use of existing models private. Its covert-project appendix stress-tests that system with a secret Chinese data center built in tunnels beside the proposed Medog Hydropower Station in Tibet. In the scenario, the dam would supply electricity, while the tunnels would make the facility's heat harder for satellites to detect. Like *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film whose virtual world depends on physical machines, advanced AI still leaves a trail of chips, electricity, cooling, construction, and people.

I think that physical trail makes inspection plausible, but visibility does not settle distribution. Plan A gives each US citizen one share in a public corporation that receives part of the permit revenue, creating a claim on income. The [Alignment calls for distributed ownership](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57) of the infrastructure and models themselves, backed by laws against a private intelligence monopoly. Plan A offers far more detail on treaties and AI safety; the book presses an ownership question that a revenue share only partly answers. I don't know whether Plan A's verification system could survive sustained US-China rivalry, and its authors do not claim certainty.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification can expose hidden AI development because the required infrastructure leaves physical evidence. A dividend can spread income, but ownership of the models and machines remains a separate source of power.</p></div>

## Exit proof makes control practical

Even when governments can see computing capacity and citizens hold revenue claims, an institution can still lose practical choice. A company may own its final documents while depending on an AI supplier for usable data, permissions, system settings, connections to other tools, and operating knowledge. Exit proof turns that dependence into a test: can a replacement team run the workflow before renewal?

The book's fictional insurer example makes the pressure concrete. One insurer keeps 5,000 claims adjusters, the people who investigate claims and decide payments. A rival deploys an AI system, cuts staff by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months ([printed p. 20](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21)). The numbers are illustrative, but the rush they create is easy to understand: sign first, ask how to leave later.

I would make renewal conditional on a supplier-funded, 30-day handover of one workflow and one complete data extract to one replacement team. The test must separate what the new team receives, what it can access, and what it must prove.

The replacement team receives usable data, a record of where it came from and how it changed, versioned system settings and model instructions, and operating and recovery guidance. It also gets the permissions and documented connections needed to use every dependent tool. Finally, it demonstrates the business outcome on an evaluation set, a fixed sample of past cases with accepted results that the buyer owns. The replacement may change the model, its instructions, how it finds supporting material, or the internal workflow, but it must stay within agreed limits for errors, time, and cost.

The chief information officer owns acceptance. Missing data, inaccessible permissions, absent recovery instructions, or results outside the agreed limits fail the test and block automatic renewal. The supplier then funds correction and transition support. If a retest still fails, the buyer can end the agreement without a termination fee while support continues for the contracted transition period. A portability promise has little value unless failure changes the deal.

Since August 2, 2026, [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), part of the European Union's main AI law, has required disclosure for certain direct interactions with AI. In specified cases, it also requires synthetic audio, images, video, or text to carry a machine-readable mark, meaning a label or metadata signal that automated systems can detect. Those rules can reveal AI use. They do not provide the data, permissions, and operating knowledge another team needs.

I think the consequence is falsifiable. If a system passes its disclosure checks but a replacement team cannot complete the bounded handover, changing supplier has already become impractical while the compliance file still looks clean. Alaska settled a public claim on shared wealth before that revenue became part of ordinary politics. A technology buyer has the same chance only before dependence hardens, because the leverage exists before the signature.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof makes renewal depend on a bounded, supplier-funded handover judged against the buyer's own past cases. A failed test blocks renewal and triggers transition rights, revealing supplier lock-in that transparency rules alone cannot show.</p></div>
