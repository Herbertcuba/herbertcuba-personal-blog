In 1976, as North Slope oil was reshaping Alaska’s finances, voters approved a constitutional amendment creating the Alaska Permanent Fund. Instead of spending all its mineral revenue, the state placed a portion in a publicly owned investment fund. The first dividend was paid in 1982, and eligible residents have received an annual cash share ever since. The fund turns wealth from a finite, shared resource into a lasting public claim.

Fifty years later, that idea sits inside two attempts to think through AI. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) appeared in July 2026. [My speculative book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), had been public since March. Both use the term “Citizen’s Dividend” and point to Alaska. That does not prove either future. It does suggest that the same social pressure is becoming hard to ignore.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows how citizens can hold a direct claim on shared wealth, and both works apply that old principle to AI-created value.</p></div>

## Plan A uses a timeline to force a choice

The AI Futures Project is a scenario-building and policy team whose six Plan A authors include four of the five authors behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its 2025 study of rapid AI progress and catastrophic loss of control. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Its scenario moves from a US–China agreement in 2029 to near-automated AI research in 2030, a pause around top-human-expert capability in 2035, and superintelligence in 2040. Here, technical takeoff means a feedback loop in which AI helps build stronger AI; superintelligence means systems that outperform humans across almost all cognitive work.

The engineering quickly becomes politics. “Compute” means the processing capacity supplied by chips and data centers. Plan A proposes tracking chips, inspecting facilities, sharing research information, and selling permits that authorize the deployment of scarce chips and robots under coordinated caps. In its scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor, meaning information and reasoning work, by mid-2031. It also projects roughly 50 percent growth in real GDP, or inflation-adjusted economic output, in 2032. As wage-linked taxes shrink, permit revenue funds a citizen dividend projected at $45,000 per person that year. These are scenario outputs, not observed facts.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A imagines a managed takeoff in which international verification limits AI capacity and permit revenue replaces part of a collapsing labor-tax base.</p></div>

## The three crucibles describe social pressure

*The Three Crucibles* divides the transition into three stages. **The Correction** (2026) begins when AI makes production less dependent on paid human labor, weakening the old link between work and income. **The Transformation** (2027–2032) is the difficult bridge, when a universal basic income, a regular payment without a work requirement, and a citizen’s dividend keep purchasing power moving while institutions adjust. **The Alignment** (2035+) asks who controls the resulting abundance: broad stewards or a small group of owners.

The point is not that three dates will arrive on schedule. The stages describe a change in value, then distribution, then legitimacy. Neither the dividend idea nor the Alaska precedent is new. What matters is the shared sequence: production relies less on labor, so citizens need another claim on its output before concentration becomes permanent.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The crucibles move from labor disruption to income redesign and finally to the political question of who owns and governs abundance.</p></div>

## The overlap ends where the method begins

Both works place the economic rupture before superintelligence. Output can rise while wages, tax receipts, and demand weaken, which is why both reach for a public dividend. They also end at the same fork: AI-created wealth can be spread broadly or concentrated among those who own the machines.

Their methods differ. Plan A relies on verification, meaning evidence that countries and companies are obeying limits through chip records, inspections, and research transparency. My book puts more weight on distributed ownership, in which citizens and smaller actors hold claims on AI infrastructure, alongside taxes on work performed by AI. Plan A’s own appendix tests its system with a hypothetical Chinese data center hidden near the Medog hydropower project; it assigns below a 10 percent chance that the project reaches AI able to dominate top human experts without detection before 2043. Low probability still matters when the stakes are this large.

Verification can restrain a race, but it cannot decide who receives the returns. Ownership can spread those returns, but it cannot by itself prevent a hidden race. Each work therefore supplies a layer the other leaves thinner.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works share an economic diagnosis but emphasize different safeguards: Plan A verifies capacity, while The Three Crucibles asks who owns its output.</p></div>

## Preparation begins before certainty

You do not need to accept Plan A’s dates to see the nearer question. If productivity separates from wages while public revenue remains tied to human work, the social contract comes under pressure long before any superintelligence appears. If that separation does not happen, a central part of both arguments weakens. The claim is serious, but it is not unfalsifiable.

I do not know what waits for us, and Plan A does not remove that uncertainty. If its authors and I are even directionally right, however, the road will be bumpy because institutions built around human labor will not adjust at machine speed. That is the purpose of my book: a preparation document for a transition we cannot predict precisely, but should not enter unprepared.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>No one knows the timetable, but preparing for a break between production, wages, and ownership is rational before that break becomes irreversible.</p></div>
