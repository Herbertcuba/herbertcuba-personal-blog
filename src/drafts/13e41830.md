[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) reaches a conclusion that already sits at the center of [*The Three Crucibles*](/books/the-three-crucibles/): if AI moves income away from labor while production keeps rising, citizens need a direct claim on machine productivity. Both frameworks call that claim a Citizen's Dividend. Both point to Alaska, where a permanent fund created in 1976 began paying residents in 1982.

A [March 23, 2026 repository commit](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) added *The Three Crucibles* PDF to my site. On July 9, [*AI 2040* reached Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). Its authors Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Eli Lifland and Daniel Kokotajlo also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/). The Citizen's Dividend phrase has older roots, but the two full frameworks assembled the same economic structure months apart.

I think the overlap is too specific to dismiss as a fashionable agreement about universal income. The two maps connect the same economic break to the same response, then separate over control. Plan A relies on verified computing capacity and transparent research. *The Three Crucibles* puts more weight on ownership. That split gives us a sharper test than either map provides alone.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 independently reaches the labor-income break and Citizen's Dividend found in The Three Crucibles. Their disagreement begins after the dividend, when society still has to decide who controls AI capacity and owns its output.</p></div>

## The cross-map has four parts

Plan A calls itself a recommendation, separate from its authors' best guess. Its takeoff path, meaning the assumed speed of AI improvement, includes a 2029 US-China agreement, a pause at top-human-expert AI in 2035, and a move toward superintelligence in 2040.

<div class="callout" role="group" aria-label="Four-part cross-map">
  <span class="callout__label">Four pressure points</span>
  <p><strong>1. The break.</strong> In Plan A's scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031. The Correction describes the same pressure: productivity shifts toward capital as wage-supported demand weakens.</p>
  <p><strong>2. The dividend.</strong> Plan A's scenario sets the dividend at $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035. The Transformation uses the same mechanism to support consumers.</p>
  <p><strong>3. The funding.</strong> Plan A sells tradable permits to build scarce robot and computing capacity. My framework proposes a tax on AI labor. The diagnosis converges while the collection point differs.</p>
  <p><strong>4. The fork.</strong> Plan A answers state competition and technical loss of control. The Alignment asks whether abundance produces broad stewardship or a new class of concentrated owners.</p>
</div>

I think the shared economic logic is stronger than any exact date. You can reject the takeoff speed and still see the demand problem. As *The Three Crucibles* puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Output rises while fewer people can buy it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The maps converge on the economic break, the dividend, and concentrated power. They split on timing and funding: Plan A prices permission to build scarce capacity, while The Three Crucibles proposes taxing AI labor.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership cover different failures

I think Plan A is strongest on verification, which checks that declared chips, datacenters, and research activity match reality. Treaties, inspections and controlled pauses could reduce the risk of a hidden race between states. Distributed ownership cannot perform that job.

Plan A also tests its own answer. Appendix D imagines China hiding computing capacity equal to 500,000 Nvidia H100 chips in tunnels near the Medog Hydropower Station, with around 200 people aware of the project. It feels like *Andor*: a vast system can watch almost everything and still miss a small network working inside its machinery. Verification makes cheating harder, but determined actors will still look for gaps.

Ownership covers the other side. Open infrastructure and wider claims on AI output can reduce dependence on one company or state, yet they cannot make a frontier model safe or expose a covert national project. My take is that these layers belong together. I don't know which failure arrives first. I think a serious governance model has to handle both.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification limits hidden races and unsafe frontier development. Distributed ownership limits economic dependence and concentrated claims on AI output. Each covers a failure that the other leaves open.</p></div>

## The fork reaches the contract desk

The insurer thought experiment in *The Three Crucibles* makes this practical. One company keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. A rival deploys a swarm of AI agents, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. Its next risk is dependence on the system that made those gains possible.

AI transparency tells us what we can inspect; exit rights tell us whether we retain agency when a supplier changes the terms. I call the practical test operational reversibility: the proven ability to move an AI-supported service, including its records and decision history, to another provider or back in-house.

<div class="callout">
  <span class="callout__label">A 90-day reversibility drill</span>
  <p><strong>Export:</strong> Move one live workflow with its source data, decision logs, model versions, evaluation cases and access rules.</p>
  <p><strong>Replay:</strong> Run a sample of past decisions on a replacement setup and explain every meaningful difference.</p>
  <p><strong>Recover:</strong> Restore the service within an agreed time and cost before the contract renews.</p>
</div>

Europe now has a natural decision point. Most of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng) began applying on August 2, 2026, while its general-purpose-model rules had applied since August 2, 2025. Disclosure makes a system easier to inspect. A live exit drill shows whether the buyer can still leave it.

I think Alaska offers the right final lesson. It settled ownership before oil revenue became too large to redesign calmly. AI gives us a shorter window, so ownership and exit rights belong in today's contracts while buyers still have leverage.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The broad control debate reaches ordinary contracts. Exporting, replaying and restoring one real workflow shows whether transparency comes with practical choice, which is the control institutions can still negotiate today.</p></div>
