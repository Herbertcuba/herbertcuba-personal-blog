In 1976, Alaska voters decided that part of the state's mineral wealth should belong to the future. They amended the state constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenue would enter the Alaska Permanent Fund, a publicly owned fund that invests the money. The related Permanent Fund Dividend began in 1982 as an annual cash payment to eligible residents. Its first check came from surplus oil revenue rather than fund income; fund earnings supported later payments.

That old decision sits at the heart of two new accounts of an AI-shaped economy. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), a scenario and policy proposal published in July 2026, uses Alaska as a model for sharing AI-created wealth. So does [my 2026 speculative book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), whose framework was in writing by February 3, more than six months before this article.

The book names three stages. **The Correction** is the economic shock as productivity depends more on AI capital and less on paid human labor. **The Transformation** is the rebuilding of income and demand through measures such as a citizen dividend, meaning direct payments funded by AI productivity. **The Alignment** is the political choice between broad stewardship of that productive capacity and concentrated ownership.

The dates may be wrong. The institutional question arrives either way: can we create a public claim on AI wealth before the wealth and power settle elsewhere?

## What Plan A actually proposes

The [AI Futures Project](https://www.aifutures.org/about), a US nonprofit studying advanced AI and policy, published Plan A on July 9, 2026. Four of its six authors also helped write *AI 2027*, the group's 2025 scenario about a fast race toward advanced AI. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” which is the right way to read its precise dates.

Its proposed sequence begins with a US-China agreement in 2029. Near-automated AI research follows in 2030, meaning AI systems perform most of the work needed to build stronger AI systems. The scenario then pauses at “top-expert” AI in 2035 before moving toward superintelligence, intelligence beyond the best humans across relevant mental tasks, in 2040.

The economic claims are just as sharp. In the scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor, work based mainly on language, judgment, and reasoning, by mid-2031. Plan A expects wage-based taxes to shrink with human labor and proposes fees for permits to use large amounts of compute, the processing capacity behind advanced AI. A Compute Dividend Corporation would give every US citizen one share and receive 25 percent of those permit fees in 2032, rising to 75 percent in 2035.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A combines a managed path to superintelligence with compute permits, public ownership, and direct citizen payments as labor income declines.</p>

## The agreement is about pre-commitment

The strongest overlap is structural. The Correction matches Plan A's warning that a tax system built around wages can erode quickly. The Transformation matches its chapter titled “The Citizen's Dividend,” including the same Alaska precedent. The Alignment matches Plan A's principle to “Diffuse AI Broadly” and its proposal that every citizen own an equal share in the dividend corporation.

This is convergence, not proof that either work developed without influence from the other. The sequence only shows that my framework preceded Plan A. More importantly, a later and much more detailed scenario reached the same underlying conclusion: waiting to redistribute wealth after ownership has concentrated may be too late.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Both accounts move from labor disruption to a citizen dividend and then to the question of whether AI ownership becomes broad or concentrated.</p>

## The disagreement is about what can hold

Plan A puts more weight on international coordination: declared data centers, tracked chips, inspections, safety cases, and agreed limits on advanced systems. The Three Crucibles puts more weight on preparing the social contract for disruption even when the technical timetable remains uncertain. Both address control and ownership, but they place their confidence differently.

Plan A also tests its own confidence. An appendix models a covert Chinese project and estimates below a 10 percent chance that it could reach “Top-Expert-Dominating AI” undetected before 2043. That threshold means systems able to outperform leading human experts across most cognitive tasks. The low estimate still makes the philosophical limit clear: verification can reduce uncertainty, but it cannot abolish it.

<p>Plan A relies more on coordinated verification, while my book treats broad economic preparation as necessary even when monitoring and timelines fail.</p>

The mapping is narrow: Plan A treats compute as a strategic input whose ownership and permitted use can shape the wider order. Governing the systems while ignoring control of that input would leave half the political problem untouched.

## A forecast we can test early

We do not have to wait for 2040 to judge the shared thesis. By 2030, it should be visible in three places: public budgets should begin accounting for weaker wage-tax growth, serious proposals should give citizens a claim on AI infrastructure or its income, and verification should move from company promises toward checks that governments can perform across borders.

For Sweden and its Nordic neighbors, the transfer test is concrete: if labor contributes less to public revenue, what claim on AI productivity replaces it? If that question remains absent while AI use rises, the shared map weakens. If it enters fiscal and ownership policy before mass displacement, the deeper prediction is holding.

No one knows what waits for us. Plan A's authors say so, and my book is a preparation document for the same reason. If these two accounts are directionally right, the road ahead is likely to be bumpy because capability can move faster than the institutions meant to contain and share its gains. Preparation means deciding which institutions should exist before that pressure makes calm choices harder.
