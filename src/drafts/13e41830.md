Alaska made its most important oil decision before the windfall arrived. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was still being built and before large revenues from Prudhoe Bay crude began flowing, voters created the Alaska Permanent Fund to save part of the state's mineral income for the public. The first dividend followed in 1982. Alaskans had established a public claim while the terms were still open.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a 2026 policy recommendation from the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that studies advanced AI and how governments might respond. Four of the five authors of [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's 2025 scenario about a rapid race toward advanced AI, also worked on it. The authors explicitly present Plan A as a recommendation expressed through a scenario, not as their best-guess prediction.

My book [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) was [published on March 22, 2026](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GTK53C9H). Its canonical sequence is the Correction, the Transformation, and the Alignment. Plan A appeared in July. That order does not establish influence, and this is not external data confirming my dates. I think the stronger observation is structural: two independent maps reach the same economic fork, then expose different requirements for control.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works share a specific economic sequence, but their overlap strengthens a strategic frame rather than proving either forecast.</p></div>

## The economic sequence converges

The Correction is the book's financial reckoning. It calls the willingness to preserve inefficient staffing the “Humane Shield,” a buffer companies can afford while capital is cheap and markets keep rising. In the book's sequence, an AI market correction destroys those conditions, so firms can no longer defer automation without paying a competitive price. Plan A starts elsewhere, but its scenario reaches a similar labor shock: by mid-2031, AI performs a third of all cognitive labor, meaning work based mainly on analysis, communication, and judgment.

That shift creates a problem beyond unemployment. Plan A projects roughly 50 percent real economic growth in 2032, yet it also warns that the tax base could collapse as a share of the economy. When paid human work falls, personal-income and payroll taxes fall with it; companies can also deduct or depreciate heavy investment in robots and computing equipment, so corporate tax does not automatically fill the gap. Household demand weakens for the same reason: machines produce, but they do not earn wages or shop. As *The Three Crucibles* puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.”

The Transformation, the second crucible, asks what replaces wages as both income and the fuel for consumer demand. Its [Citizen's Dividend](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36) gives people a direct claim on the output of AI infrastructure. Plan A uses the same name and the same Alaska precedent. In its scenario, the United States sells tradable permits to build robots or computing capacity, the chips and facilities needed to train and run AI. A fixed share of the permit fees goes to a Compute Dividend Corporation; every US citizen owns one share, and the corporation pays its profits equally as dividends. The projected payment, in inflation-adjusted 2025 dollars, rises from [$45,000 per American adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18).

The funding rules differ. [*The Three Crucibles* proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37), which charges for each human-equivalent unit of AI work according to the income-tax, social-security, and payroll revenue a person in that role would have generated. Plan A instead charges for permission to add physical capacity. I think the convergence matters because it goes beyond a general appeal for basic income: both works connect automation to weaker wages, public revenue, and consumer demand, then answer with a public claim on machine production. You do not need to accept Plan A's speed or dollar figures to take that mechanism seriously.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Correction and Transformation meet Plan A in the same economic chain: automated cognitive labor weakens wages, taxes, and demand, creating pressure for a Citizen's Dividend even though the proposed funding methods differ.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership are different controls

The Alignment, the third crucible, is the political test of who controls AI infrastructure and on what terms its gains are shared. Plan A approaches this question through takeoff governance. Takeoff is the period when AI performs enough AI research to accelerate its own improvement, potentially turning an ordinary technology race into a much faster one.

In Plan A's scenario, a 2029 agreement between the United States and China interrupts a race that might otherwise produce superintelligence by the end of 2030. Superintelligence means AI that outperforms the best humans across fields while working faster and more cheaply. Development continues within the range of human ability until a separate 2035 pause at top-expert AI, roughly the level of the strongest human specialists, before resuming toward superintelligence in 2040. These are proposed stages in a controlled path, not observed milestones.

The plan rests heavily on verification, meaning evidence that countries are obeying agreed limits. It calls for declarations of major chip and data-center transactions, reciprocal inspections, and near-total transparency in AI research. Its fictional covert-project appendix stress-tests that system: roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents, a measure of computing performance rather than a literal chip count, are hidden in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station, a huge dam under construction in Tibet. The construction provides cover while concealed power systems keep the computers running. Verification is possible because advanced AI still has a physical footprint, but the appendix shows how hard inspection becomes when a state is determined to hide it.

This is where *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film about intelligent machines running a simulated reality, becomes useful. Its green code looks weightless, but the machine world behind it still needs industrial hardware and power. Advanced AI is similar: software may move at digital speed, while its computing capacity remains attached to chips, electricity, cooling, and buildings that inspectors can try to find.

Inspection does not decide ownership. Plan A gives citizens a share in a dividend corporation, which is a legal claim on its profits rather than ownership of the chips, models, or companies producing the value. [*The Three Crucibles* argues for distributed ownership](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=56): AI infrastructure that individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities can own, including models that run locally or on their own premises. I think these mechanisms answer different risks. I don't know which balance can survive geopolitical pressure, and Plan A's authors do not claim certainty either. What I do know is that a system can be visible to inspectors while a small group still sets the terms of access and distribution.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses verification to constrain hidden computing capacity; Alignment asks who controls the capacity everyone can see and receives what it produces. Visibility and ownership solve different problems.</p></div>

## Practical control requires an exit test

Neither state verification nor a citizen claim on AI returns proves that an institution can move a critical workflow away from its supplier. [*The Three Crucibles* imagines](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21) one insurer keeping 5,000 claims adjusters while a rival uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that divide and carry out parts of the claims process. The rival cuts staff by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. This is a thought experiment, not a reported case, but it shows why adoption pressure can move faster than a buyer's ability to leave the supplier behind the automation.

My take is that a critical AI contract should renew only after exit proof: a supplier-funded handover test showing that a replacement team can run one working process. A useful first test could cover one claims workflow, one portable copy of its case data, one replacement team, and a 30-day testing window, with the chief information officer accountable for the renewal decision. The buyer should own the past cases used to evaluate the replacement. The supplier should provide the saved workflow state, model instructions, tool connections, access rights, source records, data lineage showing where each input came from, and restore guidance needed to reproduce a decision.

The replacement may change the model, its instructions, its method for retrieving source material, or the workflow itself. What matters is whether it reproduces the agreed business result on the buyer's evaluation cases within fixed limits for errors, time, and cost. Missing records, blocked access, results outside those limits, or decisions that cannot be traced back to their sources should count as failure. A failed test should block automatic renewal and trigger funded transition assistance; a second failure should let the buyer leave without a termination fee.

I think this is what can break next. Governments may verify computing capacity and citizens may receive part of its return, yet companies, hospitals, or public agencies can remain trapped inside the systems carrying out their work. Verification governs hidden capacity. Ownership governs distribution. Exit proof governs agency. Control becomes real only when the right to choose survives a failed handover.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof turns supplier choice into a funded contract test: if a replacement cannot reproduce the business result, the supplier still controls the workflow regardless of what its disclosures promise.</p></div>
