Alaska settled an ownership question before its oil income became routine. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was being built to carry North Slope oil, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/) and reserved part of the state's mineral revenue. The first resident dividend followed in 1982. The public claim came first.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a policy recommendation expressed through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit focused on advanced AI. It follows [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's April 2025 scenario, and four of AI 2027's five authors also worked on Plan A. The authors say Plan A is primarily a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my speculative framework for AI's economic transition, has a [retail publication date of March 22, 2026](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GTK53C9H); its PDF was public on my site by March 23. I use the [June 24 edition](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) here. A [public link to Plan A appeared on July 9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). The order establishes timing, not influence or predictive accuracy.

I think the narrower comparison is more useful. The works converge on an economic break and a public dividend, then split over power. Together they suggest three tests: verification constrains hidden computing capacity, ownership allocates the gains, and exit proof shows whether an institution retains bargaining power at renewal.

## The closest overlap begins in the Void

The book's labels require care because two first-party versions differ. The opening map in the [June 24 PDF names the crucibles Correction, Void, and Alignment](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6). The current book page uses Transformation as the short label for the second, while the PDF places a Transformation chapter inside the Void. I follow the edition's three-part map.

The [Correction](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=19) describes inflated AI expectations meeting uneven returns, followed by company failures and automation under pressure. Plan A has no close equivalent. It starts from geopolitical rivalry and takeoff, the period when AI automates enough AI research to speed up its own improvement.

The economic overlap begins in the Void. The book imagines output recovering while many jobs do not, and its Transformation chapter explains the demand problem: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” In Plan A's scenario, AI performs [one third of all cognitive labor by mid-2031](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=13), meaning work based mainly on information, analysis, communication, and judgment. The scenario then projects about 50 percent real economic growth in 2032 while automation erodes the labor-tax base. Heavy investment in data centers, factories, and robots also reduces taxable profit. Production can soar while demand and the tax base erode.

Both works answer with a Citizen's Dividend, a public payment tied to AI productivity. [The book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37), based on what a person doing equivalent work would have contributed through taxes and social payments. [Plan A sells tradable permits](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17) for new robot and computing capacity, then directs part of the revenue to a corporation owned equally by US citizens. In the scenario, its dividend rises from $45,000 per American adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035, in 2025 dollars.

I think the convergence is meaningful because it includes the broken income loop, the Alaska precedent, and the same policy name. Yet “Citizen's Dividend” predates both works, and Plan A's numbers are scenario projections, not observed evidence. One design taxes AI work that replaces human contributions; the other charges for permission to expand the machines doing it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The June 24, 2026 edition of Herbert Cuba Garcia's The Three Crucibles maps Correction, Void, and Alignment; Transformation is the chapter within the Void that links lost wages to a Citizen's Dividend, a public payment from AI-driven gains. The AI Futures Project's conditional AI 2040 policy scenario has no matching market Correction, but it reaches the same income-and-tax problem through automated knowledge work.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership govern different failures

Plan A is much stronger on the technical danger that the book largely brackets. Its scenario has the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029. Without it, the authors model fully automated AI research and superintelligence by the end of 2030. Under the deal, development slows, pauses at top-human-expert ability in 2035, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. Superintelligence means AI significantly better than the best humans across fields while working faster and more cheaply. These are scenario dates, not promised events.

The agreement depends on verification. Major data-center owners and chip suppliers report large transactions publicly, rival powers inspect physical infrastructure, and AI research becomes almost entirely transparent. Plan A stress-tests this design with [an alternate covert-project branch](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45): China diverts roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents to tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station, with about 200 people aware. An H100-equivalent is a measure of AI computing performance based on Nvidia's H100 processor, not a literal chip count.

*The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film about intelligent machines hiding physical domination behind a simulated world, makes the mechanism visible. Its software illusion looks weightless, but the machine world still needs hardware and energy. Advanced AI casts the same physical shadow through chips, electricity, cooling, supply records, and buildings.

The book's [Alignment passage](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=56) asks who owns AI infrastructure and receives its output. Plan A gives citizens shares in the corporation receiving permit revenue, which creates an economic claim but not direct ownership of the chips, models, or companies. The book argues for infrastructure distributed among individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, including models that can run locally.

I don't know which balance could survive geopolitical pressure. I think the disagreement matters more than a forced match. Plan A tackles secret capacity and loss of control, where the book offers no comparable plan for containing superintelligence. The book goes further on distributed ownership, which inspections cannot create. Verification may expose a breach; it cannot decide who holds economic power afterward.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The AI Futures Project's conditional AI 2040 policy scenario uses public reporting, inspections, and transparent research to constrain a race toward superintelligence, meaning AI far beyond the best human performance. Alignment, the third passage in Herbert Cuba Garcia's 2026 book The Three Crucibles, asks who owns AI infrastructure. Verification can reveal hidden capacity but cannot distribute ownership; ownership does not by itself contain superintelligence.</p></div>

## The third test appears at renewal

These state-level questions become immediate when a hospital or insurer renews an AI contract. The book illustrates the pressure with [two fictional insurers](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21). One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. The other uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that execute parts of the claims process, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The numbers are illustrative, but the mechanism is clear: adoption can outrun a buyer's ability to reverse it.

My take is that every critical AI contract should make renewal conditional on exit proof, a supplier-funded handover test showing that a replacement team can run one agreed process. A first test might cover one claims workflow, one portable data export, one replacement team, and 30 days. The chief information officer should own the renewal decision; the executive accountable for the process should judge the result.

The handover package should include versioned workflow settings, model instructions, connections to other business systems, permissions, source records, restoration guidance, and data lineage, which records where inputs came from and how they changed. The buyer should own the historical cases and expected outcomes used for evaluation. A replacement may change the model, instructions, search method, or workflow, provided the business result stays within agreed limits for error, time, and cost.

Missing records, blocked access, results outside those limits, or decisions that cannot be traced to their sources should fail the test. One failure would not prove migration impossible forever; it would show that workable exit remains unproven. Automatic renewal should stop, funded transition assistance should begin, and a failed retest should let the buyer leave without a termination fee. I think that leverage exists before signing.

The [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law, shows why disclosure is a separate control. Rules for general-purpose AI models, which can perform many tasks and be integrated into other systems, have applied since August 2, 2025; most of the Act began applying on August 2, 2026. Its transparency duties do not demonstrate that a buyer can transfer a working process.

This is the second-order failure both maps leave open. A state may verify hidden capacity, and citizens may share AI-generated wealth, while a hospital cannot move a vital workflow away from one supplier. Alaska secured the public claim before oil income became routine. With AI, the claim on output and the practical right to leave must be secured before dependence hardens into infrastructure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof is a proposed supplier-funded handover test before an AI contract renews: a replacement team must run one process within limits for error, time, and cost using buyer-owned evaluation cases. Neither the AI Futures Project's conditional AI 2040 policy scenario nor Herbert Cuba Garcia's 2026 book The Three Crucibles develops this test. Transparency without a working exit leaves bargaining power with the supplier.</p></div>
