In 1976, Alaska voters created the Alaska Permanent Fund through a constitutional amendment. The fund invests a required share of state mineral revenue so a temporary oil windfall can become a lasting public asset. Alaska sent residents its first dividend check in 1982; that initial payment came from surplus oil revenue, not fund earnings.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), a July 2026 scenario and policy proposal from the AI Futures Project, returns to that precedent. The group also produced *AI 2027*, its April 2025 scenario about rapid progress toward superhuman AI. So does [my speculative book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/). Two forecasts agreeing do not turn either one into fact, but their independent overlap deserves attention.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows how citizens can retain a claim on temporary resource wealth. Both AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles apply that principle to machine productivity.</p></div>

## Two documents with different jobs

Plan A asks how the United States and China could govern advanced AI without racing into disaster. The Three Crucibles asks what happens when paid work stops being the main route to income and status.

- **The Correction** is the economic break: AI lets capital produce more with less paid human labor, weakening wages and tax revenue.
- **The Void** is the unstable interval after the old work-and-income link weakens but before a replacement is trusted.
- **The Alignment** is the political choice between broadly stewarded machine abundance and control by a narrow group.

My framework was already public when Plan A appeared. I call the result convergence rather than confirmation: Plan A is a serious second account of the same problem, not evidence that every date or mechanism in my book is right.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A focuses on controlling advanced AI, while the three Crucibles describe the economic break, the unstable transition, and the ownership choice around abundance.</p></div>

## What Plan A actually proposes

The authors call Plan A “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” In its preferred path, the United States and China make a deal in 2029, avoiding a route in which fully automated AI research produces superintelligence by the end of 2030. Fully automated research means AI systems do most of the work of designing their successors; superintelligence means systems substantially better than the best humans across intellectual work. Capabilities instead grow within the human range, pause at the level of top human experts in 2035, and resume toward superintelligence in 2040.

The deal rests on verification, meaning evidence that each side is keeping its promises. AI compute, the chips and data centers supplying processing power, would be declared and monitored through chip tracking, site inspections, and transparency around the most capable AI research. The scenario also projects roughly 50% growth in inflation-adjusted economic output in 2032 as labor income and its tax base shrink relative to output. Permits on AI chips and robots would finance a Compute Dividend Corporation, an entity with one share per citizen, paying a projected Citizen's Dividend of $45,000 per person in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A combines a staged slowdown, inspectable limits on AI infrastructure, and a public dividend for an economy where labor supplies less income and tax revenue.</p></div>

## The economic resemblance is hard to miss

The Correction matches Plan A's warning about the labor-tax base. The Void is the gap Plan A tries to bridge with a dividend. The Alignment matches its choice between broadly shared benefits and concentrated power. Both works use the name “Citizen's Dividend” and point to Alaska as a practical precedent.

My book puts the demand problem plainly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” If machines produce more while households lose wages, greater capacity does not automatically create customers. Distribution is therefore part of the productive system, not charity added afterwards.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The strongest overlap is causal: automation can weaken wages, taxes, and demand, so citizens need a direct claim on the output of AI infrastructure.</p></div>

## Their disagreement matters just as much

Plan A places more weight on verification: track the chips, inspect the data centers, and make the most capable AI research visible enough for an international agreement to hold. An appendix models a covert Chinese project using smuggled chips and gives it less than a 10% chance of reaching AI that outperforms leading human experts without detection before 2043. Including it shows how much the plan depends on seeing what states may prefer to hide.

The Three Crucibles puts more weight on ownership. Who has a legal and economic claim on the machines' output if cooperation frays? Ownership cannot make an unsafe system safe, just as verification cannot decide who receives the gains. A credible settlement needs both layers.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A tries to make capability growth visible and enforceable. The Crucibles ask who retains a claim on the resulting wealth; neither answer is sufficient alone.</p></div>

## Preparation is the point

No one knows what waits between here and 2040. Plan A may be wrong about the pace, the 2029 agreement, or the scale of growth. My book may be wrong about the sequence. Yet both suggest a bumpy road because technical capability can move faster than tax systems, political agreements, and the social meaning of work.

That is why *The Three Crucibles* exists as a preparation document rather than a promise. If these maps are directionally right, we need verifiable limits, a durable public claim on output, and institutions ready for the difficult space between the old bargain and whatever replaces it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Neither map can tell us what will happen. Together they give us enough warning to prepare for a rough transition in capability, income, ownership, and power.</p></div>
