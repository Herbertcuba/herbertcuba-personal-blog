In 1976, Alaska voters amended the state constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenue would enter the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a publicly owned fund that invests the money for long-term benefit. The related Permanent Fund Dividend began in 1982 as an annual cash payment to eligible residents. Its first payment came from surplus oil revenue, while fund earnings supported later payments.

That choice answered a philosophical question through institutional design: who should hold a claim on wealth created from a scarce resource? Fifty years later, the same question appears in [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), a 2026 scenario and policy proposal from the AI Futures Project, a US nonprofit studying advanced AI and policy. It also sits inside my book, [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf).

The current book edition names three crucibles. **The Correction** begins when machine productivity breaks assumptions that tie income and public revenue to human labor. **The Void** is the unstable interval after that break, before trusted replacements are ready. **The Alignment** is the settlement that follows: shared stewardship of AI-created abundance, or concentrated control. The framework was in writing by February 3, 2026, more than six months before this article, and the book was public on my site by March 23. Plan A arrived on July 9.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows that ownership can be designed before the value of a new resource is known. Both AI maps return to that choice.</p></div>

## AI 2040 is a plan, not a prophecy

Plan A has six authors, four of whom also wrote *AI 2027*, the project's 2025 scenario about a rapid race toward advanced AI. Plan A describes itself as “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Its dates are scenario projections, not appointments with history.

In its preferred path, the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029, near-automated AI research follows around 2030, and development pauses at systems that outperform top human experts in 2035. The pause ends in 2040 with a move toward superintelligence, meaning AI that exceeds human ability across almost all useful cognitive work. By mid-2031, the scenario assigns a third of cognitive labor to AI; in 2032, it projects roughly 50 percent real economic growth alongside a collapsing wage-based tax base.

Plan A treats **compute**, the processing capacity supplied by advanced chips and datacenters, as the bottleneck governments can govern. It proposes caps, chip tracking, inspections, and paid permits that grant rights to build or use capacity under the cap. Part of the permit revenue would flow to a Compute Dividend Corporation in which every US citizen owns one share. Its allocation rises from 25 percent of permit fees in 2032 to 75 percent in 2035, so Plan A contains an ownership policy as well as a safety regime.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A combines a managed slowdown in advanced AI with a public claim on the revenue created by scarce computing capacity.</p></div>

## Where the two maps meet

The Correction maps to Plan A's warning that economic output can rise while taxes on wages and payroll shrink. The Void maps to the difficult years when that old link is failing but new income arrangements remain unsettled. Both documents call one proposed bridge a **Citizen's Dividend**, a direct payment that gives people a share of AI-created value, and both point to Alaska as the precedent.

The funding mechanisms differ. *The Three Crucibles* proposes taxing AI labor and spreading ownership of AI infrastructure; Plan A relies on compute-permit fees and equal shares in its dividend corporation. Their Alignment questions still converge: who owns productive capacity, who can verify its use, and who receives the returns?

This creates a testable forecast. By 2030, watch whether serious policy moves upstream from retraining displaced workers to licensing, taxing, or sharing ownership in AI infrastructure. For a Nordic country, the transfer test is simple: can a strong welfare system keep distributing value if the underlying AI assets and tax base sit elsewhere? If ownership remains outside the debate, the shared map becomes less convincing.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The strongest overlap is a predicted shift from repairing lost wages to assigning public claims on the infrastructure producing the new wealth.</p></div>

## Where agreement stops

Plan A puts far more weight on international verification: tracked chips, inspected datacenters, research transparency, and enforceable limits. Its own alternate covert-project scenario hides a Chinese datacenter in tunnels at the Medog Hydropower Station construction site, showing how difficult verification could become. My book does not solve that technical control problem. Its focus is the social contract while that problem remains open.

I do not know whether either timeline will hold. Physical limits may slow progress, politics may reject the proposed controls, and the economic transition may take another shape. Similarity between two maps proves no destination.

Yet if Plan A and *The Three Crucibles* are directionally right, the road ahead is bumpy because productive systems can change faster than the institutions that connect wealth to legitimacy. That is why my book exists as a preparation document. Alaska's useful lesson is modest: a society can decide who holds the claim before it knows how large the windfall will become.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Nobody knows what awaits, but uncertainty is precisely why ownership, verification, and distribution need attention before the transition forces the issue.</p></div>
