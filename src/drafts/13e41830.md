In 1976, Alaska voters faced a question hidden inside an oil boom: what should remain after a temporary resource is gone? They changed the state constitution and directed part of its mineral revenue into the Alaska Permanent Fund, a public investment fund designed to preserve that wealth. Since 1982, the fund's earnings have helped finance annual cash payments to eligible residents.

The deeper choice was about ownership. Alaska did not merely spend a windfall; it gave citizens a lasting claim on some of the value created from a shared resource.

Fifty years later, two documents about artificial intelligence return to that idea. [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a July 2026 policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, the nonprofit research group behind *AI 2027*, its 2025 scenario about rapid AI progress. [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my speculative book about the economic and social strain around AI; a public version was available on my site by March 23, 2026, before Plan A appeared.

That overlap does not prove either map is right. It does suggest that two different ways of thinking about the future have found the same fault lines.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska turned temporary resource wealth into a lasting citizen claim. Both AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles use that precedent to ask who should benefit from AI-created wealth.</p></div>

## What Plan A asks us to imagine

Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Its scenario has the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029, disclose more AI research, inspect data centers, and track advanced chips. Those measures are meant to verify compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI systems, so neither side can secretly race far ahead.

The scenario pauses in 2035 at systems as capable as the best human specialists. In 2040, it allows a controlled move toward superintelligence, which the authors define as AI that surpasses the best humans across tasks while working faster and more cheaply. Its appendix tests the arrangement against a covert Chinese project. The authors estimate a below 10 percent chance that it reaches top-expert-dominating AI, their term for systems surpassing top human specialists, without detection before 2043.

The economic story moves just as quickly. As AI systems and robots do more work, wages and labor taxes shrink relative to total production. Plan A therefore proposes tradable permits to build robots or compute, with most permit fees flowing into a citizen dividend. The aim is to keep purchasing power moving even when paid human work carries less of the economy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A imagines technical progress being slowed, measured, and verified while a permit-funded dividend helps society absorb the economic shock.</p></div>

## The three crucibles, in plain language

My book compresses the transition into three crucibles. They are stages of pressure rather than dates that arrive neatly one after another.

**The Correction** is the moment markets begin valuing machine output above much paid cognitive work. Production can rise while income shifts toward the owners of AI systems and infrastructure, weakening the link between employment and economic security.

**The Void** is the unstable gap after that old bargain starts breaking and before a replacement works. A citizen dividend or basic income matters here as economic circulation: people still need purchasing power for abundant production to have customers.

**The Alignment** is the political and moral settlement that follows. Society either spreads ownership and the benefits of AI infrastructure, or allows productive power to harden around a small group of owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction breaks the old labor bargain, the Void exposes the missing distribution system, and the Alignment decides whether AI abundance is shared or concentrated.</p></div>

## Agreement reveals the missing pieces

The strongest convergence is economic. Both accounts foresee rising output putting pressure on wages, consumer demand, and a tax system built around human labor. Both arrive at a citizen dividend, use the same name, and point to Alaska as evidence that a shared claim on productive wealth is institutionally possible.

Their funding mechanisms differ. Plan A prices scarce production capacity through permits to build robots or compute. My book considers AI labor taxation alongside distributed ownership and a basic income. The common idea is more important than the instrument: if machines produce a growing share of value, citizens need a claim that does not depend entirely on selling their labor.

The larger difference is where each document places its confidence. Plan A concentrates on control through transparency, inspections, chip tracking, and international verification. *The Three Crucibles* concentrates on ownership and distribution. One asks how dangerous capacity can remain visible and constrained; the other asks who benefits even when that capacity behaves as intended.

Convergence is still not confirmation. Plan A is a constructed scenario, not new economic data, and its timetable could be wrong. Yet the gaps are useful because each document makes the other's blind spot easier to see: controlled intelligence can still produce a brutal economy, while shared ownership offers little protection from intelligence that escapes control.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two maps share an economic diagnosis but emphasize different safeguards. A workable future needs both control over powerful AI and a credible claim for citizens on the value it creates.</p></div>

## Preparation without prophecy

I do not know whether AI development will follow Plan A's dates, or whether the three crucibles will unfold in sequence. Nobody does. Forecasts become dangerous when certainty is their product, but they remain useful when they expose decisions that will be expensive to make late.

That is the purpose of my book: a preparation document for a transition that may be uneven, political, and deeply human. If Plan A and *The Three Crucibles* are even roughly right, the road ahead will be bumpy. Preparation will not make the future predictable, but it can keep surprise from becoming paralysis.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>No one knows the exact path to 2040. The shared warning is enough to justify preparing now for economic disruption, concentrated power, and difficult choices about control and ownership.</p></div>
