In 1976, Alaska’s voters faced a question created by oil: how could a temporary resource leave a lasting public benefit? They created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a state-owned investment fund that receives a defined share of mineral revenue. The money is invested, and since the first payment in 1982, eligible residents have received an annual cash dividend from the fund’s earnings.

That old arrangement appears in two new maps of an AI economy. [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) uses Alaska as the model for a much larger citizen dividend. [My 2026 speculative book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), reaches the same mechanism from a different direction. The overlap does not prove that either future will happen, but it makes the shared question harder to dismiss: if machines produce more of the value, what gives people a claim on it?

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Alaska turned resource wealth into a durable public claim. Both AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles argue that advanced AI may require a similar settlement at far greater scale.</p>
</div>

## What AI 2040 asks us to imagine

The [AI Futures Project](https://ai-2040.com/about) is a research nonprofit that builds scenarios about advanced AI. Four of Plan A’s six authors also worked on [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), their 2025 scenario about rapid AI progress and catastrophic loss of control. Plan A, published on July 9, 2026, calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Its preferred path begins with a US-China agreement in 2029 and near-automated AI research in 2030. That means AI systems doing most of the work required to improve later AI systems, creating the feedback loop often called technical takeoff.

The scenario then pauses around top-human-expert capability in 2035 and proceeds to superintelligence in 2040, meaning systems better, faster and cheaper than the best humans across essentially all tasks. To control that passage, Plan A proposes declarations of chip purchases, inspections of data centers and broad research transparency. It also proposes tradable permits to build robots or compute, the processing power supplied by chips and data centers. In its economic scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, inflation-adjusted output grows by roughly 50 percent in 2032, and the wage-based tax system loses ground. Permit revenue then helps fund a citizen dividend.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A couples a fast AI takeoff with international verification, limits on compute and robots, and a dividend meant to preserve broad participation in an economy no longer organized around human labor.</p>
</div>

## The three crucibles beside Plan A

My book presents three transitions before asking what to do about them. **The Correction** is the economic shock: AI makes productive capital far more capable, so wages and employment no longer carry the same share of income. **The Void** is the gap that follows, when the old link between work, income and social purpose weakens before a replacement is ready. **The Alignment** is the political choice at the far side of that gap: whether machine abundance is broadly owned or concentrated in a few hands.

Plan A follows a remarkably similar sequence. Its collapsing labor-tax base resembles the Correction. Its chapter “2033: The Citizen’s Dividend” answers the Void with the same phrase and the same Alaska precedent used in my book. Its fear of irreversible concentration meets the Alignment’s choice between shared abundance and a new feudal order. The term “citizen dividend” is older than either work, so this is not a novelty claim. The meaningful convergence is the sequence and mechanism: my book was public on March 23, 2026, more than three months before Plan A appeared.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The two maps independently trace the same movement from labor disruption to income replacement and then to a struggle over who controls the productive system.</p>
</div>

## The difference is where each map places its trust

Plan A treats coordination and verification as the main safeguards. Verification means making compliance checkable rather than relying on promises. Yet its own appendix imagines a covert Chinese data center at the remote Medog hydropower project and gives it below a 10 percent chance of reaching AI that dominates top human experts without detection before 2043. The probability is low, but the example admits the weakness: a global agreement is only as strong as the world’s ability to see what is being built.

The Three Crucibles puts more weight on ownership. A perfect chip ledger would still leave the distribution question open, while widely shared ownership would not expose a hidden data center. So the approaches are complementary, and both have failure modes. I do not know whether international verification can hold or whether ownership can be spread fast enough. Nobody does.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A is strongest on controlling dangerous capacity; The Three Crucibles is strongest on who retains a claim on its output. A workable settlement may need both.</p>
</div>

## Preparation without prophecy

Plan A’s dates may be wrong. The economic break could arrive more slowly, and international controls could prove stronger or weaker than its authors expect. But the central sequence does not require superintelligence on schedule. Production can begin separating from wages before governments have decided how to replace lost revenue or give citizens a durable stake.

No one knows what waits ahead. If Plan A and the crucibles are even directionally right, however, the road will be bumpy because technology can move faster than the institutions around it. My book exists for that reason: as a preparation document for choices about what can be verified, where public revenue will come from, and who will own the output while those choices are still open.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Nobody can promise this timeline. The useful response is to prepare early for the verification, revenue and ownership questions that both maps place in our path.</p>
</div>
