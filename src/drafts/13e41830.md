One of Alaska's most consequential oil decisions came before the money arrived. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was being built to carry crude from Prudhoe Bay on Alaska's North Slope, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/). It would save part of the state's mineral income, and the first resident dividend followed in 1982. Alaskans secured a public claim while the terms were still open.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a policy recommendation told through a future scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit focused on advanced AI. Four of the five authors of [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's earlier scenario about rapidly advancing AI, also worked on Plan A. The authors are explicit that Plan A is primarily a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast.

My book [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) was [published on March 22, 2026](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GTK53C9H). A [public link to Plan A](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) appeared on July 9. That chronology establishes a public sequence, not influence, independence, or accuracy. I think the more useful claim is that two speculative maps reach a remarkably similar economic fork, then split over what control actually requires.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A does not validate The Three Crucibles as a forecast. It strengthens a specific economic diagnosis through thematic convergence, while the differences between the two works expose separate problems of verification, ownership, and practical freedom to leave.</p></div>

## The economic sequence converges

The public book page names the three crucibles as the Correction, the Transformation, and the Alignment. The Correction is a financial reckoning in which inflated AI expectations collide with uneven returns, weaker companies disappear, and the survivors face stronger pressure to automate. Plan A begins somewhere else, with geopolitical rivalry and a possible race to systems beyond human ability. Yet its scenario reaches a related labor shock: by mid-2031, AI performs a third of all cognitive labor, meaning work based mainly on analysis, information, communication, and judgment.

That shift creates a fiscal and economic problem even if many people find new work. In [Plan A's 2032 economy](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17), fewer paid human roles weaken income and payroll-tax receipts. At the same time, companies reinvest heavily in data centers, factories, and robots, then deduct or depreciate those costs, so corporate tax does not automatically replace the lost revenue. Production can rise while public revenue and household demand fall. *The Three Crucibles* states the demand problem more bluntly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.”

The Transformation asks what can replace wages as both household income and fuel for demand. The book proposes a Citizen's Dividend, a direct public claim on the productivity of AI infrastructure. Plan A uses the same name and the same Alaska precedent. Its scenario sells tradable permits to build robots or computing capacity, meaning the chips and facilities used to train and run AI. A fixed share of compute-permit fees belongs to a Compute Dividend Corporation; every US citizen receives one share, and profits are divided equally. The scenario projects $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and roughly $1 million in 2035, measured in real 2025 dollars.

The funding methods differ. *The Three Crucibles* proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax based on the taxes and social contributions a person in an equivalent role would have generated. Plan A charges for permission to add physical capacity. I think the overlap matters because both works connect automated labor to a broken income loop and a public claim on machine-generated wealth. Their agreement is structural. Plan A does not contain the book's market-correction mechanism, and the shared label proves neither timeline nor ownership of the phrase.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Correction and Transformation meet Plan A in the same economic chain: automated labor can raise output while weakening wages, tax receipts, and demand. Both works answer with a Citizen's Dividend, although one would tax equivalent AI labor and the other would sell permits for new capacity.</p></div>

## Verification is not ownership

The Alignment is the book's political test: whether owners of AI infrastructure become “stewards of abundance” or “the new feudal lords.” Plan A shares the fear of concentrated power, but its central lever is takeoff governance. Takeoff is the period when AI automates enough AI research to speed up its own improvement, potentially turning a technology race into a much faster and harder-to-control process.

In [Plan A's preferred path](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=4), the United States and China make a deal in 2029 that prevents fully automated AI research from producing superintelligence by the end of 2030. Superintelligence means systems that outperform the best humans across fields while working faster and more cheaply. Development continues within the human range until a 2035 pause at top-human-expert capability, then resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. These are conditional events in the scenario, not observed milestones.

The deal depends on verification: evidence that countries are following agreed limits. Major chip and data-center transactions are declared, rival powers inspect infrastructure, and AI research becomes almost entirely transparent. Yet the report's own alternate branch imagines roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents installed in tunnels beside China's Medog Hydropower Station, with only about 200 people aware of the project. An H100-equivalent compares computing power across hardware by using the H100 AI processor as a reference; it is not a literal chip count.

This is where *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film about intelligent machines maintaining a simulated reality, becomes a useful metaphor. Its green code looks weightless, but the machine world behind it needs industrial hardware and energy. Advanced AI has the same physical shadow. Chips, electricity, cooling systems, and buildings leave traces for inspectors even when software remains hidden.

Ownership asks a different question. Plan A gives each citizen a legal share in the dividend corporation, not in the chips, models, or companies producing the value. *The Three Crucibles* argues for distributed AI ownership through infrastructure held by individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, alongside models that can run locally or on an owner's premises. I think the distinction is fundamental: verification tests compliance, while ownership determines who can use capacity, claim its output, and bargain over access. I don't know which balance can survive geopolitical pressure. A system can be visible to inspectors while a small group still sets its terms.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses verification to constrain hidden computing capacity, while Alignment asks who controls visible infrastructure and receives its gains. Inspectability can expose rule-breaking, but ownership determines who can use the system and bargain over its output.</p></div>

## Control becomes real at the exit test

The comparison leaves one more boundary: whether an institution can change suppliers without losing the process that now carries out its work. *The Three Crucibles* imagines one insurer retaining 5,000 claims adjusters while a rival deploys coordinated AI agents, software systems that divide and execute the claims process. The rival cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. This is a thought experiment, not a reported case, but it shows why adoption pressure can move faster than a buyer's ability to leave.

My take is that every critical AI contract should require exit proof before renewal: a supplier-funded handover test showing that a replacement team can run one working process. A useful first scope is one claims workflow, one portable data extract, one replacement team, and a 30-day test. The chief information officer should own the renewal decision, while the buyer owns the past cases and expected outcomes used to evaluate the replacement.

The handover package should include versioned workflow settings, model instructions, tool connections, permissions, source records, restoration guidance, and data lineage, which records where each input came from and how it changed. The replacement may use another model, rewrite the instructions, change how relevant records are supplied to the model, or redesign the workflow. What must remain stable is the agreed business result on the buyer's evaluation cases, within fixed limits for error, time, and cost.

Failure needs a contractual meaning. Missing records, blocked access, results outside the agreed limits, or decisions that cannot be traced to their sources should fail the test. Failure should stop automatic renewal and trigger funded transition assistance; a failed retest should let the buyer leave without a termination fee. I think the leverage exists before the contract is signed.

Regulation covers a related boundary. Since August 2, 2025, the [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law, has applied rules for general-purpose AI models. These are models able to competently perform a wide range of distinct tasks and be integrated into downstream systems. Most of the law began applying on August 2, 2026, including transparency duties for specified AI interactions and AI-generated or manipulated content. Those duties can improve visibility, but they do not prove that a buyer can transfer a working process to another supplier.

I think this is the second-order failure both maps leave exposed. Governments may verify computing capacity and citizens may receive part of its return, yet a hospital, insurer, or public agency can remain trapped inside the systems carrying out its work. Alaska secured a public claim before oil income became routine. AI control will remain borrowed until institutions can inspect the capacity, share in its value, and leave without surrendering the work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof turns supplier choice into a funded acceptance test with a named owner, a buyer-owned evaluation set, a deadline, and contractual consequences for failure. Verification governs hidden capacity, ownership governs economic claims, and exit proof preserves the ability to move a working process.</p></div>
