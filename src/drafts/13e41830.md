In 1976, Alaska voters faced a problem that had little to do with artificial intelligence. The state's mineral wealth could produce a large windfall, but the resource would eventually run out. They amended the state constitution to place at least 25 percent of certain mineral revenues into income-producing investments.

That institution became the Alaska Permanent Fund. It turns part of a temporary resource windfall into a lasting public asset, and residents received the first dividend in 1982. The important choice came early: Alaska created a public claim on the wealth before it disappeared into ordinary spending.

The July 2026 scenario and policy proposal [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/), published by the AI Futures Project forecasting group, and my speculative book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), both return to that precedent. My framework has been public since March 23, 2026. The overlap does not confirm either future, but it exposes the same unresolved problem from two directions.

## What Plan A asks us to imagine

The AI Futures Project also produced [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its April 2025 scenario about rapid progress toward AI systems that outperform humans across intellectual work. Plan A calls itself primarily a recommendation rather than a prediction.

Its sequence begins with a U.S.–China agreement in 2029. AI research is nearly automated by 2030, meaning AI systems do most of the work of improving their successors. The plan limits capability while safety systems develop, pauses at AI that outperforms the best human specialists in 2035, then permits a move toward superintelligence in 2040. Superintelligence means systems far better than the best humans across intellectual work.

In the scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor, or knowledge work, by mid-2031, and inflation-adjusted economic output grows by roughly 50 percent in 2032. Wage-based taxes then shrink, so governments sell permits for robots and computing capacity: the chips and data centers used to build and run AI. Revenue supports a Compute Dividend Corporation in which every citizen owns one share.

Managing the race requires countries to declare computing capacity, track chips, inspect data centers, share research, and provide verification, meaning evidence that each side is keeping its promises. Plan A combines technical control with a way to spread some of the wealth.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A imagines fast progress toward superintelligence, managed through international verification and a citizen-owned dividend funded by permits on scarce AI infrastructure.</p>

## The three crucibles in plain language

The Three Crucibles organizes the transition into three social and economic phases. Here is the whole map before the comparison.

**The Correction.** Cheap machine cognition shifts economic value from human labor toward the capital that owns and runs AI. Jobs, wages, and wage-based taxes come under pressure before institutions adjust.

**The Void.** Economic output can recover while employment does not. This unstable interval needs another way to maintain purchasing power, including a citizen dividend funded by AI-driven production.

**The Alignment.** Once machines create abundance, the central question becomes political: who owns them, and who receives what they produce? That power can be shared broadly or allowed to concentrate.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Correction breaks the old link between labor and value, the Void separates growth from jobs, and the Alignment decides who owns and shares machine-made abundance.</p>
</div>

## Where the maps meet and part

Plan A's automated knowledge work and shrinking wage-tax base resemble the Correction and the Void. Both also face a demand problem: production can rise, but an economy still needs people with money to buy what it produces.

Their answer carries the same name and precedent. The Three Crucibles proposes a Citizen's Dividend supported by taxes on AI labor. Plan A's 2033 chapter proposes the Citizen's Dividend through permits on robots and chips, with one share per citizen in its corporation. Both point back to Alaska, although the funding differs.

The Alignment also resembles Plan A's choice between broad benefit and concentrated power. Yet the strongest safeguard differs. Plan A centers on verification between states and AI projects. An appendix tests that approach with a hypothetical covert Chinese project and puts the chance of reaching top-expert-dominating AI, a system better than the best human specialists, before 2043 without detection below 10 percent. My framework centers on who keeps a lasting claim on output.

These safeguards answer different failures. A citizen share cannot stop an uncontrolled AI system, while chip inspections do not put purchasing power into the hands of someone whose job has disappeared. Serious preparation requires both questions.

## Preparation is the point

Plan A may be wrong about its dates, international agreement, and explosive growth. My book may be wrong about the order or depth of its phases. Nobody knows what waits for us.

If Plan A and I are directionally right, however, the road ahead is likely to be bumpy. Capability may move faster than governance, while production moves faster than distribution. My book exists for this reason: as a preparation document for choices that become harder once a crisis makes them urgent.

Alaska addressed part of its ownership question while the resource windfall was still arriving. AI offers no guarantee of the same timing, but the questions are already visible. That is enough reason to prepare.
