In 1976, Alaska voters made a long-term decision about mineral wealth. They amended the state constitution so that at least 25 percent of certain mineral revenue would enter the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a state-owned investment fund. The first resident dividend came in 1982 from surplus oil revenue. The continuing [dividend program](https://pfd.alaska.gov/Division-Info/About-Us) is now supported by investment earnings connected to mineral royalties.

In 2026, fifty years later, that arrangement appears in two works about artificial intelligence. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) applies the Alaska model to AI infrastructure. [My 2026 speculative book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), also proposes a Citizen's Dividend: a direct payment that gives people a claim on the productivity of AI systems.

The book divides the transition into three crucibles. **The Correction** is the point where AI weakens the old link between human labor and economic value. **The Transformation** is the period when income, demand, and public revenue have to be rebuilt around that break. **The Alignment** is the political choice that follows: whether broad abundance is stewarded for many people or controlled by a small group.

The framework was in writing by February 3, 2026, and public on my site by March 23. Plan A appeared on July 9, 2026. That chronology does not prove either account right. It does make their shared sequence worth examining.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows how society can turn concentrated resource wealth into a continuing public claim. Both AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles ask whether AI wealth will require a similar choice.</p></div>

## What Plan A asks the world to coordinate

Plan A is a 90-page scenario and policy proposal from the [AI Futures Project](https://www.aifutures.org/about), a US nonprofit researching advanced AI. Four of its six authors also wrote *AI 2027*, the group's 2025 scenario about a rapid AI race. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” which is the right way to read its dates.

In its preferred path, a US-China agreement in 2029 prevents AI systems doing nearly all AI research from rushing straight into superintelligence in 2030. That means AI that broadly exceeds human cognitive ability. Development continues under shared limits, pauses around top-human-expert ability in 2035, and moves toward superintelligence in 2040.

The mechanism is control of **compute**, the processing power used to train and run AI. Plan A proposes tracked chips, inspected datacenters, research disclosure, and **safety cases**, structured evidence that a powerful system can be deployed within an agreed level of risk. Permits for advanced chips and robots would also raise public revenue, which a Compute Dividend Corporation would return equally to citizens.

The scenario projects AI performing a third of cognitive labor, meaning paid mental tasks, by mid-2031 and roughly 50 percent growth in inflation-adjusted economic output in 2032. It also gives a $45,000 dividend per person that year. These are conditional results, not observations. They depend on fast deployment, enforceable limits, and extraordinary productivity growth.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A tries to slow the most dangerous capability jump while using scarce permissions for chips and robots to fund a citizen dividend.</p></div>

## The same passage, with different safeguards

The strongest convergence lies in the order of events rather than a date or a catchy phrase. In the Correction, automated production reduces the need for paid human work. Plan A reaches the same economic problem when wage-based tax revenue falls relative to output. In the Transformation, my book reconnects production and demand through a Citizen's Dividend; Plan A uses the same term and the same Alaska precedent.

Both accounts then reach the Alignment. Plan A seeks to prevent advanced capability from concentrating in one state or company, while my book asks who has a durable economic claim on the output. I am not claiming ownership of the dividend idea. The meaningful overlap is that both works place distribution between labor disruption and the final struggle over power.

Their main difference is where they put trust. Plan A leans on verification: count chips, inspect facilities, disclose research, and make international limits visible. Its own covert-project appendix shows the difficulty, estimating below a 10 percent chance that a hidden effort could reach “Top-Expert-Dominating AI,” meaning systems able to outperform the best specialists, while remaining undetected until 2043.

My book leans harder on ownership and taxation. That cannot solve technical safety, and inspection alone cannot decide who receives the wealth. Plan A centers its bargain on the US and China, although the distribution problem crosses their borders. If compute moves internationally or limits fail to bind, permit revenue also weakens. Each work covers a different part of the institutional problem.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works agree on disruption, distribution, and a political fork. Plan A prioritizes verifiable control of capability; The Three Crucibles prioritizes who owns the resulting productivity.</p></div>

## Preparation without prophecy

Plan A's schedule remains uncertain. Physical constraints may slow deployment, international coordination may fail, and other economic arrangements may emerge. We can still watch the nearer mechanism: whether output rises while wage income, labor-tax revenue, and household purchasing power fall behind.

That is why my book exists as a preparation document rather than a timestamped prophecy. Preparation means deciding how a public claim on AI productivity could work, who would enforce it across borders, and what evidence would tell us the Correction has begun. Ordinary automation can create that pressure before superintelligence.

No one knows what waits on the way to 2040. But if Plan A and The Three Crucibles are directionally right, the road will be bumpy: productive capacity will move faster than the institutions that distribute its benefits. We still have time to work on those institutions, but uncertainty is no reason to leave them undesigned.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Neither account can predict the future. Their shared warning is useful because the institutions for ownership, income, and control must be prepared before the pressure arrives.</p></div>
