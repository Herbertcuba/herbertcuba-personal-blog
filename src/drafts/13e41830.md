In 1976, Alaska's voters made a decision about wealth before it fully arrived. They created the Alaska Permanent Fund, a public investment fund that saves part of the state's revenue from oil, a resource that will eventually run out. Since 1982, eligible residents have received an annual cash dividend from the fund's earnings.

The timing matters. Alaska gave citizens a claim on a shared source of wealth before the windfall became normal. Both [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) and [my book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), use that example to ask who should benefit if machines become a new productive base.

The manuscript of *The Three Crucibles* dates to December 2025, and a downloadable PDF was added to my site in March 2026. AI 2040 became publicly available in July. That sequence cannot prove intellectual independence, but it shows that the framework was already in place.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska assigned citizens a share in oil wealth early. AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles use the same precedent to ask who should hold a claim on AI-driven production.</p></div>

## What Plan A asks us to imagine

[AI 2040](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 90-page policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, a US research nonprofit. Four of the five authors of *AI 2027*, the group's April 2025 scenario about rapid AI development, also worked on Plan A. The new report calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.”

Plan A imagines a US–China agreement in 2029 that prevents fully automated AI research and development in 2030. That means stopping AI systems from doing most of the work required to improve their successors. Development continues within the range of human ability, pauses in 2035 around the level of leading human experts, then resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. The report defines superintelligence as AI broadly better than the best humans while also faster and cheaper.

By mid-2031, AI performs a third of cognitive labor, or paid mental work, in the scenario, weakening revenue from wages and payroll taxes. Governments respond by capping new compute, the chips and datacenters used to train and run AI, along with robot capacity. They sell tradable permits to add more, then direct part of the fees to the Compute Dividend Corporation, in which every US citizen receives one share and equal dividends. Inspections, chip tracking, and research transparency are meant to make the limits enforceable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A proposes an international pause, visible limits on AI capacity, and a citizen dividend funded partly by permits for new computing and robotic infrastructure.</p></div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

*The Three Crucibles* describes three pressures rather than three appointments on a calendar.

**The Correction (2026)** breaks the old labor bargain. As productive capacity moves into AI systems and other capital, routine human work loses bargaining power and an economy built around wages begins to strain.

**The Transformation (2027–2032)** rebuilds distribution. The book proposes a Citizen's Dividend, a direct transfer from the productivity of AI infrastructure to the people who still create demand. Machines can produce goods, but they do not become customers.

**The Alignment (2035 onward)** decides who controls the result. Here, alignment means more than making an AI system follow instructions. It asks whether society becomes a broad steward of abundance or lets control harden around a small group of owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction breaks the labor bargain, the Transformation rebuilds income and demand, and the Alignment decides whether the gains are broadly owned or tightly concentrated.</p></div>

## The overlap ends at the hardest question

The shared sequence is hard to miss. Both maps expect AI labor to weaken a tax system tied to human wages. Both answer with a citizen dividend, use Alaska as the precedent, and treat concentrated power as the final danger. Their timelines can be wrong while that causal chain remains useful.

Their methods differ. *The Three Crucibles* emphasizes ownership of AI infrastructure spread across many people, a public income floor, and taxes on AI labor. Plan A starts with limits on scarce capacity, sells permits for more, and shares some of the proceeds. One gives priority to the citizen's claim on production; the other gives priority to the state's ability to measure and constrain production.

Visibility and ownership therefore solve different problems. A chip audit might expose an undeclared datacenter, but it says nothing about whether citizens share in the output of declared facilities. Plan A's appendix tests its system with a hypothetical undeclared Chinese AI project. It puts the chance of that project reaching AI that dominates leading human experts without detection before 2043 below 10 percent. Even if that estimate holds, inspection protects the agreement, not the distribution of lawful gains.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works agree on the economic sequence but guard against different failures: Plan A makes dangerous capacity visible, while The Three Crucibles asks who owns the value produced within the rules.</p></div>

## Preparation without prophecy

No one knows whether AI development will follow either map. The US and China may never sign Plan A's agreement, and progress may slow, accelerate, or meet limits neither document captures.

That uncertainty is the reason to read them together. Their value lies in the sequence: labor institutions can weaken before distribution is rebuilt, and distribution can change without resolving ownership. That is what *The Three Crucibles* is for. It is a preparation document for the difficult middle, not a claim that every date will hold.

If Plan A and *The Three Crucibles* are even directionally right, the road ahead will be bumpy. We cannot see every turn, but waiting for certainty would leave the largest decisions until after the new wealth and power have already found their owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Neither map can know the future, but both suggest that labor, distribution, and ownership will come under pressure in that order. Preparation has to begin before certainty arrives.</p></div>
