In 1976, Alaska struck a deal with itself. The oil under the North Slope belonged to everyone, so when the oil companies came to drill, the state took a cut and put it in a fund. Since 1982, every Alaska resident, every year, receives a dividend check just for existing. Usually somewhere between one and three thousand dollars. No work requirement. No means testing. You live in Alaska, you own a share of the resource, you get paid.

The fund is called the Alaska Permanent Fund, and for fifty years it has been a quiet proof that a society can tax an extractive boom and hand the proceeds directly to its citizens.

I opened with this because in December 2025 I published a book called The Three Crucibles, and one of its core arguments was that AI is the new oil. The models were trained on humanity's collective intellectual output, every book and article and conversation ever digitized. I argued that a slice of compute revenue should flow back to the public as a licensing fee for the species' knowledge. I called it a Compute Dividend, funded partly by a Labor Equivalence Tax: when an AI agent does the work of a salaried employee, the company owes roughly what that employee would have paid in income tax and social contributions.

Last month, the AI Futures Project published AI 2040: Plan A. Same team as AI 2027. Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Brendan Halstead, Eli Lifland, Ryan Greenblatt, Daniel Kokotajlo. Their proposed Citizen's Dividend is legally owned by a "Compute Dividend Corporation," and I am quoting their footnote here: "This is similar to the setup of the Alaska Permanent Fund, except at a much larger scale."

Six months after my book, one of the most rigorous forecasting groups in the world put the same machine on the table. I want to walk through where we converge, where we diverge, and what I think each of us misses.

## What Plan A says

The AI Futures Project built a detailed scenario for how humanity navigates superintelligence without dying or ending up ruled by a handful of CEOs.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A is a recommendation more than a forecast. The authors propose a US-China deal with total research transparency, a pause on frontier training, and verified compute limits, so dozens of companies scale slowly together instead of racing in secret.</p></div>

The mechanics, in brief. In 2029 the US and China agree to avoid a reckless race. In 2030, when AI R&D would otherwise fully automate and trigger an intelligence explosion within the year, the deal kicks in: a training pause, total research transparency, and verification hardware inspectors can trust. Between 2030 and 2035 the world scales inside the human range, up to top-expert-level AI. In 2035 there is a deliberate pause at that level to keep humans in control. In 2040, the world unpauses and scales to superintelligence. Hence the title.

The economics are the part that matters for this article. Under Plan A, robot and compute capacity is capped and allocated through cap-and-trade permits. The permits get expensive, around $200k per robot and $10k per chip, and by 2032 the US government collects roughly $50 trillion a year in permit fees, about ten times current federal revenue. Most of that money funds the Citizen's Dividend: $45,000 per American adult in 2032, climbing to roughly $1 million per person by 2035, as the AI share of economic labor rises from about 20% to about 85%.

The authors are honest about what this is. Plan A calls itself "primarily a recommendation, not a prediction." It is a stress test for their policy proposals, not a claim about what will happen.

## The three crucibles, in short

Before I map my framework onto theirs, let me explain it. The book maps three passages onto the Gartner Hype Cycle.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>First Crucible: the hype bubble bursts and the crash forces companies to replace human cost structures with AI. Second Crucible: the economy recovers but the jobs do not, and society must answer what people are for, through mechanisms like UBI funded by taxing AI labor. Third Crucible: the final fork, where abundance either gets distributed to everyone or concentrates into techno-feudalism.</p></div>

The First Crucible is the Correction. We are at the Peak of Inflated Expectations, and when the bubble bursts, the "Humane Shield" that protects jobs at the cost of efficiency collapses. Companies face a prisoner's dilemma: automate or get crushed by a competitor who does.

The Second Crucible is the Void. The economy recovers, corporate profits climb, and the jobs stay gone. Productivity decouples from labor. The tax base built on payroll starts to die, so the state needs new revenue mechanisms, and the Citizen's Dividend becomes arithmetic rather than generosity. Robots do not buy iPhones. If labor income collapses, so does consumption, so the automated economy must pay people to keep its own circulatory system running.

The Third Crucible is the Alignment, the fork. Path One is abundance for all, with distributed AI ownership and a real dividend. Path Two is techno-feudalism: five corporate ecosystems own the intelligence layer, and the dividend shrinks to a subsistence stipend calibrated to keep people docile. Path Two is the default outcome if nothing forces power to distribute.

## Where we converge

Three mappings, and the third one surprised even me.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A and The Three Crucibles agree on the mechanism for funding people after labor dies, on why the tax base breaks when AI replaces payroll, and on the shape of the endgame: distribute the abundance or lose everything to concentration.</p></div>

First, the dividend. Plan A's Compute Dividend Corporation, one share per citizen, profits paid out equally, is structurally identical to my Compute Dividend. Both of us reached for Alaska. Both of us treat the dividend as a royalty on shared heritage, not welfare.

Second, the tax diagnosis. Plan A funds the state through permits on compute and robots because the old revenue base erodes as AI labor replaces human labor. My Labor Equivalence Tax exists for the same reason: when a company swaps ten salaried humans for agents, income tax and payroll contributions vanish overnight while productivity stays. Different instruments, same hole in the budget. Plan A even caps the economy's growth through permit scarcity, which is a blunter version of the same instinct: the productivity gains must keep funding the society that made them possible.

Third, the fork. Plan A's whole reason to exist is avoiding the AI 2027 ending: takeover or "extreme concentration of power," a tiny group controlling the world's only army of superintelligences. That is my Path Two, described in different vocabulary. Their postscript says the quiet part: "We think the world is asleep at the wheel." Their alternative plans include one, Plan S, where a US president tries a global moratorium because a permanent oligarchy is the realistic failure mode. I wrote the same dystopia as Techno-Feudalism and called it the default outcome of inertia. When a forecasting team this careful independently lands on your worst case, the convergence is worth pausing on.

## Where we diverge

The deepest disagreement is about which variable decides the outcome.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A trusts centralized governance: an international deal, verified by hardware, that slows everyone down together. I think the ownership structure of AI matters more than the speed. A slow race that five companies still own ends in the same feudalism, just later.</p></div>

Plan A is a governance story. Its heroes are diplomats, inspectors, and verification devices. Its bet is that if you can see every training run and cap the compute, you can buy enough time for alignment and institutions to catch up. I think that bet is fragile in two ways.

It assumes the deal holds. Their own scenario admits it almost does not: China attempts a covert AGI project mid-scenario, and the plan survives on verification luck. A framework that depends on every major power behaving, forever, under the largest incentive to defect in human history, is a framework with a single point of failure.

And it leaves ownership mostly untouched. Under Plan A, the frontier companies keep operating inside the permit system. The state skims revenue through permits, yes, but the models, the chips, the datacenters stay in the same few hands. I think that is the load-bearing wall. My book argues the counterweight to Path Two is distributed ownership: open models, local deployments, millions of small operators who own their slice of the intelligence layer. Plan A manages concentration. It does not dissolve it.

Here is the sci-fi comparison that clarifies it for me. In *The Matrix*, the machines built a perfectly managed world. The simulation ran smoothly. The problem was that nobody inside it owned anything, including themselves. A capped, permitted, beautifully governed intelligence economy with five owners is a well-run Matrix. I would rather have a messier world where the compute is spread across millions of hands.

I will admit the asymmetry cuts both ways. Plan A covers what my book treats lightly: verification, covert projects, international enforcement, the actual mechanics of a US-China deal. I wrote about the sociology of the transition and they wrote about its plumbing. I genuinely don't know which failure mode is more likely, a deal that collapses or a concentration that never gets challenged. Maybe both happen at once.

## What this means if you build with these systems

If you are a junior engineer working with LLMs, this debate is not abstract. It decides what your skills are worth and who you work for.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both frameworks agree labor income from routine knowledge work is ending. The safe move is the same under both: own assets instead of selling hours, and build skills that stay valuable whether the future is governed or distributed.</p></div>

Under Plan A's world, the permit economy makes intelligence a regulated utility. The valuable humans are the ones who direct agents toward goals worth pursuing, because execution inside the caps is cheap. Under my framework's Path One, the valuable humans are owners: people with their own models, their own data, their own holding companies. Either way, the losing position is the same one. Selling hours for wages while the wage share of the economy falls from 55% toward Plan A's projected 10%.

The practical move is identical in both futures. Learn to specify outcomes precisely, because agents execute specifications literally. Build things you own: a product, a dataset, a reputation, a workflow encoded as an asset. Treat every hour of wage labor as a way to buy capital, because capital is the only side of the new equation that survives.

I think the most honest sentence in Plan A is its label: a recommendation. The Three Crucibles made the same move from the other direction, a map of the fire with an argument about what to build from the ashes. We named different parts of the same animal. They drew the treaty. I drew the fork. The part nobody has drawn yet is how you get distributed ownership and a working international deal at the same time.

That drawing is the work of the next five years. I intend to keep sketching.
