In 1976, the US state of Alaska put part of its oil wealth into a permanent fund. Payments began in 1982, giving eligible residents a direct claim on a shared resource.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) is a 2026 governance scenario from the AI Futures Project, a forecasting group focused on advanced AI. Four authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), a 2025 rapid-AI scenario about losing human control. Plan A is a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast, and it uses Alaska as the model for a future Citizen's Dividend: a public payment funded by AI-era production.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my book about AI, work, and ownership, reaches the same mechanism. An [author-controlled repository record](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) dates its PDF to March 23, 2026; a [public submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) dates *AI 2040* to July 9. Neither tells us who had the idea first in private. I think the narrower finding matters: independent arguments trace production pulling away from wages, then split over control.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both works use Alaska's public dividend as a model for sharing AI-era wealth. The dates support comparison, not a claim of influence.</p></div>

## The economic break is the real match

The [June 24 edition of *The Three Crucibles*](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment. The Correction begins when AI raises output while reducing paid work. In the Void, production and household income separate; its Transformation chapter proposes a Citizen's Dividend to sustain demand because, as the book puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.” The Alignment asks who owns the productive systems.

Plan A reaches the same break through its scenario numbers. By mid-2031, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, meaning work based mainly on analysis, communication, research, or professional judgment. As paid work shrinks, governments collect less from wages and households lose income. Plan A therefore sells permits for robots and AI computing capacity, the chips and data centers that support AI work, then directs most of the revenue to a Citizen's Dividend projected at $45,000 per adult in 2032 ([PDF pages 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17)).

My book proposes a tax on AI work based on what a person could have done; Plan A uses permits. The phrase “Citizen's Dividend” predates both works. I think the real convergence is the same cause-and-effect chain and the shared Alaska precedent, not a funding formula or date.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction and the Void describe production separating from wages and demand. Plan A models the same break, but the book taxes AI work while Plan A charges for robots and computing capacity.</p></div>

## The maps split over control

Plan A focuses on takeoff, a feedback loop in which AI automates research that improves AI. In its scenario, the United States and China agree in 2029 to slow automated AI research and growth in AI capability, avoiding a route to superintelligence by the end of 2030. That means systems better than humans across almost all intellectual work. The governments pause in 2035 at top-expert capability, roughly the level of leading human specialists on defined tasks, then resume progress in 2040.

The deal depends on verification: required transaction records and physical inspections that reveal undeclared chips and data centers. Plan A's covert-project appendix tests that logic with an imagined Chinese facility near the Medog Hydropower Station, a planned dam in Tibet. Its software may be secret, but the processors still need chips, power, cooling, and people. In *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film, a virtual world hides a physical machine beneath it. Plan A follows the machine back to the software.

The Alignment asks a different question. Inspection can expose productive capacity, but it cannot give citizens a claim on its output. I don't know whether Plan A's international system could survive US-China rivalry, and its authors do not claim certainty. I think its verification logic is serious, yet a perfectly visible concentration of ownership is still a concentration of power.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses records and inspections to expose hidden AI capacity. The Alignment asks who owns that capacity and who receives what it produces.</p></div>

## Ownership reaches the contract desk

The ownership problem appears at company scale when a recurring process moves to an AI supplier. The supplier may hold the usable data, access permissions, system settings, connected tools, and operating instructions. Control then concentrates even if the buyer owns the final documents on paper.

The book's [fictional insurer example](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21) makes the pressure concrete. One insurer keeps 5,000 claims adjusters, the people who investigate claims and decide payments. A rival deploys AI, cuts staff by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. Those are illustrative numbers, not a reported case.

I call the practical test exit proof: a funded, independent handover showing that another team can keep one business process running. The chief information officer, or CIO, should own the renewal decision.

The original contract should make renewal conditional on a supplier-funded 30-day test covering one workflow, one usable data extract, and one replacement team. The buyer owns the evaluation set, a fixed sample of past cases used to judge the result. A replacement may change the AI model or workflow, but it must stay within agreed limits for errors, time, and cost. Missing permissions, unusable data, or failed cases trigger corrections and paid transition support before renewal.

Most of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's main AI law, began applying on August 2, 2026; rules for general-purpose AI models, systems built for many tasks, had applied since August 2, 2025. Its disclosure rules do not test whether a buyer can leave. I think that is the board-level question: can another team keep a critical workflow running without the current supplier? The leverage only exists before the contract is signed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Make renewal depend on a supplier-funded handover of one bounded workflow. If an independent team cannot reproduce the accepted outcome from usable data and operating materials, the buyer does not control the process.</p></div>
