Alaska settled an ownership question before its oil income became routine. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was being built to carry North Slope oil, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/) and reserved part of the US state's mineral revenue. The first resident dividend followed in 1982. The public claim came first.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a policy recommendation told through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit that forecasts advanced AI and proposes ways to navigate it. It follows [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's April 2025 scenario, and four of that work's five authors also contributed to Plan A. The authors explicitly say Plan A is primarily a recommendation rather than their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my speculative book about AI's economic transition, has a [retail publication date of March 22, 2026](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GTK53C9H), and its PDF was public on my site by March 23. A [public link to Plan A appeared on July 9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). That establishes public order, not influence or predictive accuracy.

I think the narrower comparison is more useful. The two works approach the future from different directions, yet converge on an economic break and a public dividend before separating over power. Their disagreement exposes three questions often compressed into *AI governance*:

| Test | Failure it addresses | Evidence required | Decision owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Infrastructure verification | Undeclared large-scale computing capacity | Transaction records and physical inspection | States, regulators, or treaty bodies |
| Economic ownership | AI-generated gains accrue to a narrow group | A legally enforceable claim on output or revenue | Legislatures and the people named in law |
| Supplier exit | A vital process cannot move away from one supplier | A live handover against buyer-owned cases | Accountable business and technology owners |

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A is a conditional policy scenario, while The Three Crucibles is my speculative book. Their thematic overlap does not validate either timeline. It does reveal that inspecting AI infrastructure, allocating its gains, and retaining the ability to leave a supplier are separate tests of control.</p></div>

## The economic break is the real convergence

The public book page, which is authoritative for my taxonomy, names the First Crucible as The Correction, the Second as The Transformation, and the Third as The Alignment. The Correction describes inflated AI expectations meeting uneven returns, followed by company failures and stronger pressure to automate. Plan A has no close equivalent because it begins with geopolitical rivalry and a contest to improve advanced AI. The works start in different places.

They meet in The Transformation. My book imagines output recovering while many jobs do not and states the demand problem plainly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” In Plan A's scenario, AI performs [one third of all cognitive labor by mid-2031](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=13), meaning work based mainly on information, analysis, communication, and judgment. The scenario then projects about 50 percent real economic growth in 2032 even as automation erodes the tax base.

The mechanism matters because production and purchasing power can move in opposite directions. When wages disappear, governments collect less personal-income and payroll tax. Companies can also expense or depreciate spending on data centers, factories, and robots, reducing taxable profit during the build-out. Output may rise quickly while household demand and public revenue weaken.

Both works answer with a Citizen's Dividend, a public payment tied to AI-driven productivity. [My book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37), based on what a person doing equivalent work would have contributed through taxes and social payments. [Plan A proposes tradable permits](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17) instead: the state issues limited permissions to add robot or computing capacity, builders can buy and sell them, and part of the revenue goes to a corporation owned equally by US citizens. In the scenario, the dividend rises from $45,000 per American adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035, measured in 2025 dollars.

The Alignment carries this economic question into politics: will citizens become stewards of abundance, or will a narrow group control the productive system? Plan A also tries to spread the gains rather than allow irreversible concentration. I think the convergence matters because it includes the broken income loop, the Alaska precedent, and the same policy name. Yet “Citizen's Dividend” predates both works, and Plan A's figures remain projections. A later scenario gives my framework a detailed stress test; it does not turn speculation into evidence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The canonical sequence in my book is The Correction, The Transformation, and The Alignment. Plan A has no matching market correction, but both works reach the same income-and-tax problem and propose a Citizen's Dividend. They agree on the economic break while choosing different ways to fund the response.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership govern different failures

Plan A is much stronger on a technical danger that my book largely brackets. Its scenario has the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029. Without that deal, the authors model fully automated AI research and superintelligence by the end of 2030. Under the agreement, development slows, pauses at top-human-expert ability in 2035, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. *Takeoff* is the period when AI automates enough AI research to accelerate its own improvement; *superintelligence* means AI significantly better than the best humans across fields while also working faster and more cheaply. These are scenario dates, not promised events.

That acceleration requires a physical build-out. Plan A projects growth from roughly 20 million H100-equivalents in 2026 to 60 billion in 2034. An H100-equivalent measures AI computing performance against Nvidia's H100 processor, so it is a unit of capacity rather than a literal chip count. To constrain secret capacity, major data-center owners and chip suppliers would report large transactions, rival powers would inspect physical infrastructure, and AI research would become almost entirely transparent.

Plan A stress-tests this design with [an alternate covert-project branch](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45). In this fictional branch, China diverts roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents into tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station, a remote Chinese hydroelectric project whose power and underground setting make it useful as a concealment site in the scenario. About 200 people know. The branch exposes the verification problem from inside Plan A's own argument: records can be false, officials can collude, and physical evidence can arrive too late.

The Alignment in my book asks another question: who owns AI infrastructure and receives its output? Plan A gives citizens shares in the corporation receiving permit revenue, creating an economic claim without giving them direct ownership of chips, models, or AI companies. My proposal distributes infrastructure among individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, including AI models that can run on local hardware.

I don't know which balance could survive geopolitical pressure. I think the disagreement is more informative than a forced match. Plan A tackles secret capacity and loss of control, where my book offers no comparable plan for containing superintelligence. My book goes further on distributed ownership, which inspections cannot create. Detecting a breach does not decide who holds economic power afterward, and broad ownership cannot by itself contain a system more capable than its owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses reporting, inspections, and transparent research to constrain a rush toward superintelligence. The Alignment asks who owns AI infrastructure and its output. Each addresses a gap left by the other because observing concentrated power and distributing economic power are different jobs.</p></div>

## The third test starts before renewal

These state-level choices become immediate when a hospital or insurer renews an AI contract. My book illustrates the pressure with [two fictional insurers](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21). One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. The other uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that carry out parts of the claims process, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The numbers are illustrative, but the mechanism is clear: adoption can outrun a buyer's ability to reverse it.

My take is that exit proof should be mandatory at renewal for a business-critical AI workflow that handles regulated decisions or an operationally essential process. Exit proof is a live handover test showing that a replacement team can run the process to an agreed standard. Low-risk tools may need only a tested data export and documented settings. High-impact workflows need a full handover test with named business and technology owners. The requirement belongs in the contract at signing, while the buyer still has leverage.

A practical sequence is to classify the workflow, contract the test at signing, run it before renewal, and escalate only failed critical workflows. For a first pilot, I would use one workflow, one replacement team, a 30-day window, and supplier reimbursement of test costs up to five percent of the annual contract value; the buyer approves and pays any excess. That is a proposed starting rule, not an industry standard.

The accountable business owner should schedule the test and judge whether business outcomes pass. The technology owner should define the technical baseline and restore the original service if the exercise fails. A cross-functional AI portfolio forum, which reviews AI investment and risk decisions, can settle disputes among procurement, legal, data, risk, the supplier, and the business. If no such forum exists, the buyer should designate an equivalent group before signing.

The acceptance record can stay short:

| Field | Required record |
| --- | --- |
| Inputs | Frozen, buyer-owned test cases |
| Baseline | The current supplier's results on the same cases |
| Grader | The named person or system that evaluates outcomes |
| Limits | Agreed error, completion-time, and cost thresholds |
| Evidence | Source trail, decision log, and retention period |
| Recovery | The owner and deadline for restoring the original service |

The handover package needs configuration and access materials, including versioned workflow settings, model instructions, system connections, and delegated permissions. It also needs source-and-recovery materials, including data lineage, the record of where inputs came from and how they changed. A replacement should fail even if it reaches an acceptable decision when it cannot reconstruct the source trail, exceeds delegated permissions, or leaves the buyer unable to restore service safely. One failed test shows that a workable exit remains unproven, not that migration will always be impossible; it should trigger funded transition help and a retest.

This goes beyond familiar controls. Data portability moves files, source-code escrow releases code under agreed conditions, and ordinary workflow migration moves known components. Exit proof tests the larger business claim: can another team operate the process to the accepted standard without hidden dependence on the current supplier?

The [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law, shows why disclosure is separate. Rules for general-purpose AI models, which can perform many tasks and be integrated into other systems, have applied since August 2, 2025; most of the Act began applying on August 2, 2026. Its documentation and transparency duties do not prove that a buyer can transfer a working process.

I think exit proof also reveals where technical portability ends. A replacement may reproduce the workflow while exclusive data, licensing terms, or scarce computing capacity still let the original supplier dictate the price. The handover can succeed and the buyer can remain economically dependent. That is not a defect in the test. It identifies the next control problem.

Alaska secured a public claim before oil income became routine. We still have the same choice with AI infrastructure: establish ownership claims and tested exit rights while the rules are fluid, or negotiate them after dependence has hardened. The hardware may arrive under either arrangement. The public claim will not appear unless we make it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof is a proposed handover test for critical AI workflows, agreed at signing and run before renewal against buyer-owned cases, measurable limits, traceable sources, and safe restoration. A technically successful handover can still leave the buyer dependent on exclusive data, licensing, or scarce capacity. AI infrastructure may become routine; ownership claims and practical exit rights will not secure themselves.</p></div>
