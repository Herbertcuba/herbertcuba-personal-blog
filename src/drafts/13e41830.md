In 1976, Alaska voters made an unusual decision about mineral wealth. They amended the state constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenues would enter the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/the-fund/), a publicly owned pool whose principal is protected and invested. The first Permanent Fund Dividend, a cash payment to eligible residents, followed in 1982.

That old arrangement now sits inside two maps of an AI future. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) appeared in July 2026. The core framework of [my book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), was in writing by February 3, more than six months before this article, and the book was publicly downloadable by March 23, 2026.

The overlap does not prove that either map is right. It does reveal the same underlying question: if human labor stops being the main route to income, how do we contain machine power and give people a durable claim on what it produces?

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Alaska made public ownership explicit before distributing resource wealth. AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles apply that logic to an economy increasingly powered by machines.</p>

## Plan A imagines a managed acceleration

The [AI Futures Project](https://ai-2040.com/about), a research nonprofit that studies advanced AI and possible policy responses, presents Plan A as “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Four of its six authors also wrote *AI 2027*, the group’s 2025 scenario about a rapid race toward advanced AI.

Plan A’s conditional path includes a United States–China agreement in 2029, monitoring of compute, meaning the processing capacity supplied by advanced chips and data centers, and a pause around top-human-expert AI in 2035. In 2040, the scenario moves to superintelligence: AI that is better, faster, and cheaper than the best humans across essentially every task.

Its economic passage is just as dramatic. By mid-2031, AI performs a third of cognitive labor, or work based mainly on analysis, language, planning, and decisions. Plan A then projects roughly 50 percent real, inflation-adjusted economic growth in 2032. As wage taxes lose ground, permits for compute and robots fund a Citizen’s Dividend modeled on Alaska.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A proposes international controls over advanced computing while machine work erodes the wage-tax base and permit income finances citizens directly.</p>
</div>

## The three crucibles name the social passage

My book divides the transition into three periods of institutional pressure:

- **The Correction (2026):** AI breaks the old bargain in which more productivity usually supports more human employment and wages.
- **The Transformation (2027–2032):** society rebuilds purchasing power through universal basic income, a regular payment not tied to employment, a Citizen’s Dividend, and taxes on AI labor.
- **The Alignment (2035+):** ownership and governance decide whether abundance has broad stewards or a small group of powerful owners.

These are directional periods rather than appointments on a calendar. Their purpose is to make the social sequence visible before events make every choice urgent.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Correction breaks the work-income bargain, the Transformation rebuilds distribution, and the Alignment decides who holds power over abundance.</p>
</div>

## The agreement hides a deeper disagreement

The first match is economic. Plan A gives numbers to the Correction, then places a Citizen’s Dividend in the same structural position as the Transformation. Both works also use Alaska as their practical precedent. I am not claiming the phrase itself; the meaningful overlap is the chain from automated labor to a weaker tax base and then to a public dividend.

The funding methods differ. My book emphasizes taxes on AI labor and output. Plan A sells permits for scarce compute and robot deployment, limiting scale while raising public revenue. Yet permits can improve technical control while favoring companies already able to buy them. A dividend spreads value only when citizens receive a durable legal claim rather than a benefit that can be withdrawn.

The deepest split appears in the Alignment. Plan A trusts declarations of chip transactions, inspections, and international enforcement to keep powerful systems within agreed limits. My map puts more weight on distributed ownership. Either side can fail alone: an inspected AI economy may still concentrate its gains, while broad ownership may spread access to systems that remain dangerous. Plan A’s own appendix keeps that risk alive, assigning an undetected covert project less than a 10 percent chance of reaching AI that dominates top human experts before 2043.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The maps agree on the economic passage but protect against different failures. Verification limits capability; ownership determines who benefits from it.</p>
</div>

## Preparation without certainty

Nobody knows what waits between here and 2040. The useful response is to keep both maps falsifiable rather than turning their overlap into belief.

Plan A offers a checkpoint in mid-2031. I would treat the shared diagnosis as materially weaker if credible studies put AI below 10 percent of cognitive work while both United States labor’s share of national income, the portion paid to workers, and median real wages remain at or above their 2026 levels. That test gives the argument somewhere to fail.

If the signals move the other way, the institutional work cannot begin after the disruption. The purpose of *The Three Crucibles* is preparation: giving names to the passage while choices about control, income, and ownership remain open. No forecast removes uncertainty. If Plan A and my book are even directionally right, we have a bumpy road ahead.
