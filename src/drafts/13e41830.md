[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) lays out a survivable route through superintelligence. Four of its six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/); they frame Plan A as a recommendation rather than their best forecast.

Plan A begins with a US-China race; [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) begins inside firms where automation weakens wages and demand. Both reach the same chain: less labor income, a smaller tax base and a Citizen's Dividend funded by AI infrastructure. The name and Alaska precedent match too.

This overlap makes the timing worth stating carefully. A [March 23, 2026 repository commit](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) records the book PDF in my site's public repository; a [July 9 Hacker News submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) later linked Plan A. The record shows sequence alone. I think the structural evidence is stronger: a detailed safety scenario reaches the economic frame already on the page.

My take is that the disagreement is more useful: Plan A focuses on verifying frontier development, while The Three Crucibles focuses on ownership and returns. Together they expose a practical question: can an institution leave its AI supplier without breaking the service?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both maps connect automation to weaker labor income and a Citizen's Dividend. They split over control of the productive systems.</p></div>

## The economic map lines up

Plan A's scenario turns on a 2029 US-China agreement that slows takeoff, meaning the speed at which capable AI improves. Systems stay within the human range through 2035, pause at top-expert ability and move toward superintelligence in 2040 (p. 4). By mid-2031, AI performs a third of cognitive labor in the scenario (p. 13). Readers can question the calendar while examining its economic chain.

In the scenario, falling labor income erodes labor-tax revenue, so the US auctions tradable permits to build robots or computing capacity. Most fees finance a Citizen's Dividend: $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million by 2035 (pp. 17–18).

The Three Crucibles calls the labor shock the Correction and the demand repair the Transformation: “Robots do not buy iPhones” (p. 35). The Alignment asks whether owners become stewards or feudal lords. The book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax and distributed ownership (pp. 36, 56), while Plan A collects money when firms expand machine capacity. I think the shared diagnosis can hold even if both timelines prove wrong.

Both works point to Alaska's public fund, paying residents since 1982. It makes their idea concrete: when machine output displaces paid work, citizens need a claim on its returns. The open argument is where to collect that return and who owns the asset.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both maps share the labor-income break and dividend response. Plan A prices new machine capacity; The Three Crucibles taxes deployed AI labor and spreads ownership.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership solve different failures

Verification means checking whether declared chips, datacenters and research activity match reality. Plan A has major datacenter owners and chip suppliers declare major transactions, rival powers inspect infrastructure, and frontier research become almost entirely transparent (pp. 7, 11). This addresses a state race toward unsafe systems, a problem that an enterprise contract cannot solve.

Appendix D tests this with a hypothetical branch: China hides capacity equal to 500,000 H100 chips in tunnels near the Medog Hydropower Station, with roughly 200 people aware (pp. 45–47). Like *Andor*, machinery beyond the inspection route supports a project the declared system never sees. This illustrates a possible failure and offers no real-world detection result. Yet the appendix puts the chance of reaching top-expert AI undetected before 2043 below 10 percent, giving verification substantial credit.

That still leaves the economic layer. The Three Crucibles calls for distributed ownership, open infrastructure, open models and legal guardrails against a private intelligence monopoly (p. 56). A secret national program remains a separate problem. Meanwhile, public evidence about chips and research gives an insurer no transfer mechanism for its records, permissions or operating process.

I would add operational reversibility: a tested ability to move a working service to another provider. I don't know which failure becomes urgent first, but I think robust governance needs both levels. AI transparency tells us what we can inspect; exit rights tell us whether we retain agency when a supplier changes the terms.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification limits hidden frontier development, while distributed ownership limits economic concentration. Operational reversibility tests the remaining buyer-level risk: whether a working service can move.</p></div>

## Put the exit test at renewal

The book uses an insurer thought experiment. One company keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; a rival deploys AI agents, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent and wins within six months (p. 20). Claims then depend on records, decision history, model settings, permissions and acceptance rules. Supplier-only formats turn a provider change into a rebuild.

Before renewal, assign a business owner and run one bounded handover drill. Export records and decision history, configuration, permissions, test cases and operating instructions. A replacement team runs representative claims, checks outcomes against agreed criteria, and restores the workflow within a set time and cost. The test fails when the replacement setup cannot reproduce sampled outcomes or meet the recovery target.

Europe gives this test a natural trigger. The [EU AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai) largely applied from August 2, 2026; general-purpose model obligations applied from August 2, 2025. Depending on the use and role, it can require documentation, logging, oversight and risk management. Those records may help, while portable formats, transition support and a recovery commitment belong in the renewal contract.

If the handover succeeds, my claim that transparency can coexist with concentrated supplier power weakens. That makes the exit test falsifiable. I think buyers should demand the evidence before renewal, because agency exists only when the exit works under real conditions.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Before renewal, require a tested handover of records, permissions, evaluation evidence and operating instructions, with a named owner and agreed limits for recovery time and cost.</p></div>
