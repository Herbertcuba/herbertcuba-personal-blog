In [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), two fictional insurers each employ 5,000 claims adjusters. One deploys a swarm of AI agents, cuts its staff by 40 percent and premiums by 20 percent, then wins within six months (p. 20). The move works for one company. Across an economy, the same move erodes the wages people use to buy what machines produce.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) takes that pressure to national scale. Daniel Kokotajlo, Thomas Larsen, Eli Lifland and Romeo Dean also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/). They present Plan A as a recommended path, while saying their best-guess forecast may look different.

I think the comparison exposes three different control problems. Plan A aims to stop a hidden state race, while the book asks who receives the wealth AI creates; I would add an exit proof, a timed handover of one working AI service that shows whether a buyer can change suppliers.

The book PDF entered my [public repository on March 23, 2026](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8), and a [public AI 2040 submission followed on July 9, 2026](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). These dates show only the public sequence; each idea's private origin remains unknown. I use Plan A as an external test of the book's argument.

## The maps meet at the demand problem

In Plan A's scenario, a 2029 US-China agreement blocks an end-of-2030 rush to superintelligence. Systems stay within human capability, pause in 2035 at top-expert AI, meaning they match the best human specialists, and resume toward superintelligence in 2040 (p. 4). By mid-2031, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor (p. 13).

That pace breaks familiar tax math. Plan A says labor taxes shrink as machines do more work, so the United States auctions tradable permits for new robots and computing capacity. Most permit fees fund a Citizen's Dividend, estimated within the scenario at $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035 (pp. 17–18).

The book maps the same demand problem through its three crucibles. The Correction describes labor losing economic value. The Transformation proposes a Citizen's Dividend because “Robots do not buy iPhones” (p. 35). Its AI Labor Equivalence Tax would charge each human-equivalent unit of deployed AI labor, while Plan A charges for new machine capacity. The Alignment asks whether abundance becomes broadly owned or concentrated.

I think this is meaningful convergence because it can be tested before any superintelligence arrives. Labor's share of income, tax receipts and household demand will show whether the chain is forming.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both frameworks connect automated work to weaker labor income and a Citizen's Dividend. Plan A funds it through permits for new machine capacity; the book proposes a charge on deployed AI labor.</p></div>

## The disagreement is about control

Verification means evidence that declared chips, datacenters and research on the strongest models match reality. Plan A proposes declarations for major transactions, reciprocal inspections and near-total research transparency, while people can use finished models privately (pp. 7, 11). It targets state cheating and dangerous training.

Appendix D tests that answer. Its scenario imagines China hiding 500,000 H100-equivalents, a measure of high-end AI computing power, in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station; roughly 200 people know. It is a *Rogue One* problem: knowledge can be divided, yet power and cooling leave traces. Plan A puts the chance of reaching top-expert-dominating AI undetected before 2043 below 10 percent. At that threshold, systems outperform the best human experts (pp. 45–50).

The book places control elsewhere. Its distributed ownership proposal calls for open infrastructure and models, plus legal limits on a private intelligence monopoly (p. 56). I think Plan A gives the stronger answer to a hidden state race, while the book gives the stronger answer to who receives AI-created wealth. A fully inspected system can still concentrate its gains.

I don't know which risk becomes decisive first. My take is that both controls are necessary: verification can expose hidden capacity, while ownership determines who benefits from systems that follow the rules.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses inspection and research transparency to constrain a hidden state race. The book asks who owns productive AI and receives its gains. Solving either problem leaves the other open.</p></div>

## Supplier choice needs a working test

For an insurer, the ownership question becomes concrete at contract renewal. Choose one claims workflow and name its business owner. Agree on a deadline, a cost ceiling, an acceptable error rate and a sample of past decisions. The exit proof passes only when a replacement team restores the workflow within the time and cost limits, then reproduces the sample within that error rate.

The buyer supplies usable data and decision logs, model and prompt versions, quality-test results, system interfaces, permissions and a restore guide. A missing permission or an error rate above the limit fails the test. Supplier choice becomes measurable before a wider migration puts the service at risk.

The exit proof also shows the limit of transparency rules. Since August 2, 2026, [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50) has required disclosures for certain AI interactions and generated content. Those disclosures can reveal AI use. The contract still has to secure usable migration records and transition support.

AI transparency tells us what we can inspect; an exit proof tells us whether an institution retains agency when a supplier changes the terms. I think boards should demand both before renewing a critical service. A buyer that cannot move one working workflow within the agreed deadline, cost ceiling and error rate has supplier choice only on paper.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An exit proof tests one service against a deadline, cost ceiling and acceptable error rate. Until a replacement team passes that test, supplier choice exists only on paper.</p></div>
