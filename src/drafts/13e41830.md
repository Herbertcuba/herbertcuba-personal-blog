Buried inside [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a company that does not exist. Every US citizen would own one share in it, and part of the fees charged for permission to build robots and computing capacity would flow into it. The company turns an AI safety plan into a draft economic constitution.

Plan A is the AI Futures Project's 2026 policy recommendation, told through a conditional scenario rather than as its best guess. The research nonprofit studies advanced AI and published [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/) on April 3, 2025. Four of that scenario's five front-page authors return in Plan A, showing continuity in the program.

The chronology needs care. My site's repository records an initial PDF and download-link change for [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) on March 23, 2026, and a current-PDF update on June 24. A public AI 2040 link appeared July 9. That proves artifact order, not influence, independent discovery, or predictive success. AI 2040 is not data confirming mine.

I think the comparison matters because both works reach the same break between machine production and wage-based income, then divide over the rights that should survive it. Plan A makes computing capacity and system danger open to checking. My book asks who owns the productive assets and income streams. Organizations face a third test: can they leave an AI supplier?

## The economic break is the real convergence

The current PDF's opening map names The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. Separate chapters called The Void and The Transformation develop the Second Crucible, while the landing page uses The Transformation as the middle label. The sequence moves from an AI market correction to automated value without enough work or demand, then to ownership and distribution.

Plan A reaches it differently. In its scenario, AI performs one third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, meaning work based mainly on reasoning, analysis, communication, and judgment. It models roughly 50 percent inflation-adjusted economic growth in 2032 while the tax base shrinks as a share of the economy. Employment-linked receipts fall, while firms expense or depreciate datacenters, factories, and robots, reducing taxable income and current corporate-tax liability. Output rises as the channels funding households and government weaken.

The remedies expose the convergence. Plan A sells capacity permits, paid and tradable legal permissions to build limited robot or computing infrastructure. Its scenario projects $50 trillion in US federal permit revenue in fiscal 2032 and sends most American permit fees to a Citizen's Dividend for adults. The modeled payment rises from $45,000 per person in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035, in 2025 dollars. These are scenario outputs, not observed results.

[The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) also proposes a Citizen's Dividend and an AI Labor Equivalence Tax: a charge for each human-equivalent unit of AI work, based on what a person in that role would have generated for public revenue. Its demand-side explanation is blunt: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Machine abundance can grow while its customers disappear.

Both works point to the Alaska Permanent Fund, the state investment fund created by a 1976 constitutional amendment to invest part of Alaska's mineral revenue and support resident dividends, first paid in 1982. I think the echo is causal. Each treats productive capacity as public income because wages may no longer sustain demand or public revenue.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Both works describe output growing while wages, demand, and employment-linked taxes weaken. Plan A charges for scarce capacity; The Three Crucibles charges for human-equivalent AI work. Each reconnects production with purchasing power through a citizen dividend.</p>

## Verification and ownership govern different failures

Plan A's technical story begins with takeoff, a feedback loop in which AI automates AI research and speeds its own improvement. In the scenario, the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029, avoid a 2030 route to superintelligence, pause at top-human-expert capability in 2035, and resume in 2040. Superintelligence means AI significantly better than the best humans across all tasks while also faster and cheaper.

The restraint depends on compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI. Major chip transactions would be declared, datacenters inspected, and leading research broadly disclosed. Developers would submit safety cases, written arguments that a system will not cause irreversible catastrophe, open to public and expert criticism. I think Plan A's strongest contribution is making restraint a problem of evidence rather than trust.

Its fictional covert-project branch stress-tests that evidence. China hides processors in a tunnel datacenter beside the unfinished Medog Hydropower Station project and falsifies records. Industrial-scale software still collides with chip supply, construction, power, and cooling. Verification can fail, but secret capacity leaves more than a secret file.

Ownership answers another failure. Plan A's Compute Dividend Corporation would own a fixed share of permit fees, with one equal share for every US citizen. I think that is stronger than a discretionary payment, yet it remains a claim on a centrally managed fee stream, not on robots, datacenters, models, or AI companies. My book instead calls for individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities to own AI infrastructure directly.

The endpoints differ too. In Plan A's 2040 storyline, institutions and equipment are gradually handed to AIs until humans could no longer shut the whole system down. The Three Crucibles asks whether people remain stewards of abundance or depend on a narrow owner class. I don't know which institutional mix could survive a broken agreement or sustained geopolitical pressure. My take is that safety rules and economic rights must be joined because neither can do the other's job.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A uses declarations, inspection, and safety arguments against hidden capacity and catastrophic systems. The Three Crucibles pushes ownership outward. Verification cannot allocate the gains; broad ownership cannot make a dangerous system safe.</p>

## The fork reaches the contract desk

My book's fictional comparison gives one insurer 5,000 claims adjusters while another uses coordinated AI agents, software systems that carry out parts of the claims process, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The mechanism is illustrative: adoption can outrun a buyer's ability to reverse it.

States own infrastructure verification; legislatures and citizens own economic rights. In my proposal, the chief information officer, the executive accountable for technology, owns supplier exit with the business leader. Exit proof is a live handover in which an independent team runs one critical workflow to an agreed standard before renewal.

This borrows from disaster-recovery drills and failover exercises, established tests of operation after failure. The AI-specific addition is to grade an independent replacement against frozen, buyer-owned cases when the model's internals may be inaccessible. My proposed pilot covers claims triage, the initial sorting of insurance cases, over 30 days and about 15 people-days shared by a process owner, replacement engineer, security reviewer, and procurement lead. It is not an industry benchmark.

The acceptance record should name the cases, grader, and limits for error rate, time, and cost. It should preserve each decision's source trail, define permitted tools and data, and name who restores service after failure. A file export is not enough if another team cannot operate safely.

Where a company already maintains system descriptions, risk controls, and logs for the European Union's 2024 risk-based AI law, the EU AI Act, my proposal would reuse them. Compliance documents describe a system; a handover test shows whether a buyer can operate it without the current supplier.

I think the next fault line is reversibility capture. Suppliers with clear interfaces should welcome proof that they are easier to leave; others may set the price, control certification, or refuse the test. If exclusive data, licensing, or scarce computing capacity still gives the original supplier pricing power after a successful handover, exit proof has exposed its limit. The argument returns to ownership.

AI 2040 cannot validate The Three Crucibles, but it sharpens the test: can machine output rise while labor income, purchasing power, and wage-linked public revenue weaken? If those channels remain resilient under broad automation, the thesis weakens. If they erode, rules for inspection, ownership, and exit will decide who carries power into the next economy.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Exit proof tests a live supplier handover against buyer-owned cases and measurable limits. It extends recovery practice without replacing compliance. A successful handover may still leave the buyer dependent on exclusive inputs or scarce capacity, so verification, ownership, and exit remain separate.</p>
</div>
