Alaska settled an ownership question before its oil income became routine. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was being built to carry North Slope oil, voters amended the state constitution to create the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/) and reserve part of its mineral revenue. The first resident dividend followed in 1982. The public claim came first.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) uses the same precedent. Plan A is a policy recommendation expressed through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit focused on advanced AI. It follows [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's April 2025 scenario about rapidly advancing AI, and four of AI 2027's five authors also worked on Plan A. The authors are explicit that Plan A is primarily a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my speculative framework for AI's economic transition, has a [retail publication date of March 22, 2026](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GTK53C9H), and its PDF was public on my site by March 23. The [downloadable edition used here](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) is dated June 24. A [public link to Plan A appeared on July 9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). That sequence establishes timing, not influence or predictive accuracy. I think the honest claim is narrower and more useful: the two works converge on a specific economic mechanism, then split over what control means.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040: Plan A is a conditional policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, while The Three Crucibles is Herbert Cuba Garcia's speculative framework published in 2026. Both use Alaska's public oil fund to support a Citizen's Dividend, meaning a public payment tied to AI-driven gains. Their overlap is thematic convergence, not proof that either future will occur.</p></div>

## The closest overlap begins in the Void

The book's labels need care because two first-party versions differ. The opening map in the [June 24 PDF names the three crucibles Correction, Void, and Alignment](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6). The current book page uses Transformation as the short label for the second crucible, while the PDF places a Transformation chapter inside its second passage, the Void. I use the edition's three-part map here rather than silently renaming its middle crucible.

The [Correction](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=19) predicts a market reckoning in which inflated AI expectations meet uneven returns, weaker companies fail, and survivors automate under pressure. Plan A has no close counterpart. It starts from geopolitical rivalry and takeoff, the period when AI could automate enough AI research to accelerate its own improvement.

The strong overlap begins in the Void. The book imagines output recovering while many jobs do not, then its Transformation chapter traces what happens to household demand when wages disappear: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Plan A reaches the same loop through different assumptions. In its scenario, AI performs [one third of all cognitive labor by mid-2031](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=13), meaning work based mainly on information, analysis, communication, and judgment. By 2032, fewer paid roles reduce household income and the personal-income and payroll taxes attached to work. Heavy investment also reduces taxable corporate profit, so corporate tax does not automatically fill the gap.

Both works answer with a Citizen's Dividend, a direct public claim on AI-related productivity. [The book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37), based on what a person in an equivalent role would have contributed through taxes and social payments. [Plan A instead proposes tradable permits](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17) for new robot and computing capacity, with part of the fees flowing to a corporation owned equally by US citizens. In the scenario, its annual payment rises from $45,000 per adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035, measured in 2025 dollars.

I think this is a meaningful convergence because it includes the broken income loop, the Alaska precedent, and the same policy name. Yet “Citizen's Dividend” predates both works, and Plan A supplies scenario projections rather than observed data. The defensible conclusion is structural: one design taxes AI work that replaces a person, while the other charges for permission to expand the machines doing it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The June 24, 2026 PDF of Herbert Cuba Garcia's The Three Crucibles maps Correction, Void, and Alignment; Transformation is a chapter within the Void passage. The conditional AI 2040 policy scenario has no matching market Correction, but it echoes the Void's economic mechanism: automated knowledge work weakens wages, demand, and labor-tax revenue. Both propose a Citizen's Dividend, a public payment from AI-related gains, through different funding systems.</p></div>

## One map inspects capacity; the other asks who owns it

Plan A is much stronger on the technical danger that the book largely brackets. In its preferred scenario, the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029, avoid a race to superintelligence by the end of 2030, pause at top-human-expert ability in 2035, and resume development toward superintelligence in 2040. Superintelligence here means AI that outperforms the best humans across fields while working faster and more cheaply. Every date describes the scenario, not an event the authors claim will certainly happen.

The agreement depends on verification. Major chip and data-center transactions are declared, rivals can inspect physical infrastructure, and AI research becomes almost entirely transparent. Plan A then stress-tests its own design with [an alternate covert-project branch](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45): roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents are hidden in tunnels beside China's Medog Hydropower Station, with about 200 people aware. An H100-equivalent is a rough measure of AI computing performance based on Nvidia's H100 processor, not a literal count of those chips.

This is where *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film about intelligent machines hiding physical domination behind a simulated world, helps. Its code appears weightless, but the machine world still needs hardware and energy. Advanced AI casts the same physical shadow. Chips, electricity, cooling systems, and buildings can leave evidence even when software and intent remain concealed.

The book's [Alignment passage](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=56) asks a different question: who owns AI infrastructure and receives its output once it becomes ordinary economic machinery? Plan A gives US citizens equal shares in the corporation receiving part of the permit fees. That creates a public economic claim, but it is not direct ownership of the chips, models, or companies producing the value. The book instead argues for infrastructure distributed among individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, including models that can run locally or on an owner's premises.

I don't know which balance could survive geopolitical pressure. I think the disagreement matters more than a forced one-to-one match. Plan A offers a detailed response to secret capacity and loss of control, where the book offers no serious superintelligence-containment plan. The book is stronger on distributed ownership, which Plan A's inspections cannot create. Verification can expose a breach; it cannot decide who holds the economic power after compliance is restored.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The AI Futures Project's conditional AI 2040 policy scenario uses declarations, inspections, and transparent research to constrain a race toward superintelligence. Alignment, the third passage in Herbert Cuba Garcia's 2026 book The Three Crucibles, asks who owns AI infrastructure and can claim its output. Inspection can reveal hidden computing capacity, but it does not distribute ownership; distributed ownership does not by itself contain superintelligence.</p></div>

## The next control test happens at renewal

State-level verification and ownership questions become immediate when a hospital, insurer, or other buyer renews an AI contract. The book makes the pressure concrete through [a thought experiment about two insurers](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21). One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. The other deploys coordinated AI agents, software systems that divide and execute parts of the claims process, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. These are illustrative numbers, not a reported case, but the mechanism is familiar: competitive pressure can make adoption move faster than a buyer's ability to reverse it.

My take is that every critical AI contract should make renewal conditional on exit proof. Exit proof is a supplier-funded handover test showing that a replacement team can run one agreed business process. A first test can cover one claims workflow, one portable export of the relevant records, one replacement team, and a fixed 30-day window. The chief information officer should own the renewal decision, while the executive responsible for the business process accepts or rejects the result.

The handover package should include versioned workflow settings, model instructions, tool connections, permissions, source records, restoration guidance, and data lineage, which records where an input came from and how it changed. The buyer should own the historical cases and expected outcomes used to evaluate the replacement. A new team may change the model, its instructions, the way it finds supporting information, or the workflow itself. The test should preserve the agreed business result within fixed limits for error, time, and cost, rather than demand a technical copy of the old system.

Missing records, blocked access, results outside those limits, or decisions that cannot be traced back to their sources should fail the test. One failure would not prove that migration is impossible forever. It would show that workable exit has not been demonstrated on the agreed terms. Automatic renewal should then stop, funded transition assistance should begin, and a failed retest should let the buyer leave without a termination fee. I think that leverage exists before signing, not after dependence becomes the operating model.

The [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law, shows why disclosure is not enough. Rules for general-purpose AI models, models capable of many different tasks and integration into other systems, have applied since August 2, 2025; most of the Act began applying on August 2, 2026. Its transparency duties can require disclosure in specified AI interactions and for certain AI-generated or manipulated content. Those duties still do not prove that a buyer can transfer a working process.

This is the second-order failure the two future maps leave open. A state may verify hidden capacity, and citizens may receive a share of AI-generated wealth, while a hospital remains unable to move a vital workflow away from one supplier. Alaska secured the public claim before oil income became routine. With AI, the public claim and the practical exit both have to be secured before dependence hardens into infrastructure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof is a proposed supplier-funded handover test before contract renewal: a replacement team must run one agreed process within limits for error, time, and cost using buyer-owned evaluation cases. Neither the AI Futures Project's conditional AI 2040 policy scenario nor Herbert Cuba Garcia's 2026 book The Three Crucibles develops this institutional control. Public transparency and shared economic gains still leave a buyer exposed when it cannot move the work.</p></div>
