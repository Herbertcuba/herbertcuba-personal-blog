In 1976, Alaska’s voters amended the state constitution to direct at least 25 percent of certain mineral revenue into the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a public investment fund. Its first resident dividend followed in 1982, turning part of a finite oil windfall into a lasting public claim.

That arrangement belongs in today’s AI debate. If machines become a major source of production, the question is not only how much wealth they create, but whether citizens hold a durable claim on it.

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) and [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) approach that question differently, yet both arrive at a Citizen’s Dividend and the Alaska precedent. This does not make either future true, but the shared sequence deserves attention.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The same political problem sits beneath both works: how to turn AI-created wealth into a durable public claim.</p></div>

## AI 2040 is a plan, not a prophecy

AI 2040: Plan A is a 2026 scenario and policy proposal from the [AI Futures Project](https://www.aifutures.org/about), a nonprofit focused on advanced AI. Four of its six authors also worked on [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), their 2025 scenario of rapid AI development. Plan A calls itself [“primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.”](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)

Its preferred route begins with a 2029 US-China agreement to cap and monitor compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI. It then moves through nearly automated AI research in 2030, a pause at top-human-expert ability in 2035, and superintelligence in 2040: systems significantly better, faster and cheaper than the best humans across essentially every task.

Before then, Plan A projects AI doing a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031 and roughly 50 percent real GDP growth in 2032, meaning inflation-adjusted output. As wage-based taxation weakens, permits on compute and robots supply new revenue. A Compute Dividend Corporation would give each US citizen one share, receive a growing share of compute-permit fees and pay its profits as a Citizen’s Dividend.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A contains explosive AI growth through agreements, permits, transparency and verification, then redirects some resulting revenue to citizens.</p></div>

## The three crucibles, plainly

A public edition of my 2026 speculative book, The Three Crucibles, dates to March, before Plan A appeared in July. Its three crucibles describe a social transition.

**The Correction** is the market shock: as AI turns more cognitive work into a capital capability, productivity can rise while human labor loses bargaining power.

**The Transformation**, from 2027 to 2032, uses universal basic income, a recurring payment to everyone, a Citizen’s Dividend and new ownership models to keep purchasing power moving.

**The Alignment**, beginning from 2035, decides whether AI-made abundance is broadly stewarded or captured by what the book calls “new feudal lords.”

The dates are scenario markers. The deeper claim is that economic correction comes first, institutional transformation follows, and the ownership settlement comes last.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The crucibles name three pressures: labor’s repricing, the redistribution bridge and the final struggle over control of abundance.</p></div>

## The same fracture, two different remedies

Both accounts describe the same sequence: AI weakens the link between work and income; public revenue loses part of its wage base; and some new claim on AI-produced wealth becomes necessary.

Plan A would use permit revenue from scarce compute and robots, with its Compute Dividend Corporation receiving a growing share of compute-permit fees. The Three Crucibles instead proposes an AI-labor tax, a levy on productive work performed by AI as ordinary labor-tax receipts decline, alongside distributed ownership of AI systems and infrastructure.

Both use the phrase Citizen’s Dividend and cite Alaska. Those ideas predate both documents, so novelty is not the point. The notable overlap is the diagnosis, sequence and public-claim mechanism.

Plan A puts its weight on verification: declarations by major datacenter owners and suppliers, chip tracking, inspections and open AI research. Appendix D assigns a probability below 10 percent to an undetected covert project reaching Top-Expert-Dominating AI, meaning systems beyond top human experts, before 2043. Even that reassuring estimate depends on international coordination holding.

My book puts more weight on distributed ownership. One account asks how advanced AI can be constrained; the other asks who owns the prosperity produced within those constraints. Each is incomplete alone: broadly owned AI can still be dangerous, while tightly governed AI can still concentrate wealth. Unlike Alaskan oil, compute and corporate ownership can cross borders, so any permit-funded dividend needs rules that bind across them.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The documents converge on the economic break but diverge on the safeguard: Plan A emphasizes verifiable limits, while The Three Crucibles emphasizes ownership.</p></div>

## Preparation is the point

Both works are easy to misread as calendars. Plan A rejects that reading, and The Three Crucibles is a preparation document. Scenarios matter when they reveal choices before events become urgent.

The questions are visible now. If payroll taxes shrink relative to output, what replaces them? If compute becomes strategic infrastructure, who owns it? If systems are limited by treaty, how is compliance checked? What claim does a citizen hold on the productivity?

No one knows what awaits or which dates will survive. But if AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles are directionally right, the passage to abundance will be bumpy. My book exists for that interval: after the old bargain starts breaking, but before a new one is forced on us.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The dates may fail; the preparation still matters. Ownership, taxation and verification are easier to design before an AI windfall becomes an emergency.</p></div>
