A future scenario can be useful without getting every date right. Its real test is whether it exposes choices that we would otherwise postpone until pressure makes them for us.

Consider the Alaska Permanent Fund. Alaska voters created it through a constitutional amendment in 1976, requiring a share of the US state's mineral revenue to be invested. Dividend payments to residents began in 1982. Instead of distributing oil, Alaska gave the public a continuing financial claim on income from a shared resource.

That institution appears in both [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) and my book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/). The documents approach AI differently, yet ask the same difficult question: if machines produce more of the economy, how do people retain a claim on the result?

## What AI 2040 actually proposes

Published in July 2026, AI 2040 is a scenario and policy proposal from the AI Futures Project, the forecasting group behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), its 2025 scenario about rapid progress toward superhuman AI. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.”

Its preferred path begins with a US–China agreement in 2029. The deal averts the scenario's alternative 2030 route, in which fully automated AI research leads to superintelligence by year-end. Development stays within the human range through 2035, when the parties pause at systems roughly as capable as top human experts. In 2040, the scenario permits development to proceed to systems that broadly exceed human cognitive ability.

The plan relies on verification: evidence that countries declare AI research, track advanced chips and allow datacenters to be inspected. It also expects automation to shrink labor income and its tax base. Permits for AI chips and robots help fund a Compute Dividend Corporation, where each citizen receives a share and profits are paid as dividends. Compute here means the processing capacity supplied by chips and datacenters. Alaska is the stated precedent.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A combines international controls on AI development with a citizen dividend funded by machine capacity.</p>
</div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

My 2026 speculative book was public on my site by March, months before Plan A appeared. It is a preparation document built around three stages rather than a detailed policy timetable.

**The Correction** is the economic shock. As AI makes cognitive work cheaper, labor becomes less scarce and the old link between a job, an income and social value weakens.

**The Void** is the exposed interval after that bargain starts breaking but before replacement institutions are trusted. A citizen's dividend offers one possible bridge because people still need purchasing power when less income reaches them through wages.

**The Alignment** is the political choice that follows. Society must decide whether AI infrastructure produces broadly shared abundance or concentrates ownership in a small group with feudal levels of power.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Correction breaks the labor bargain, The Void exposes the missing bridge, and The Alignment decides who holds power afterward.</p>
</div>

## Agreement does not mean certainty

The strongest overlap is a mechanism, not a date. When machines perform more paid work, output can rise while wages, labor-tax revenue and household purchasing power lose ground. My book puts the demand problem bluntly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.”

Both documents then use the name “Citizen's Dividend” and cite the Alaska Permanent Fund. They also see the same political risk: machine-produced wealth can be spread widely, or control can harden around the institutions that own the machines. This specificity is useful, but it does not validate either forecast.

The main difference is where each begins. AI 2040 asks whether governments can see enough activity to stop a hidden race across a dangerous capability threshold; its appendices test the proposed controls against a covert AI project. My book asks who owns the productive infrastructure and who receives its returns.

A well-inspected AI system could still concentrate wealth, while broad ownership would not by itself make a frontier system safe. Verification protects the boundary around development. Ownership shapes the social settlement inside it. I do not know which sequence will prove closer to reality, but any serious preparation needs both layers.

<p>The documents share an economic warning but address different gaps: safe development and legitimate distribution.</p>

## Preparation is the point

No one knows what waits beyond the current wave of AI. Plan A's dates may slip, and the three crucibles may unfold differently. But if the documents are directionally right, the difficult part will be renegotiating work, ownership and authority while AI is already changing the economy.

That is why The Three Crucibles exists as a preparation document. We may disagree about the route and still take the warning seriously: the road ahead is likely to be bumpy, and certainty will arrive too late to do the preparing for us.
