Buried inside the [AI 2040](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) scenario is a company that does not exist. Every US citizen would hold one share in it, and a fixed portion of the fees charged for permission to build robots and computing capacity would flow into its accounts.

I think this imagined company is more revealing than the report's 2040 date for superintelligence, its term for AI that is significantly better than the best humans at everything while also working faster and more cheaply. The company turns a technical safety plan into a draft economic constitution. It decides who may expand productive capacity, who collects the resulting scarcity value, and who receives a legal claim on it.

That makes AI 2040 worth reading beside [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my book about the passage from AI hype to automated production and a fight over ownership. Plan A is a conditional policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit focused on advanced-AI futures, and its authors say it is a recommendation rather than their best guess. Four of the five authors credited on the group's [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/) scenario, published April 3, 2025, appear among Plan A's six named contributors.

The chronology needs equal care. The repository for my site records the initial book PDF and download-link change on March 23, 2026, the dedicated book page on June 1, and the current PDF update on June 24. AI 2040 was publicly available by July 9. This establishes the order of the artifacts. It proves neither influence nor independent development, and a later scenario is not data that validates an earlier framework.

The comparison still matters because both works reach the same economic fracture from different starting points. Their unusually specific agreement identifies a common problem, while their disagreement shows what each leaves unresolved.

## Growth no longer guarantees public revenue

Plan A imagines a 2029 agreement between the United States and China that prevents a 2030 takeoff, the feedback loop in which AI automates AI research and rapidly helps create more capable AI. Development stays within the range of human ability, pauses at top-human-expert level in 2035, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. Compliance is checked by tracking compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI, and inspecting the infrastructure around it.

The economic break arrives earlier. By mid-2031, the scenario assigns one third of cognitive labor, work based mainly on reasoning, analysis, and communication, to AI. It then models roughly 50 percent inflation-adjusted economic growth in 2032 while warning that the tax base can shrink as a share of the economy. Personal-income and payroll-tax receipts fall as people lose jobs, while deductions for datacenters, factories, and robots reduce taxable corporate profit. More output no longer guarantees more wages or public revenue.

Plan A responds with capacity permits: paid, tradable legal permissions to build a limited amount of robot or computing infrastructure. Its model produces $50 trillion in US federal permit revenue in fiscal 2032, then directs most American permit fees to a Citizen's Dividend for adults. The projected payment rises from $45,000 per person in 2032 to roughly $1 million in 2035, in 2025 dollars. These are scenario outputs, not evidence. The mechanism shifts public revenue from employment to permission to expand scarce productive capacity.

The current PDF of [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) maps its stages as The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. In Part Two, The Great Decoupling, its name for productivity separating from employment, The Void and The Transformation are separate chapters that develop the Second Crucible; the landing page uses The Transformation as the middle label. The underlying sequence remains clear. AI hype collapses, machine production creates value without restoring enough jobs or demand, and the final stage asks who owns the systems and distributes their output.

My book proposes a Citizen's Dividend and an AI labor levy tied to what a person in the displaced role would have contributed to public revenue. Both works cite the Alaska Permanent Fund, created by a 1976 constitutional amendment and paying its first dividend in 1982, as a way to spread oil income. I think the important convergence is causal rather than verbal: output can grow while wages, demand, and tax revenue weaken, forcing a new public claim on production.

<p>Both works foresee a fiscal break in which automated output grows faster than the wage-based systems that fund households and government. Plan A sells permissions to expand capacity; The Three Crucibles taxes displaced labor value, but both route part of machine productivity back to citizens.</p>

## One dividend contains two theories of power

Plan A's Compute Dividend Corporation goes beyond an annual cash transfer. It would legally own a fixed portion of permit fees, every US citizen would hold one share, and profits would be divided equally. Yet that share is a claim on a centrally managed fee stream rather than on the robots or datacenters themselves. Its value depends on the permit cap, the international agreement, and the corporation's political durability.

The Three Crucibles makes distributed ownership a separate pillar: individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities would own AI infrastructure rather than depend only on payments from a few owners. Its labor levy aims to preserve public revenue, while ownership moves rights closer to the people using and governing the systems. I think this is the sharper divergence. One framework distributes income from a controlled bottleneck; the other also tries to distribute productive assets.

Plan A supplies technical detail that my book leaves thin. Major datacenter owners and chip suppliers would declare large transactions, rival powers would inspect facilities, and developers would publish safety cases explaining why a system should not cause irreversible catastrophe. Technical control means limiting an AI's ability to cause disaster even if it deliberately tries. Giving more people an economic stake cannot make an unsafe system safe.

A system can also pass inspections and remain under human direction while its gains concentrate. I don't know which institutional blend could survive serious geopolitical pressure. My take is that a complete settlement needs a technical rule for what may be built and how compliance is verified, joined to an economic rule for who holds enforceable rights to what the permitted machines produce.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A gives citizens a legal share of permit revenue and supplies technical safeguards. The Three Crucibles pushes ownership further outward. The two safeguards have to be joined, because distributing an unsafe system spreads danger while controlling a system says nothing about who receives its gains.</p>
</div>

## Test the claim before testing the date

We cannot test a 2040 superintelligence scenario today, but we can test whether an economic right survives when its assumptions change. The citizen's claim should still make sense if capability growth is slower, a government revises the permit regime, or an international agreement breaks down. A settlement that works only under one forecast and one political coalition is fragile.

This durability test does not automatically favor one proposal. Plan A's legally owned fee stream is stronger than a discretionary benefit, but a central regime still creates the fees. An AI labor levy follows deployed work more directly, yet a human-equivalent unit becomes harder to measure when AI changes jobs instead of replacing them neatly. Direct ownership can preserve local power, but it brings coordination and safety duties that the book does not fully specify.

I think the framework should now be judged against observable economic evidence. Its urgency weakens if broad automation leaves labor income, household purchasing power, and wage-linked public revenue resilient. It strengthens if machine output rises while those channels erode, especially when citizens receive payments that lawmakers can change but no lasting right to the underlying production. AI 2040's publication is not that evidence. Its value is narrower: a detailed safety scenario reaches the same public-revenue problem and exposes the choice between receiving income from a system and holding power within it.

When a safety plan decides who may build productive machines and where the proceeds go, it is already deciding the shape of society. I think technical control is only half of a legitimate settlement; the other half is a durable citizen right to the wealth those machines create.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Judge AI policy by whether citizens retain an enforceable claim across changing forecasts, governments, and international bargains. A machine can remain technically controlled while citizens lose leverage over the wealth it creates.</p>
</div>
