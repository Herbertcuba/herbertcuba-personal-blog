In 1969, Alaska received $900 million from an oil-lease sale on the North Slope, then spent most of it on infrastructure and public programs. Voters changed the rule in 1976: at least 25 percent of certain mineral revenues would go into the Alaska Permanent Fund, a public investment fund designed to preserve part of the state's oil wealth. Its earnings have supported annual payments to eligible residents since 1982.

The arrangement turned income from a finite resource into a lasting public claim. Alaska kept the oil fields intact while giving residents a share in their value.

Fifty years later, [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) and [my 2026 speculative book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), reach for the same idea. The book page has been public since March 23, 2026; AI 2040 appeared publicly in July. That timing proves neither influence nor accuracy, but the structural overlap deserves attention.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows how society can turn concentrated resource income into a durable public claim, which is the economic problem both AI accounts eventually confront.</p></div>

## What AI 2040 actually proposes

The AI Futures Project, a US research nonprofit behind the 2025 race scenario *AI 2027*, presents Plan A as a recommendation rather than a prediction. It asks what a relatively successful path through advanced AI might require.

Under its assumptions, a US-China agreement in 2029 prevents the full automation of AI research and development that would otherwise occur in 2030. Capability grows within the human range until a 2035 pause at a level matching the best human experts. In 2040, the plan allows a controlled move toward superintelligence, defined as systems significantly better than the best humans at everything while also being faster and cheaper.

Control rests on verification: tracked chips, inspected data centers, and transparent research make treaty violations easier to detect. Compute means the chips and infrastructure used to train and run AI. Plan A also proposes permits to build robot or compute capacity and a Compute Dividend Corporation in which every US citizen owns one share. In its projected 2032 economy, real gross domestic product grows by roughly 50 percent and the dividend reaches $45,000 per US citizen. Those are scenario outputs, not observed evidence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A couples a pause-and-verification system for advanced AI with a national ownership mechanism meant to return part of the resulting wealth to US citizens.</p></div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

My book describes the transition through three crucibles, meaning periods when existing institutions come under enough pressure to be remade. The author-verified taxonomy used here is The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. The current public landing page still uses *The Transformation* for the middle period.

**The Correction (2026)** begins when AI productivity weakens the old link between labor, income, and company value. Work does not vanish at once, but labor loses some of its economic leverage.

**The Void (2027–2032)** is the unstable interval before replacement systems are ready. Universal basic income, a regular payment made without a work requirement, becomes one possible bridge. The book's Citizen's Dividend goes further by tying that payment to AI infrastructure and output.

**The Alignment (2035 and beyond)** is the political settlement. Society either distributes ownership and acts as a steward of abundance, or allows control to harden around a small group of owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The three crucibles describe a labor correction, a dangerous institutional gap, and a final choice over who owns and governs AI-created abundance.</p></div>

## The overlap is economic, the split is political

Both accounts follow the same economic chain. As AI replaces more cognitive labor, wages and labor taxes carry less of the economy, but people still need purchasing power. My book puts it bluntly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Plan A models the same tax pressure, then reaches the same phrase and precedent: a Citizen's Dividend modeled on Alaska.

The funding mechanisms differ. My book emphasizes distributed AI ownership and taxes on AI labor. Plan A would ration the right to build robot and compute capacity through permits, then give each US citizen a share in a national corporation. Both give the public a claim on productive capital before labor stops doing that job reliably.

Their deepest disagreement concerns which risk comes first. Plan A prioritizes control through verification, treaties, and pauses; its own appendix about a covert Chinese data center shows how much depends on finding hidden capacity. My book prioritizes ownership and legitimacy. A controllable AI can still create a deeply unequal society, while broad ownership cannot make an uncontrollable AI safe. Both problems remain.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two accounts converge on the need to replace labor-based distribution, but one prioritizes verifiable control while the other prioritizes broad ownership.</p></div>

## Preparation matters more than prediction

No one knows what lies between today's AI systems and 2040. Forecasts can change the decisions that make them true or false, and both timelines may turn out to be wrong. Their convergence does not validate my book. It shows that different lines of reasoning can still lead to the same questions about income, control, and ownership.

That is why my book exists as a preparation document. If the AI Futures Project and I are directionally right, the road will be bumpy because several institutions must change faster than they usually do. Certainty may never arrive in time. Preparation still can.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Neither account can know the future, but their shared warning is reason enough to prepare for a compressed and politically difficult transition.</p></div>
