Two 2026 works share one economic problem: AI can raise output while income from human work falls.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, a forecasting group focused on advanced AI. Four authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), a 2025 scenario about a race beyond top human ability. Plan A proposes how nations could govern superintelligence, meaning AI far more capable than the best people across most intellectual work. Its authors call it a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my 2026 book about automation, labor, and ownership. Its PDF was in my [public repository](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) by March 23; this edition is dated June 24. A [public link to Plan A](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) followed on July 9. That establishes only the public sequence; private origins remain unknown.

Both works call their proposed public payment from AI-era production a Citizen's Dividend. Both cite the Alaska Permanent Fund, a public fund created in 1976 to save part of the state's oil wealth. Annual payments began in 1982, giving residents a claim on income from a shared resource.

I think that specific overlap is meaningful thematic convergence. It cannot validate the whole book. The disagreement is more useful: verification checks whether declared computing capacity, the chips and data-center hardware used to train or run AI, is present and operating; ownership determines who receives its gains; and exit proof is a funded handover that shows whether another team can keep one service working.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works connect automation to weaker labor income and a public dividend. Their divergence separates three controls: verifying physical AI capacity, owning its returns, and proving that an institution can change suppliers.</p></div>

## The economic map converges

The [opening map in the June 24 edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment. The Correction begins as AI raises returns to capital and weakens jobs. The Void follows when production recovers without restoring them, reducing household labor income. The Alignment asks who governs the machines and receives their abundance. In this edition, Transformation is a chapter inside the Void; the canonical second crucible is the Void.

Plan A puts scenario numbers on that chain. By mid-2031, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, meaning work based mainly on analysis, communication, and professional judgment. As jobs disappear, income and payroll-tax revenue contracts. The United States sells permits for new computing capacity and robots, directing most proceeds to a Citizen's Dividend projected at $45,000 per adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035 ([PDF pages 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17)).

The funding differs. The book's [Transformation chapter](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36) proposes charging each human-equivalent unit of AI labor, meaning work a person would otherwise perform; Plan A charges for permission to add machines. Both respond to the same demand problem: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Cheap production cannot sustain demand when households lose income.

On distribution, Plan A's public Compute Dividend Corporation gives each US citizen one share and pays out most permit revenue; the book calls for distributed ownership of AI capacity and its returns. I think the causal chain converges even though the timing and policy tools do not: automation weakens labor income, and a dividend reconnects production with purchasing power.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction, the Void, and the Alignment map to Plan A's concerns about automated work, lost tax revenue, public dividends, and concentrated wealth. The closest match is the Void's path from lost labor income to a Citizen's Dividend.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership solve different problems

The maps separate over technical control. Plan A models takeoff, when AI-assisted research could outpace governments. Its scenario has a US-China agreement in 2029 preventing a rush to superintelligence by the end of 2030, a pause at top-expert level in 2035, and a restart in 2040. *The Three Crucibles* does not model that race or systems escaping human control.

Plan A answers with verification: evidence that claims about chips, data centers, and research on frontier AI, the most capable systems under development, match reality. It would require large purchases and sales to be declared, track chips, allow reciprocal infrastructure inspections, and make almost all AI research visible. A secret project may hide its purpose, but it still needs hardware, electricity, cooling, and construction.

[Appendix D](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45) imagines a covert Chinese data center beside the proposed Medog Hydropower Station, a huge dam in China's Medog region. Its computing power equals 500,000 H100-equivalents; one equivalent benchmarks Nvidia's H100 data-center AI chip. In the scenario, the chance of producing AI better than top human experts across domains without detection before 2043 stays below 10 percent.

This is the Death Star problem in *Rogue One*, the 2016 *Star Wars* film about a hidden planet-destroying weapon. The Empire can conceal its purpose while materials and energy still leave traces. I think Plan A is strongest when it treats advanced AI as an industrial system with an observable footprint.

The book's [Alignment chapter](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57) addresses another danger: declared, legal capacity can still concentrate control and income. It calls for distributed ownership, open infrastructure and models, and laws against a private intelligence monopoly. Open means giving independent operators the model and operating components needed to inspect and run the service without complete dependence on its provider.

My take is that verification constrains hidden capacity, while ownership allocates the gains from visible capacity. I don't know which control will face the harder test, and neither do Plan A's authors. I think both are necessary, but neither proves that an institution can move a running AI service when a supplier relationship fails.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses tracking and inspections to reveal concealed AI chips and data centers. The book asks who controls lawful capacity and its gains. Neither shows whether a buyer can keep operating after changing suppliers.</p></div>

## Exit proof makes bargaining power testable

The unresolved problem appears at the contract desk. *The Three Crucibles* compares [two fictional insurers](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21): one keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; the other uses AI, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. Competition forces adoption before governance is settled.

Once the service is live, its supplier may control the setup, data connections, permissions, and tools. A contract can promise choice while those dependencies keep another team from operating it. Power then concentrates one contract at a time.

I call the practical test exit proof. At renewal, the chief information officer should own a supplier-funded, 30-day handover of one live workflow and one complete data export to an independent team. The business owner approves the result, while the buyer owns the evaluation set, the past cases and accepted outcomes used to judge whether the replacement works.

The handover package should include the model version, standing instructions, information sources, permissions, connected tools, saved information needed to resume work, and rebuilding steps. It must show what each tool expects and returns, and where important inputs came from. The replacement may change the model, instructions, or workflow if it preserves the agreed business result within the deadline, error limit, and cost ceiling.

Failure must change the deal. If the replacement cannot rerun the past cases or misses an agreed limit, automatic renewal stops, access continues during transition, and the supplier funds repair and support. Exit proof turns a promise of supplier choice into a pass-or-fail handover with consequences in the contract.

Since August 2, 2026, Article 50 of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's law for AI systems, has required disclosures for certain AI interactions and AI-generated or manipulated content. Those disclosures reveal AI use. A handover shows whether a service survives a supplier change. I think a failed handover predicts dependence even when the applicable rules are met.

Alaska created a public claim early in the life of its oil wealth. Supplier power has the same timing problem: the leverage exists before the contract is signed, and it becomes real only when the handover works.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Make renewal conditional on a supplier-funded, 30-day handover judged with buyer-owned past cases. A right to leave is meaningful only when another team can reproduce the business result within agreed limits.</p></div>
