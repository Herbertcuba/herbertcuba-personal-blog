Two maps of the AI transition arrive at the same economic fork.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 2026 policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, a forecasting group focused on advanced AI. Four of its six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), a 2025 scenario about AI surpassing top human ability. Plan A recommends how nations could govern a path to superintelligence, meaning AI far more capable than the best people across most intellectual work. Its authors call it a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my book about automation, labor, and ownership. The earliest public record I can verify shows that its PDF was added to the public code repository storing my site's files on [March 23, 2026](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8). The edition cited here is dated June 24; I cannot substantiate an earlier publication date. On July 9, 2026, a user named kschaul [submitted a link titled “AI 2040: Plan A”](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) to Hacker News, a public technology discussion site; its timestamped submission record preserves the title and destination. These two records establish only that the book PDF was public by March 23 and a Plan A link was public by July 9. They cannot show when either project began in private.

Both works call a proposed public payment from AI-era production a Citizen's Dividend, and both cite the Alaska Permanent Fund. Alaska created the fund in 1976 to save part of its oil wealth; annual payments to residents began in 1982. I think this is meaningful thematic convergence, though Plan A is another model rather than observed data and cannot validate the whole book. The disagreement may matter more: verification checks for hidden computing capacity through records, tracking, and inspections; ownership decides who receives the gains from capacity that is visible and legal; and exit proof reveals whether an institution can actually change suppliers.

Their overlap supports the economic diagnosis, while their disagreement exposes distinct questions about hidden capacity, ownership, and supplier dependence.</p></div>

## The economic map converges

The [opening map in the June 24 edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment. In the Correction, AI raises output while investment and asset owners receive a larger share of income and wages weaken. The Void follows when production recovers without restoring enough paid work to support household demand. Transformation is a chapter inside the Void, not the official second crucible in this edition. The Alignment asks who owns the machines and whether abundance is shared or concentrated.

Plan A puts scenario numbers on a similar chain. By mid-2031, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, meaning work based mainly on analysis, communication, and professional judgment. As people lose paid work, wage income and the personal and payroll taxes collected from it fall. The scenario's US government sells permits to add robots and computing capacity, then directs most proceeds to a Citizen's Dividend projected at $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035 ([PDF pages 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17)).

The funding mechanisms differ. The book's [Transformation chapter](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36) charges each unit of AI work that replaces the work of one person, while Plan A charges for permission to add machines. Both address the same demand problem: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” If households lose wages, cheaper production does not create the customers needed to buy it. I think the convergence is strongest here because the causal chain survives even if both timelines are wrong.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction, the Void, and the Alignment connect rising machine output to weaker wages, lower household demand, and a fight over ownership. Plan A reaches the same economic problem but funds its dividend through permits for new machines rather than a charge on AI work.</p></div>

## A fast takeoff makes verification physical

The maps separate when Plan A models takeoff: a feedback loop in which AI speeds up the research that improves AI, so capability can rise faster than governments can coordinate. Its scenario has a US-China agreement in 2029 that stops fully automated AI research from driving a rush to superintelligence by the end of 2030. Systems stay within human ability until a 2035 pause at top-expert level, meaning roughly the ability of the best human specialists across fields, then restart toward superintelligence in 2040. The pauses buy time to test control; *The Three Crucibles* does not model this loop or the risk of systems escaping human direction.

Plan A therefore makes verification physical. Governments would declare major chip and data-center transactions, track advanced chips, inspect one another's infrastructure, and make almost all frontier-AI research visible. Frontier AI means the most capable systems under development. A secret project can hide its purpose, but it still needs hardware, electricity, cooling, and construction.

Its [covert-project appendix](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45) tests that claim with an imagined Chinese data center in tunnels beside the planned Medog Hydropower Station, a huge dam in China's Medog region. The facility has 500,000 H100-equivalents, a standardized measure of computing performance based on the H100 AI data-center processor sold by Nvidia, a US chip designer. The measure compares processing capacity rather than counting literal H100 chips. At that scale, the project is powerful enough to matter but physically difficult to conceal. Plan A estimates less than a 10 percent chance that it could produce AI better than the top human expert in every domain without detection before 2043.

This is the Death Star problem in *Rogue One*, the 2016 *Star Wars* film about a hidden planet-destroying weapon. The Empire can conceal the mission, yet the materials and energy needed to build the weapon still leave traces. I think Plan A is strongest when it treats advanced AI as an industrial system with an observable footprint.

The book's [Alignment chapter](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57) covers the danger on the other side of inspection: capacity can be declared and legal while still placing income and control in very few hands. It calls for distributed ownership, giving citizens or independent operators a claim on AI infrastructure and its returns. My take is that hardware tracking constrains a covert race, while ownership constrains the power created by visible machines. I don't know which layer will face the harder test. Neither work proves that a buyer can keep an AI-supported service running after its supplier leaves.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A tracks the industrial footprint of advanced AI because rapid, AI-assisted research could outrun political coordination. The book addresses a different risk: even fully visible and legal systems can concentrate control through ownership.</p></div>

## The next failure appears at renewal

The book's [fictional insurer example](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21) shows why this gap matters. One insurer keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; another deploys AI, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The numbers are illustrative, but the mechanism is concrete: competition can force adoption before control questions are settled.

Once an AI-supported claims process is live, the supplier may control its instructions, data connections, permissions, and connected tools. A contract may say the buyer can move the service while those dependencies prevent another team from reviewing claims and producing comparable decisions. That is how bargaining power can disappear one integration at a time.

I call the practical test exit proof. Before renewal, the chief information officer should own a supplier-funded, 30-day handover to an independent replacement team. It covers one recurring business process, such as reviewing a defined class of claims, plus a usable copy of the necessary data. The business owner judges the result against a buyer-owned evaluation set: representative past claims paired with outcomes already accepted as correct.

The supplier documents which model is used, its instructions and input sources, the permissions it needs, what each connected tool receives and returns, any information needed to resume a case, and the rebuilding steps. The replacement may change the model or workflow, but it must reproduce the agreed business outcome within a fixed deadline, error limit, and cost ceiling. If it cannot rerun the past cases or misses a limit, renewal stops automatically, access continues during transition, and the supplier funds repair and handover support.

This test also separates disclosure from control. Since August 2, 2026, Article 50 of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's law for AI systems, has required disclosures for certain AI interactions and AI-generated or manipulated content. Those rules can reveal AI use, but they do not make the process runnable by someone else. A failed handover therefore predicts supplier dependence even when the disclosures are correct.

Alaska secured a public claim on resource income before decades of dividends were paid. A buyer's window is narrower: once a supplier's tools and permissions become part of daily work, the cost of leaving rises and negotiating power falls. I think the right to change suppliers becomes real only when an independent team can keep the business process working.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Make renewal depend on a supplier-funded handover of one defined business process and its necessary data. If an independent team cannot reproduce the accepted result within agreed time, cost, and error limits, the buyer has disclosure on paper but no working exit.</p></div>
