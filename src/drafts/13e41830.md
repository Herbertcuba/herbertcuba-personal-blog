Who gets a claim on the value when machines do more of the work?

In 1976, Alaska voters amended the state constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenue would enter the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a publicly owned investment fund, rather than go into current spending. Since 1982, eligible residents have received an annual dividend supported by the fund's investment earnings. The arrangement turns a shared resource into a lasting public claim.

[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), a 90-page scenario and policy proposal published on July 9, 2026 by the US nonprofit AI Futures Project, applies that principle to AI infrastructure. My 2026 book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), uses the same Alaska precedent and the same phrase, a Citizen's Dividend. Its framework was in writing by February 3, more than six months before this article, and public on my site by March 23. The chronology proves no forecast. It makes the independent agreement worth examining.

## What the two maps actually say

Four of Plan A's six authors also created [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), the AI Futures Project's 2025 rapid-AI scenario. Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” so its figures are conditional on governments following its preferred route.

That route begins with a US-China agreement in 2029. The countries track advanced chips and major sources of compute, the processing capacity supplied by those chips in datacenters. Plan A then moves through nearly automated AI research in 2030, a pause at systems that outperform top human experts in 2035, and a controlled move toward superintelligence, intelligence far beyond human ability across cognitive work, in 2040.

Its economic mechanism is just as important. In the scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031 and real GDP grows about 50 percent in 2032. As income from work and the taxes attached to it shrink, permits on compute and robots fund a corporation in which each citizen owns one share. The projected dividend rises from $45,000 per person in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035. These remain scenario outputs rather than promises.

The public edition of my book names three crucibles:

**The Correction (2026)** begins as capital drives more productivity and workers lose bargaining power, weakening wages and a tax system built around work.

**The Transformation (2027–2032)** bridges into a new distribution model. Universal basic income, a regular unconditional payment, provides stability; a Citizen's Dividend gives people a claim on AI-generated output.

**The Alignment (2035 onward)** asks who stewards abundance and gains power from it. In this book, alignment is an institutional question as well as a technical one.

Plan A's labor-tax problem maps to the Correction, its dividend to the Transformation, and its concern about concentrated power to the Alignment. Alaska, basic income, and taxes on automation all predate both works. The notable convergence is the sequence, not the ingredients.

  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Both maps move from pressure on labor income, through a public dividend, to a struggle over control. They reach that sequence from different starting points.</p>

## Their agreement ends at the institution

Plan A treats coordination and verification as its main safeguards. Chips are tracked, datacenters inspected, research disclosed, and rival states asked to pause together. Its own appendix estimates a below-10-percent chance that an undetected covert project reaches top-expert-dominating AI before 2043. Compute that escapes monitoring also escapes the permits meant to fund the dividend.

The Three Crucibles puts more weight on ownership. Verification can restrain dangerous development while compliant infrastructure remains concentrated. Ownership determines who keeps receiving income and influence when coordination fails. Plan A addresses this through one share per citizen, while my book stays at the institutional level rather than offering a technical AI-safety plan. I think the two layers need each other.

Europe offers a current example of the distinction. The [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj), the European Union's risk-based AI law, placed obligations on general-purpose AI models from August 2, 2025; most of the law began applying on August 2, 2026. It assigns duties, but does not settle ownership of the productivity.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Verification limits what powerful actors may do. Ownership determines who benefits. A stable transition probably needs both.</p>
</div>

## Preparation is the point

A useful test comes before either map reaches its dramatic ending. If advanced AI spreads through 2032 without sustained pressure on labor income or tax revenue from work, our shared diagnosis weakens. If those pressures arrive without a durable public claim on AI output, the economic problem remains even if the safety regime works.

No one knows what is waiting for us. The Plan A authors do not, and neither do I. But if the AI Futures Project and The Three Crucibles are even directionally right, the road to abundance will be bumpy because production can change faster than ownership and public institutions. That is why my book exists: as a preparation document, not a timetable.
