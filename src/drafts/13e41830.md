In 1976, Alaska put oil revenue into a permanent fund. Its first dividend in 1982 gave eligible residents a direct claim on income from a shared resource.

*AI 2040: Plan A* is a 2026 governance scenario from the AI Futures Project, the forecasting group behind *AI 2027*; four of its six authors worked on that 2025 scenario. Plan A is a recommendation, not a best-guess forecast, and models a Citizen's Dividend on Alaska.

*The Three Crucibles*, my book, reaches the same mechanism. A [GitHub commit](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8), a dated change in a version-controlled public record, shows the PDF on my site on March 23, 2026. I cite a fixed June 24 edition. A [July 9 link post on Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425), a public technology discussion site, pointed readers to *AI 2040*. The records establish public sequence, not private origin or influence.

I think the parallel matters. Both works trace automated production toward weaker wages and a public dividend, then split: Plan A asks governments to find hidden AI capacity; the book asks who owns its output.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both works reach a Citizen's Dividend through one economic chain. Their dates show public sequence, not who conceived it first.</p></div>

## The Correction and the Void meet Plan A

The [commit-pinned June edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment, with Transformation as a chapter inside the Void. The Correction describes output rising as paid work falls. The Void traces demand as wages disappear: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Transformation answers with a Citizen's Dividend, a public share of AI infrastructure income (printed pp. 5 and 35–36).

In Plan A's scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, meaning work based on analysis, research, communication, or judgment. Labor taxes then weaken. The scenario auctions permits for new robots and AI computing capacity, the chips and data centers running AI, and directs most fees to a Citizen's Dividend projected at $45,000 per American adult in 2032 ([PDF pp. 13, 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=13)).

My book proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax on each unit of machine work measured against equivalent human work (printed p. 36). I think this is meaningful convergence, although the funding rules and timelines differ.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both maps connect automated work to weaker wages, taxes, and demand. The book taxes deployed AI work; Plan A charges for permission to add machines and computing capacity.</p></div>

## Verification does not settle ownership

Plan A models takeoff, a loop in which AI improves later AI systems. In its scenario, a 2029 US-China deal avoids a route to superintelligence by the end of 2030. Development pauses near leading-human-expert capability in 2035, then resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. The term means capability beyond humans across almost all intellectual work.

The deal relies on verification: declarations and inspections that expose undeclared chips and data centers. Its covert-project appendix imagines a secret Chinese facility in tunnels beside the proposed Medog Hydropower Station in Tibet, whose electricity could run it. Like *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film whose virtual world rests on physical machines, hidden AI still needs chips, power, cooling, and people.

I think that physical trail makes inspection plausible. The Alignment calls for distributed ownership through open infrastructure, open models, and laws against a private intelligence monopoly (printed p. 56). Inspection can reveal capacity without giving citizens a claim on its output. I don't know whether Plan A's system could survive sustained US-China rivalry, and its authors do not claim certainty.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification can expose hidden AI capacity because infrastructure leaves evidence. Ownership remains separate: visible machines can still concentrate income and power.</p></div>

## Exit proof makes control practical

Ownership has a company-level analogue. A buyer can own final documents yet depend on its AI supplier for usable data, permissions, settings, tool connections, and operating knowledge. Exit proof tests whether that paper ownership works in practice.

The book's fictional insurer example makes the pressure concrete (printed p. 20). One insurer keeps 5,000 claims adjusters, who investigate claims and decide payments. A rival uses AI, cuts staff by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The figures show the rush to sign before asking how to leave.

I call for a supplier-funded, 30-day handover of one workflow to one replacement team before renewal. It includes usable data plus records of its sources and changes, transferable permissions, saved versions of settings and prompts, working tool connections, and operating and recovery instructions. The buyer owns the evaluation set, a fixed sample of past cases used to judge results. The replacement may change the model or design, but must meet limits for errors, time, and cost.

The chief information officer owns acceptance. Missing inputs or results outside the limits block ordinary renewal and trigger supplier-funded correction and transition support.

Since August 2, 2026, [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's main AI law, has required disclosure in certain AI interactions and software-detectable marks on some AI-generated or manipulated content. That can reveal AI use, but it cannot equip a replacement team.

I think bargaining power is credible only when another team passes the test before renewal. Once failure or a price threat arrives after switching has become impractical, the choice is gone. A failed handover predicts supplier lock-in even when every transparency box is checked.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof makes a supplier-funded team run one workflow before renewal. Failure blocks renewal and triggers transition support. Control that cannot survive a handover is only paperwork.</p></div>
