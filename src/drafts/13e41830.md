Two AI maps arrive at the same economic problem. [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) imagines automation shifting income from labor so fast that government must rebuild purchasing power. [*The Three Crucibles*](/books/the-three-crucibles/) puts it simply: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Both propose a Citizen's Dividend and cite Alaska's oil dividend.

A [March 23, 2026 repository record](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) added *The Three Crucibles* PDF to my site. A July 9 [Hacker News submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) linked to *AI 2040*. This establishes a narrow sequence: my framework was public before the July discussion. “Citizen's Dividend” has older roots; the meaningful convergence is the economic chain around it.

I think that convergence validates the shape of the problem. Four of *AI 2040*'s six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/). Their map begins with state rivalry and AI control; mine starts with firms replacing labor. Both reach redistribution, then split over control.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both frameworks link machine productivity to weaker labor income and a Citizen's Dividend. Their agreement supports the diagnosis; their disagreement exposes the control question.</p></div>

## Where the economic maps meet

Plan A calls itself a recommendation and uses an illustrative scenario to test policy. A 2029 US-China agreement slows takeoff, meaning the speed at which capable AI systems improve. Systems reach the level of the best human experts, pause in 2035, and move toward superintelligence in 2040. I think the economic chain survives even if that calendar fails.

The Correction says AI productivity shifts income toward capital and weakens the wages supporting demand. The Transformation restores purchasing power through a Citizen's Dividend tied to AI infrastructure. The Alignment asks whether abundance produces broad stewardship or a new class of feudal owners.

In Plan A's scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031 (p. 13). The US sells permits to build robots and computing capacity, with most fees funding a dividend of $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035 (pp. 17–18). The book applies an AI Labor Equivalence Tax to deployed machine work (p. 36). One charges new capacity; the other charges machine labor in use. The choice changes who pays and which behavior government can shape.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both maps connect automation to weaker labor income, repaired demand, and concentrated power. Plan A sells capacity permits; The Three Crucibles taxes deployed AI labor.</p></div>

## Where the maps split

I think Plan A is strongest on verification: checking declared chips, datacenters, and research activity against reality. This addresses state rivalry and secret training. A supplier switch cannot audit a national chip stockpile or control a frontier model.

Appendix D tests this policy. Its fictional covert branch hides the equivalent of 500,000 Nvidia H100 chips in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station, with roughly 200 people aware. The setup shows how chip records and power inspections leave gaps. Like *Andor*, a huge system watches the visible machinery while a small network survives in its seams.

*The Three Crucibles* adds ownership through open infrastructure, open models, and legal safeguards against a private intelligence monopoly (p. 56). More suppliers and wider claims on output can expand choice, but a buyer still needs usable records and contract rights. My take is that the layers belong together. I don't know which failure arrives first, but they demand different defenses.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification makes secret AI development harder, while distributed ownership widens access to capacity and output. One protects the frontier; the other creates the conditions for economic choice.</p></div>

## The fork is already inside the contract

The insurer thought experiment in *The Three Crucibles* brings this down to earth. One company keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. A rival deploys AI agents, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months (p. 20). As claims move through the supplier's system, its decision records, evaluation cases, access rules, and workflow history can turn fast gains into deep dependence.

I would add a practical ownership check called the AI exit test. It measures operational reversibility: the proven ability to move records, permissions, evaluations, and service continuity to a replacement setup. Before renewal, the business owner exports one live workflow, has a replacement team replay past decisions, and restores service within an agreed time and cost. The test fails when the export cannot reproduce a sampled decision under the documented replacement setup.

Europe has a current decision moment. The bulk of the EU AI Act began applying on August 2, 2026. Obligations for general-purpose AI models had been in force since August 2, 2025. Depending on the system and the buyer's role, duties can include documentation, logs, transparency, oversight, and risk management. Portability and transition support still have to be negotiated. AI transparency tells us what we can inspect; exit rights tell us whether we retain agency when a supplier changes the terms.

I think Alaska offers the cleanest lesson. It settled the public claim before oil revenue became too large to redesign calmly. For an AI buyer, the equivalent moment is the contract renewal, while export formats, transition help, and recovery limits are still negotiable. The machines may arrive on either calendar; the power fork appears when we decide who owns their output and who keeps the right to leave.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The AI exit test turns a distant governance debate into a current contract decision. A live export, replay, and recovery exercise shows whether transparency comes with a practical ability to change suppliers.</p></div>
