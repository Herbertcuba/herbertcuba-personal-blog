Two works about advanced AI arrive at the same specific idea. Both call for a “Citizen's Dividend,” a public payment funded by the machine economy, and both cite the Alaska Permanent Fund. Alaska created that state investment fund through a 1976 constitutional amendment and paid its first dividends in 1982, turning income from a scarce productive asset into a durable public claim.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) is a 2026 policy recommendation expressed through a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, the nonprofit behind the April 2025 *AI 2027* scenario. [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my framework for the shift from human labor toward AI-dominated production. The cubagarcia.com repository records its book PDF and page link on March 23, 2026; Plan A's official materials show a July 2026 revision. That dates artifacts, not influence or correctness.

Plan A says it is “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” It cannot be data confirming another future map. I think the better question is why the economic structures converge and where their controls separate. That comparison produces a falsifiable test: advanced-AI strategy needs proof that dangerous computing capacity can be seen and stopped, plus separate proof that citizens hold an enforceable claim on the value it creates. If those outcomes cannot fail independently, the distinction adds nothing.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The shared Citizen's Dividend and Alaska precedent are a specific structural echo, not empirical validation. Their real value is the question they expose: can technical control and public economic rights survive separately?</p></div>

## The overlap is an economic sequence

In Plan A's conditional path, a 2029 agreement between the United States and China prevents fully automated AI research and development in 2030, meaning AI cannot take over most work of designing better successors. The authors say the unrestricted path would otherwise reach superintelligence that year. AI instead stays within the human range, pauses near top-expert ability in 2035, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040, defined as systems better than the best humans at every task while also faster and cheaper ([AI 2040, pp. 4–5](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

*The Three Crucibles* does not depend on that takeoff path. Its public page calls the movements the Correction, the Great Decoupling, and the Alignment; the PDF calls its middle map “The Void” and later uses “The Transformation” for movement into it. Beneath the varied wording, capital gains productive power, labor loses economic weight, redistribution becomes necessary, and society chooses between broad stewardship and concentrated ownership.

Plan A reaches the break through tax arithmetic. In its scenario, personal-income and payroll-tax revenue shrinks as paid work disappears, while companies deduct heavy investment in new capacity from taxable income. It still projects roughly 50 percent real economic growth in 2032. Output rises while the wage-based tax system loses its grip on the new value.

The book states the demand problem more bluntly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Machines may produce more, but displaced workers cannot sustain demand without another source of income. I think this is the genuine convergence. Neither map needs the other's timeline for the mechanism to matter: when labor stops being the main route to income and public revenue, the public needs a claim tied to machine production.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The timelines differ, but the causal chain is shared: automation expands output, weakens labor income and wage-based revenue, and makes a new public claim on machine-created value necessary.</p></div>

## The same dividend can fail in two directions

Plan A would cap new robot production and AI computing capacity, the processing power supplied by chips and datacenters. Its cap-and-trade system sets a total allowance, auctions permits, and lets companies trade them. In the United States part of the scenario, a legally specified share of compute-permit fees goes to a publicly owned Compute Dividend Corporation. The share starts at 25 percent in 2032 and rises to 75 percent in 2035; every US citizen owns one share, and profits are divided equally ([AI 2040, pp. 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)).

*The Three Crucibles* collects value elsewhere. Its AI Labor Equivalence Tax charges firms in proportion to the public revenue associated with the human work their AI replaces. It pairs that tax with distributed AI ownership and wider access to infrastructure and models. Plan A gives citizens scheduled permit revenue; the book reaches toward the productive layer itself.

That difference creates separate weaknesses. Plan A relies on chip tracking, datacenter declarations, monitoring of large training runs, research transparency, and safety cases. A safety case is an evidence-backed argument that a system will not cause irreversible catastrophe, tested by outside criticism ([AI 2040, pp. 13–14](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). The book's labor tax instead requires officials to decide what human work an AI system replaced and what public revenue that work represented.

Plan A stress-tests its own weakness in a fictional Covert Branch. China hides an AI datacenter in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station project, where legitimate construction supplies cover and underground heat is harder for infrared satellites to detect ([AI 2040, Appendix D, pp. 45–47](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf)). This did not happen. The imagined case shows that verification governs what enters the record while remaining vulnerable to capacity kept outside it.

The economic protection can fail alone too. Declared facilities could pass inspection while lawmakers weaken the public share; registered activity could pay a dividend while an off-record run escapes the stop system. None of these policy ingredients is new. My take is narrower: each protection needs its own owner, evidence, pass condition, and rejection rule because either can fail while the other appears healthy.

Neither work supplies a Nordic case where one national protection passes and the other fails. That absence is a research test, not an invitation to invent a success story. I don't know which mechanism would survive sustained political and technical pressure. I think we should discard the Two-Key Test if observed cases never separate its outcomes.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical visibility can fail while a dividend is paid, and public rights can weaken while every declared facility remains visible. The two-part test earns its keep only if real cases can separate those outcomes.</p></div>

## Put the test into the decision record

A company board cannot create a national compute register or a Citizen's Dividend, but supplier governance should not masquerade as an answer. *The Three Crucibles* offers a thought experiment: one insurer keeps 5,000 claims adjusters while another deploys AI agents, software that performs multistep claims work, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and overwhelms its rival within six months. This is an unvalidated test for a decision that is expensive to reverse after deployment.

Before signature, the business and technical owners should record the exact supplier and model version, claim types in scope, baseline period, and benefit and error thresholds. For claims work, errors can be measured per 1,000 cases over a fixed evidence window, with a stated false-negative tolerance for prohibited automated decisions. The record also needs an evidence cadence, audit trail, review date, cost envelope, worker-consultation trigger, named pause owner, and proof required for restart.

Procurement turns that record into duties: audit access, evidence deadlines, notice before a version change, and rights to pause, terminate, or exit. A changed model, missed threshold, withheld evidence, or system that cannot be paused must invalidate approval rather than merely schedule another meeting. The leverage exists before the contract is signed.

Nordic and European rules clarify the boundary. Section 11 of Sweden's 1976 Co-Determination in the Workplace Act generally requires an employer bound by a collective agreement to negotiate with the relevant union before an important change, although exceptional reasons may allow a decision first. On August 2, 2026, the bulk of the European Union's AI Act, its risk-based law for AI systems, started applying; obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models, broadly capable models used across many tasks, had applied since August 2, 2025. Consultation and compliance can strengthen a deployment decision, but neither creates a public ownership claim.

I think that is where the two future maps become useful now. A society that can stop dangerous AI but cannot claim its value may remain technically controlled and economically dispossessed. One that shares the proceeds but cannot stop hidden capacity is being paid for a risk it does not govern.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Make the technical test enforceable before signature, and state openly which economic rights a company cannot create. Control without a public claim leaves extraction intact; distribution without stop power leaves the underlying risk intact.</p></div>
