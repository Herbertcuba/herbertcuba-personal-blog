Take an insurer with 5,000 claims adjusters. In [*The Three Crucibles*](/books/the-three-crucibles/), a rival deploys an Agentic Swarm, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent and beats it within six months. The thought experiment gives us one company to follow through a larger argument.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) begins in Washington and Beijing. Four of its six authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/). They call Plan A a recommendation and make clear that their actual forecast may differ.

Our insurer makes the overlap easy to see. Both documents expect automation to weaken labor income, forcing a new way to keep demand alive. They eventually face the same political problem: too much technical power in too few hands. I think the disagreement that follows is more useful than the overlap.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 is a policy scenario, and both futures remain untested. It reaches the same economic break as The Three Crucibles from a different direction, then splits over control.</p></div>

## The same economic break

Plan A moves fast. In its scenario, a 2029 agreement between the United States and China blocks fully automated AI research from producing superintelligence by the end of 2030. AI systems scale within the human range, pause at top-human-expert level in 2035, then resume toward superintelligence in 2040.

By mid-2031, AI does a third of cognitive labor in the scenario. Claims, underwriting and customer service can all become cheaper while the wage base around our insurer shrinks.

*The Three Crucibles* draws a similar arc through the Correction, the Transformation and the Alignment. The labor bargain breaks, a Citizen's Dividend keeps people in the economy, and ownership decides how abundance is shared. I think the sequence matters more than Plan A's dates. A claims department can feel the pressure years before superintelligence.

The book's blunt line is "Robots do not buy iPhones." Perfect claims handling still fails when customers lose the income that pays the premiums.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both maps begin with the same basic problem: AI can raise output while cutting the wages that support demand. The timing may vary, but the insurer faces the pressure well before superintelligence arrives.</p></div>

## The dividend is the shared answer

Plan A repairs the loop with capacity permits. Under the international deal, the United States caps robot and compute production, then sells tradable build permits to the highest bidders. In the scenario, most compute and robot permit fees eventually fund a Citizen's Dividend. It starts at $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and rises to about $1 million in 2035.

Each U.S. citizen owns one share in a Compute Dividend Corporation and receives an equal dividend. The authors compare it with the Alaska Permanent Fund, the same precedent used in *The Three Crucibles*.

The book appears in cubagarcia.com's public repository on March 23, 2026, months before *AI 2040* appeared. I think that timing is useful because the overlap is specific, while both futures remain open.

They split on how to collect the money. Plan A auctions scarce capacity, while *The Three Crucibles* calls for AI labor taxation and distributed ownership. Our insurer's AI supplier buys permits in one map. In the other, replaced human work carries a social bill. I think Plan A gives government an easier place to collect. The book asks the harder question: after the dividend is paid, who owns the machines generating the wealth?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both documents use a Citizen's Dividend to reconnect automated production with human demand, and both point to Alaska. Plan A funds it through tradable permits for scarce capacity. The Three Crucibles pairs redistribution with AI labor taxation and distributed ownership.</p></div>

## Transparency can still leave you trapped

Plan A puts enormous weight on visibility. Major datacenter owners and chip suppliers declare large transactions, rival powers inspect infrastructure, and frontier AI research becomes transparent while inference stays private.

Then Appendix D stress-tests that bet. In its covert branch, China hides 500,000 H100-equivalents beside the Medog Hydropower Station, with around 200 people aware. The appendix feels like *Andor*: a vast administrative system can inspect almost everything and still miss a determined group working in the gaps. I think this is Plan A at its most honest. Verification narrows the room for cheating without closing it.

I don't know whether the bigger long-term danger is a hidden state project or a legal supplier that becomes impossible to leave. For our insurer, the second version looks boring enough to pass procurement. It moves claims data and decision history into one vendor's system. Renewal by renewal, switching becomes harder.

*The Three Crucibles* gives ownership more weight through open infrastructure and models, backed by legal guardrails against private monopoly. It says less about international inspection or how to keep a powerful model aligned.

At the insurer's board, the practical test fits in two questions:

<div class="callout">
<span class="callout__label">The insurer's exit test</span>
<p>Can we export our data, decision history, evaluation records and permissions in a usable form?</p>
<p>Can another provider run the service if this one fails or changes the deal?</p>
</div>

The [EU AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai) transparency rules began applying in August 2026, a year after its rules for general-purpose AI models became effective. Regulation tells the insurer what it must record and control. Exit rights decide whether it still has a choice.

I think this is where a 2040 debate becomes a 2026 contract question. The insurer meets the fork at renewal, inside clauses on data export and service continuity. The models will be impressive by then. The freedom to leave will be the valuable part.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A relies on transparency, declarations, inspections and controlled capacity. The Three Crucibles adds an ownership test: can an institution take its data and decision system elsewhere? For our insurer, that future arrives in a vendor contract before it arrives in 2040.</p></div>
