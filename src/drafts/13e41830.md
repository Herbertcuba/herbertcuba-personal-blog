In 1976, Alaska voters decided that part of the state’s mineral revenue should outlive the oil boom. They created the Alaska Permanent Fund, which invests that public money. Since 1982, its earnings have helped fund an annual cash dividend for eligible residents.

That old institution returns in [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/), a July 2026 proposal for navigating advanced artificial intelligence. It also appears in [my 2026 speculative book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), which was public on March 23, 2026, before Plan A appeared. The timing does not show that either map is right or that one influenced the other. The interesting part is their shared structure: both expect machine wealth to force a new argument about income, power, and ownership.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska shows how shared wealth can become a lasting citizen claim. Both works apply that question to an economy increasingly produced by machines.</p></div>

## What Plan A proposes

AI 2040 comes from the AI Futures Project, the research nonprofit behind *AI 2027*, its April 2025 scenario about rapid progress toward superhuman AI. The authors call Plan A “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” It describes a path they want governments to choose.

That path turns in 2029, when the United States and China reach an agreement that avoids a race into fully automated AI research, a route the authors believe could produce superintelligence in 2030. Superintelligence means systems far more capable than the best humans across intellectual work. Plan A instead allows AI to scale within the human range through 2035, pauses near the level of leading experts, and lifts the limits in 2040 after stronger oversight is in place.

Verification carries much of the plan. It means checking compliance by declaring and inspecting compute, the chips and data centres that provide AI’s processing capacity. The scenario then has AI doing a third of reasoning and knowledge work by mid-2031, while real gross domestic product, the inflation-adjusted value of economic output, grows by about 50 percent in 2032. Permit fees on advanced chips and robots fund a Citizen’s Dividend, a direct payment projected to rise from $45,000 per person in 2032 to around $1 million in 2035.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A tries to slow the jump beyond human ability, make national compliance checkable, and redirect part of AI-driven wealth to citizens.</p></div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

My book divides the transition into three social stages. **The Correction** begins when AI lowers the human labor needed to produce value, forcing companies, careers, and asset prices to adjust. **The Void** is the unstable passage in which paid work no longer distributes income or purpose reliably, while no replacement social contract is secure. **The Alignment** is the political choice that follows: whether abundance is broadly stewarded or captured by what the book calls “new feudal lords.”

The proposed responses include taxing AI labor, meaning machine work that replaces paid human tasks, using the proceeds for direct transfers, and spreading ownership of AI infrastructure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction breaks the old labor bargain, The Void exposes the missing replacement, and The Alignment decides who controls the abundance that follows.</p></div>

## Where the maps converge

The deepest agreement is economic. Both works expect automation to weaken the link between production and wages. That also weakens the tax base because governments rely heavily on taxes from human earnings. Output can keep rising while household income and consumer demand fall behind. As *The Three Crucibles* puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Apple’s smartphones are the example; the wider point is that machines can increase supply without replacing the households that create demand.

Their response is unusually specific. Both use the name Citizen’s Dividend, and both use Alaska to show how people can hold a direct claim on revenue from a shared productive asset. They also see the same political fork: wider distribution or lasting concentration of wealth and authority.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The maps converge on a broken wage system, a citizen claim on machine wealth, and the danger that abundance could harden into concentrated power.</p></div>

## Where the maps separate

Plan A places its main bet on coordination and verification. Its appendices test a hidden Chinese computing project; the authors estimate below a 10 percent chance that it could reach AI capable of dominating top human experts without detection before 2043. That low estimate supports their case that inspections and chip tracking can make secrecy difficult enough for a pause to hold.

*The Three Crucibles* places more weight on ownership. Chip inspections may slow a race, but they do not decide who receives the output. Distributed ownership and an AI-labor tax can spread that output, but they cannot reveal a hidden data centre or keep an advanced system under control. We may need both layers, and neither document proves that its preferred institutions will survive political pressure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A protects the transition through verifiable limits; The Three Crucibles protects its outcome through wider ownership. Each addresses a gap left by the other.</p></div>

## A map for preparation

No one knows whether AI will follow Plan A’s calendar, whether governments will cooperate, or whether labor will lose its economic role this quickly. Yet if AI 2040 and *The Three Crucibles* are directionally right, the road will be bumpy because technical capability will move faster than the institutions that distribute its gains and restrain its power.

That is the purpose of my book. It is a preparation document rather than a claim to certainty, written to name the social tests while choices remain open.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The dates may fail, but the distribution and power questions remain. Preparation matters because the future is unknown and the transition may be rough.</p></div>
