In 1976, Alaska voters made a decision about oil before they knew how valuable it might become. They amended the state constitution so that at least a quarter of certain mineral revenue entered the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/), whose principal is invested for the future. The first Permanent Fund Dividend was distributed in 1982. Together, the fund and dividend established a durable idea: citizens can keep a direct claim on wealth created from a shared resource.

Fifty years later, that idea appears in two documents about artificial intelligence. One is [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/), a detailed global scenario published in July 2026. The other is my 2026 speculative book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), whose framework predates Plan A. Their overlap does not prove either future, but it reveals the same question beneath the technology: who benefits when labor is no longer the main source of productive value?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska turned resource wealth into a lasting public claim. Both AI documents ask whether societies can do something similar with AI-created wealth.</p></div>

## What the two documents actually say

AI 2040 comes from the AI Futures Project, a nonprofit that studies advanced AI and recommends responses. It follows [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's earlier advanced-AI scenario, and all five of that scenario's listed authors contributed to Plan A. The new document calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” so its dates are conditional rather than settled facts.

Plan A imagines a United States–China agreement in 2029, a pause in 2035 when AI reaches the level of top human experts, and a controlled move in 2040 toward superintelligence: AI better, faster, and cheaper than the best humans across essentially all tasks. By mid-2031, AI performs a third of cognitive labor, meaning paid work based mainly on reasoning and information. In 2032, the scenario has economic output after inflation growing about 50 percent.

Its response includes chip tracking, data-center inspections, and permits for robots and compute, the specialized computing capacity used to train and run AI. It also proposes a Compute Dividend Corporation that gives every United States citizen one share and pays equal dividends from its profits.

My book describes the social transition through three crucibles:

1. **The Correction:** AI productivity shifts value from labor toward capital, weakening the old link between jobs and income.
2. **The Void:** that link is breaking, but society has not yet built a dependable claim on machine output.
3. **The Alignment:** abundance is either stewarded broadly or controlled by a small group of new feudal lords.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A supplies a dated technical and policy scenario. The crucibles describe the social passage from labor disruption to a choice about who governs abundance.</p></div>

## The agreement is about sequence

The first overlap is the labor economy. When AI systems do more paid work, wages and wage-based taxes can shrink as a share of output unless the rules change. Consumer demand then comes under pressure too. As my book puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.”

The second is the response. Both documents use “Citizen's Dividend” and point to Alaska. Plan A reaches it through permits and a citizen-owned corporation; my book emphasizes AI labor taxation and distributed ownership. Their mechanisms differ, but both give people a claim on the productive system rather than only relief after work disappears.

Finally, both arrive at concentrated power. AI 2040 fears loss of control and irreversible concentration; The Alignment asks whether people become stewards of abundance or subjects of its owners. This convergence supports the structure of the question, not the accuracy of either timetable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both documents move from weakened labor income to a citizen dividend and then to concentrated power. Their shared sequence matters more than agreement on a year.</p></div>

## Control and ownership are separate problems

Plan A concentrates on verification, meaning evidence that governments and companies respect agreed limits on AI capacity. Its covert-project appendix tests the blind spot. Under its scenario assumptions, the authors assign a below-10-percent chance that a hidden project reaches “Top-Expert-Dominating AI,” their label for systems above the best human experts, without detection before 2043.

Permits can help because each approved addition of robots or computing capacity becomes a recorded event that inspectors can check. Yet they can also deepen concentration if only the largest firms can afford them. Control may improve while ownership narrows.

That creates four possible futures. Broad ownership with credible control is the hopeful case; narrow ownership with weak control is the obvious disaster. The mixed cases matter too: tightly controlled AI can support a stable oligarchy, while broadly owned but poorly controlled AI can spread risk along with wealth. AI 2040 is strongest on control. The Three Crucibles is strongest on the public claim.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification can limit capability without sharing its gains, while broad ownership cannot make an unsafe system safe. A durable settlement needs both.</p></div>

## Preparation is the point

I do not know whether Plan A's dates will hold, whether its treaty could survive, or whether AI will replace labor that quickly. Nobody does. If AI performs nowhere near a third of cognitive labor by 2031, one central near-term claim will have failed in plain view.

That uncertainty is why my book is a preparation document rather than a promise. Decisions about what can be controlled and who owns the result become harder after capacity and wealth have concentrated. If AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles are broadly right, the passage ahead will be rough. We still have some say in its direction.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>No one knows which timeline will survive contact with reality. Both documents expose choices about control and ownership before they become emergencies.</p></div>
