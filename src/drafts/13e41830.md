I tried to read [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) as a distant forecast. By page 18, its authors had reached the same Citizen's Dividend I published in [The Three Crucibles](/books/the-three-crucibles/).

Four of the five authors behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/) returned for this 90-page scenario. They begin with a US-China deal meant to slow the race toward superintelligence. I began with two insurance companies fighting over one claims department.

Plan A's 2033 chapter is called "The Citizen's Dividend." My book uses the same name and the same Alaska Permanent Fund model. It has been public on my site since March 23. AI 2040 appeared in July.

The phrase is older than either document. I think the useful signal is independent arrival. A team focused on frontier labs and national security reached the same economic problem as an operator looking at automation inside a normal company.

<div class="article-tldr"><span class="article-tldr__label">Article TL;DR</span><p>AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles both expect automation to shrink labor income and push governments toward a Citizen's Dividend. Plan A funds it with permits for scarce compute and robots. My book starts with AI labor taxes and distributed ownership. The split becomes practical when a junior engineer builds an agent that must survive a vendor exit.</p></div>

## A damaged car contains the macro problem

One insurance claim is enough to make a national tax crisis visible.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The insurer in The Three Crucibles shows Plan A's tax problem at company scale. Agents remove salaries from the claims department, and the taxes tied to those salaries leave with them.</p></div>

The book compares two insurers. Company A keeps 5,000 claims adjusters and raises premiums to cover its costs. Company B puts an imperfect agentic system into production. It cuts headcount by 40 percent, drops premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months.

That is the First Crucible, the Correction, in a form you can see on an org chart. A competitor automates first. Everyone else has to respond because customers still compare prices.

Plan A runs the same pressure through the US tax system. Its 2032 chapter says personal income and payroll taxes brought in roughly ten times more federal revenue than corporate taxes in 2026. As paid work falls, that revenue falls. Companies can also deduct much of the spending on datacenters and robots. The report says the tax base would collapse as a share of GDP without reform.

The Second Crucible, the Transformation, starts when the claims system keeps producing while former workers have less money to spend. My book puts it bluntly: "Robots do not buy iPhones." The Third Crucible, the Alignment, asks who holds a claim on the output once machines do more of the work.

I think Company B is the useful starting point. You can reject Plan A's takeoff speed and still see the tax problem inside one automated department.

## The same dividend arrives through a different bill

On page 18, Plan A turns Company B's missing payroll into a permit fee.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A charges for scarce compute and robot capacity, then gives citizens a share of the fees. The Three Crucibles taxes AI labor after it produces value. The payment has the same job while the collection point changes.</p></div>

Plan A caps advanced compute and robot production. Companies bid for permits. In the scenario, those fees bring the US government $50 trillion in 2032. A fixed share flows into a Compute Dividend Corporation, with one share for every US citizen.

The authors compare it directly to the Alaska Permanent Fund. Alaska set aside part of its oil wealth in 1976 and began paying dividends to qualified residents in 1982. Plan A applies that resource logic to compute. Its projected payment starts at $45,000 per person in 2032 and reaches about $1 million in 2035. Those are scenario numbers, and the authors say their economics model is uncertain.

The Three Crucibles collects the money later. Its AI labor tax follows the work after an agent performs it. That ties the bill to business output, but somebody has to measure how much value the agent created. Plan A has the opposite problem: permit fees work only while chips and robots remain scarce enough to count.

I think the exact payment is the least durable part of the forecast. The stronger claim is that people need a legal share of machine output before wages stop doing the distribution work.

## Verification can find the machine. Ownership keeps the exit

Plan A's treaty works only while chips and datacenters stay countable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses declarations, inspections, and chip tracking to keep the race visible. The Third Crucible asks who can keep using the system and receiving its gains. Company B needs both visibility and an exit.</p></div>

Countries in Plan A declare compute. Inspectors enter datacenters. Chip sales are tracked through the small number of advanced fabrication plants. Total research transparency gives the other parties access to frontier training methods.

I think this is Plan A's strongest engineering move. It replaces trust with records, audits, and physical counts. The report also stress-tests its own idea. In one covert branch, Chinese intelligence diverts about 500,000 smuggled H100-equivalents into tunnels next to the Medog Hydropower Station. The authors estimate that only around 200 people would need to know.

That appendix exposes the weak point. A registry describes the machines people declare. It cannot make a hidden cluster declare itself.

The same gap appears inside Company B. The insurer may have perfect logs for every claim and still depend on one model provider that can raise prices, block access, or retire the model.

In *Alien*, the Nostromo crew eventually sees the threat. Weyland-Yutani still controls the mission through Ash and an order that treats the crew as expendable. Visibility gives them information. Control still sits elsewhere.

I don't know whether verification or ownership will break first. Ownership still needs governance. Someone has to keep the shareholder registry honest and enforce the payout rules. I think Plan A's registry and the Third Crucible's ownership rules work better together than either map does alone.

## The fork appears in your StateGraph

A junior engineer can give Company B an exit before the procurement team signs the model contract.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Audit logs show what a claims agent did. Portable state and tested fallback models keep the workflow alive when a provider changes its terms. That is distributed ownership at company scale.</p></div>

Suppose Company B uses ReAct for a damaged-car claim. ReAct is a loop: the model chooses an action, calls a tool, reads the result, and chooses again. It might read the policy, request a repair estimate, then send a large payment to a human reviewer.

LangGraph is a Python library that turns this loop into an explicit workflow. Its StateGraph connects the steps and passes a shared state between them. TypedDict is a Python type that tells the team which fields that state must contain:

```python
class ClaimState(TypedDict):
    claim_id: str
    repair_estimate: float
    model_id: str
    needs_human_review: bool
```

Each StateGraph node reads or updates those fields. The policy node adds coverage data. The estimate node adds the repair cost. A routing node checks `needs_human_review` before any large payment moves.

This small design choice turns [reversibility into an engineering constraint](/posts/reversibility-is-the-new-correctness/). Store the model ID with every decision. Keep the state in a format another provider can read. Run the same graph against a fallback model before production.

An eval is a repeatable test of agent behavior. Company B can save a set of old claims, expected tool calls, and allowed outcomes. The fallback model should pass that eval set before the primary vendor fails.

Put two tests in CI before signing the contract:

1. Run the same stored claims through a second model and compare the allowed outcomes.
2. Export the state and tool traces, then replay a claim without the primary provider's API.

The contract should also say what happens to the prompts, fine-tunes, and eval suite on exit. Your negotiating power peaks before signature. The code has to prove that the exit clause can work.

I think junior engineers should treat that exit path as a production requirement. Procurement can write the clause. Your graph has to make it true.

AI 2040 puts the fork in a treaty between nations. Your first version of it may be a fallback model in a claims graph this week.
