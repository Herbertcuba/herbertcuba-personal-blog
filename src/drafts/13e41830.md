Alaska made a decisive oil choice before the dividend existed. In 1976, while the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was being built to carry North Slope crude, voters created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/). The fund set aside part of the state's mineral income, and in 1982 it paid the first resident dividend. The public claim came before the money became routine.

Fifty years later, [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) returns to that precedent. Plan A is a policy recommendation told through a conditional future scenario by the AI Futures Project, the research nonprofit behind [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), an earlier scenario about rapidly advancing AI. Four of AI 2027's five authors also worked on Plan A. Its authors say plainly that Plan A is primarily a recommendation, not their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) was published on March 22, 2026, and the [PDF edition used here](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) is dated June 24. A [public link to Plan A appeared on July 9](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425). That sequence establishes timing, but it cannot establish influence, independence, or predictive accuracy. The tempting headline is external validation. Because a policy scenario is not observed data, I think the defensible claim is narrower and more useful: the two works converge on one economic mechanism, then expose different kinds of control. Verification governs hidden capacity, ownership allocates the gains, and exit proof tests whether an institution has bargaining power when renewal arrives.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040: Plan A is an AI Futures Project policy recommendation expressed through a conditional scenario, while The Three Crucibles is Herbert Cuba Garcia's speculative framework. Plan A does not validate the book's forecast; their thematic convergence instead reveals separate questions about inspecting AI computing capacity, owning a claim on its gains, and retaining the practical ability to leave an AI supplier.</p></div>

## The closest match sits inside the second crucible

The book has two first-party labels for its middle passage, so the edition matters. Its [current landing page](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) uses Transformation as the short label for the second crucible. The June 24 PDF, which carries the argument mapped here, names the three crucibles [Correction, Void, and Alignment](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6). Transformation is a chapter within that second passage, following the Void and the Silent Recovery.

[The Correction](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=19) predicts a financial reckoning in which inflated AI expectations meet uneven returns, weaker companies disappear, and survivors automate under pressure. Plan A starts elsewhere, with geopolitical rivalry and a possible race toward systems beyond human ability. It does not contain the book's market-correction mechanism. That is the first clear divergence.

The strongest convergence begins in the Void. The book describes an economy that recovers while many jobs do not, then [the Transformation chapter](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36) follows the consequence into household demand: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Plan A reaches a similar problem through a different route. In its scenario, AI performs [one third of all cognitive labor by mid-2031](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=13), meaning work based mainly on analysis, information, communication, and judgment.

[By 2032 in that scenario](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17), fewer paid human roles shrink income and payroll-tax receipts. Companies also invest heavily in data centers, factories, and robots, then deduct or depreciate those costs, so corporate tax does not automatically replace the lost revenue. Output can rise while public revenue and household demand weaken. The two works therefore agree on the broken loop: when machines perform more work, wages stop distributing enough purchasing power and labor taxes stop funding enough of the state.

Both answer with a Citizen's Dividend, a payment that gives the public a claim on AI-driven productivity. [*The Three Crucibles* proposes an AI Labor Equivalence Tax](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37) based on the taxes and social contributions a person in the equivalent role would have generated. [Plan A instead auctions tradable permits](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17) for new robot or computing capacity. A fixed share of the permit fees goes to a Compute Dividend Corporation, in which every US citizen receives one share and profits are divided equally. [The scenario projects](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18) $45,000 per American adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035, in real 2025 dollars.

I think this overlap matters because it is unusually specific: the same demand problem, the same Alaska precedent, and the same policy name. The funding designs still encode different ideas. One taxes labor replaced by AI; the other charges for permission to expand physical capacity. The convergence strengthens the economic question without proving either timetable.

The AI Futures Project's conditional Plan A scenario does not share the book's Correction, but it closely echoes the Void-to-Transformation income loop: automated knowledge work weakens wages, demand, and labor-tax revenue. Both propose a Citizen's Dividend, meaning a public payment from AI-related gains, while using different funding mechanisms.</p></div>

## The maps disagree about control

Plan A's central lever is takeoff governance. Takeoff is the period when AI automates enough AI research to accelerate its own improvement, making the race much faster and harder to control. [In Plan A's preferred scenario](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=4), the United States and China reach a deal in 2029 that prevents fully automated AI research from producing superintelligence by the end of 2030. Superintelligence means systems that outperform the best humans across fields while working faster and more cheaply. Development continues within the human range, pauses at top-human-expert ability in 2035, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. Every date here belongs to the scenario.

[The deal relies on verification](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=8): evidence that countries are following agreed limits. Major chip and data-center transactions are declared, rival powers inspect infrastructure, and AI research becomes almost entirely transparent. Plan A even stress-tests its own system with [an alternate branch](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45) involving roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents hidden in tunnels beside China's Medog Hydropower Station, with only about 200 people aware. An H100-equivalent is a rough measure of AI computing capacity using the H100 AI processor as a reference, not a literal chip count.

This is where *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film about intelligent machines hiding physical domination behind a simulated world, becomes useful. Its green code looks weightless, but the machine world requires industrial hardware and energy. Advanced AI has the same physical shadow. Chips, electricity, cooling, and buildings can leave evidence for inspectors even when software and intentions remain concealed.

[The Alignment](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=56), the book's third crucible, asks a different question: who owns the machines and distributes their output once AI becomes ordinary infrastructure? Plan A gives each citizen a legal share in the dividend corporation, not direct ownership of the chips, models, or companies producing the value. The book argues for AI infrastructure distributed among individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, including models that can run locally or on an owner's premises.

I think each map sees what the other places in the background. Plan A examines international coordination, technical loss of control, and the difficulty of detecting secret capacity. The book puts more weight on who can use AI infrastructure, claim its output, and bargain over access. I don't know which balance can survive geopolitical pressure. Yet inspection and ownership plainly govern different relationships: one tests compliance with a rule, while the other assigns economic rights and practical agency.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A, the AI Futures Project's conditional policy scenario, tries to constrain rapidly self-accelerating AI development through declared computing capacity, inspections, and transparent research. Alignment, the ownership-and-power test in Herbert Cuba Garcia's 2026 book The Three Crucibles, asks who owns AI infrastructure and receives its gains. Inspection can reveal rule-breaking; ownership determines who can use the system and bargain over its output.</p></div>

## A contract can test the missing power

A third boundary appears when these civilizational maps reach an ordinary institution. [*The Three Crucibles* uses a thought experiment](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21) about two insurers. One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; the other deploys coordinated AI agents, software systems that divide and execute the claims process, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. These are illustrative numbers rather than a reported case, showing how competitive pressure can make adoption move faster than a buyer's ability to leave.

My take is that a critical AI contract should require exit proof before renewal. Exit proof is a supplier-funded handover test showing that a replacement team can run one agreed business process. A useful first scope is one claims workflow, one portable export of the relevant records, one replacement team, and a 30-day window. The chief information officer should own the renewal decision, while the buyer owns the historical cases and expected outcomes used to evaluate the replacement.

The handover package should include versioned workflow settings, model instructions, tool connections, permissions, source records, restoration guidance, and data lineage, which records where each input came from and how it changed. The replacement may change the model, instructions, how relevant records are selected and supplied, or the workflow design. What must remain stable is the agreed business result on the buyer's evaluation cases, within fixed limits for error, time, and cost.

Failure also needs a contractual meaning. Missing records, blocked access, outcomes outside the agreed limits, or decisions that cannot be traced to their sources should fail the test. That result would not prove migration impossible forever. It would show that the buyer has not demonstrated a workable exit on the agreed terms, so automatic renewal should stop, funded transition assistance should begin, and a failed retest should allow departure without a termination fee. I think the leverage exists before the contract is signed.

Regulation addresses a related boundary. Since August 2, 2025, the [European Union's AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the bloc's 2024 risk-based AI law, has applied rules for general-purpose AI models, which can perform many different tasks and be integrated into other systems. Most of the law began applying on August 2, 2026, including transparency duties for specified AI interactions and AI-generated or manipulated content. Those duties can improve visibility, but a compliant disclosure does not demonstrate that a buyer can transfer a working process. A failed handover despite compliant disclosure is evidence of current supplier dependence.

Neither Plan A nor the book develops this institutional test. Plan A concentrates on states seeing hidden capacity, while the book concentrates on distributing ownership and income. But a hospital, insurer, or public agency can receive transparent information and still be unable to exercise a credible exit. That is where bargaining power fails next.

Alaska secured a public claim before oil income became routine. With AI, I think we need to make three questions concrete while the terms remain open: can hidden capacity be inspected, who holds the economic claim, and can the work move when a supplier relationship ends? Control remains borrowed until the answer to the last question has been tested.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof, proposed here as a supplier-funded handover test before renewal, asks whether a buyer can move one working process to a replacement team within agreed limits for error, time, and cost. Neither the AI Futures Project's conditional Plan A policy scenario nor Herbert Cuba Garcia's 2026 book The Three Crucibles supplies this institutional test. A failed test does not prove permanent lock-in, but it does show that current transparency and contract terms have not produced a demonstrated exit.</p></div>
