On March 23, 2026, a download link for [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my book about the economic and political stages of an AI transition, appeared on my site. The book page followed on June 1, and the PDF is dated June 24. [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), the AI Futures Project's policy scenario for advanced AI, became public on July 9.

The texts reach the same economic break from different starting points. The AI Futures Project also produced [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), an April 2025 scenario in which AI automates research on better AI; four of its five front-page authors return for *AI 2040*. The dates establish sequence, but neither influence nor predictive success.

Plan A calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction” and says it is not the authors' best guess. It uses a conditional future to stress-test policy. I think the defensible claim is narrower than validation: a separate forecasting group reached the book's economic structure, then trusted a different institutional mechanism.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 is a conditional policy scenario, not confirming data. Its convergence with The Three Crucibles reveals a shared economic diagnosis and a consequential disagreement about control.</p></div>

## The same break appears on different clocks

Plan A turns on a proposed 2029 agreement between the United States and China. Page 4 says the deal prevents fully automated AI research in 2030, meaning systems doing the work needed to design better AI. They then improve within human range, pause around the level of the best researchers in 2035, and scale in 2040 to superintelligence: systems significantly better than the best humans at everything, while also faster and cheaper.

*The Three Crucibles* uses a social clock. Its public page names The Correction in 2026, The Transformation from 2027 to 2032, and The Alignment from 2035 onward, meaning the political settlement over who owns and governs abundance. The PDF's opening map calls the middle period “The Void,” then describes transformation within it. Labor loses bargaining power, income distribution is rebuilt, and society chooses between stewardship and concentrated ownership.

The strongest convergence is economic. On page 17, Plan A imagines roughly 50 percent growth in inflation-adjusted output in 2032 while income- and payroll-tax receipts shrink because machines perform work previously assigned to paid people. Corporate tax does not automatically replace that revenue: companies can reinvest in datacenters, factories, and robots, then deduct or depreciate the spending before tax. High output and a weak tax base can arrive together.

The book gives the demand-side version in one line: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Plan A caps computing and robot capacity, auctions tradable permits, and directs a growing share of the fees into a Citizen's Dividend. Computing capacity means the chips and datacenters used to train and run AI. On pages 17–18, scenario permit revenue reaches $50 trillion in fiscal year 2032, and the dividend starts at $45,000 per American adult; the report calls its model uncertain.

Both works cite the Alaska Permanent Fund. Alaska created it through a 1976 constitutional amendment to save part of the state's mineral revenue, then began paying residents annual dividends in 1982. Plan A proposes a Compute Dividend Corporation with one share per U.S. citizen; *The Three Crucibles* describes a Citizen's Dividend as a direct transfer from AI infrastructure's productivity. I think the shared name and precedent matter. They do not prove influence, but they trace the same route from automated labor toward a public claim on productive assets.

Their funding methods differ. Plan A sells permission to build scarce capacity; the book proposes a post-deployment AI labor tax and broader ownership. One captures scarcity at the gate, while the other captures value once machines are working.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both works trace automation to weaker wage taxes and consumer demand, then to a public claim on machine-created value. Plan A uses capacity permits; The Three Crucibles uses AI labor taxation and distributed ownership.</p></div>

## The disagreement begins at the institution

Plan A's answer is verification. Governments would declare advanced chips, inspect datacenters, share AI research across approved projects, and require safety cases: evidence-backed arguments that a new AI system will not cause an irreversible catastrophe. The machinery is meant to slow a dangerous race without asking the United States and China to trust each other's promises.

Appendix D, on pages 45–47, supplies the warning. In that scenario branch, China diverts about 500,000 H100-equivalents, a measure of processing capacity relative to Nvidia's H100 AI chip, into a covert datacenter beside the Medog Hydropower Station. The dam provides eventual power and construction cover, while tunnels hide waste heat from satellites. About 200 people know of it. I think this is the report's hardest stress test: extensive verification remains vulnerable to undeclared capacity.

*The Three Crucibles* is stronger where Plan A is thinner. Page 57 names distributed AI ownership, meaning infrastructure and AI models kept open rather than monopolized; a universal basic income, or regular public cash payments without a work requirement, funded, tested, and scaling by 2028; and AI labor taxation. Its named AI Labor Equivalence Tax would charge companies for each human-equivalent unit of AI labor deployed. That goes beyond a dividend by asking how the productive layer itself is held. These ideas cannot detect a secret datacenter or prove a model safe, while an inspection regime can pass every audit on declared sites without giving citizens a durable right to the wealth.

My take is that technical control protects the boundary, while ownership determines who benefits from it. I don't know whether hidden capacity or concentrated ownership will break the arrangement first. Plan A's own scenario gives us reason to treat both as live failure modes.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification and ownership solve different problems. Monitoring can miss covert AI capacity, while transparent development can still concentrate economic power; distributed ownership can spread gains, but it cannot make an unsafe system controllable.</p></div>

## Evidence has to match authority

Companies and states hold different powers. A chief financial officer cannot create a citizen's legal claim on national AI wealth, while a legislature cannot negotiate one company's software exit clause. Strategy has to keep those levels separate.

The European Union shows the boundary. The bulk of the EU AI Act, its risk-based law for AI systems, began to apply on August 2, 2026; obligations for companies providing broadly reusable AI models had already been in force since August 2, 2025. For a Swedish organization covered by the law, this creates compliance duties but no citizen claim on AI-generated wealth. That requires a separate law, ownership instrument, or contract.

At country level, a designated regulator should own the capacity record and stop power. The record must set the trigger amount, operator, authorized capacity, inspection method, and maximum undeclared capacity the regime can miss. A legislature should create the public claim; a treasury or equivalent body should record the revenue source, beneficiaries, payment formula, audit method, and who may change the rules. Capacity above the miss limit defeats the control claim. A captured or quietly rewritten payout defeats the ownership claim.

A company board has a narrower job. Before procurement, technology and legal teams can freeze the task, baseline period, comparison unit, minimum improvement, error ceiling, required evidence, and contractual stop right. Finance, human resources, and worker representatives can record where verified gains will go, such as pay, shorter hours at the same salary, training, or lower prices. This is company-level gain sharing, not public ownership unless a law or ownership instrument makes it so.

For customer-support AI, compare correctly resolved cases per paid hour and complaint rates over equivalent six-month periods. Set the improvement floor and error ceiling before launch. More tickets touched cannot excuse a missed threshold, and the distribution promise fails if named recipients do not receive their agreed share by the review date.

I think this is where the comparison becomes falsifiable. Before launch, group comparable programs by process type and investment band. By 2032, compare how often programs with both deployment evidence and a gain-sharing commitment meet their operating thresholds and distribution promises against programs documenting only one side. If the combined approach does not improve both measures, the framework has lost strategic value. “We could not observe the failure” cannot count as success.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Governments need auditable records for controlled capacity and enforceable public claims. Companies need frozen deployment evidence and gain-sharing commitments within powers they actually hold. If the combined approach cannot improve both durable deployment and delivered gains by 2032, discard it; control without a protected claim leaves the central political risk intact.</p></div>
