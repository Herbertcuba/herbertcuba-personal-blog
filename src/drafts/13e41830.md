AI can keep production rising while the income attached to human work falls. Two documents published months apart reach that economic fork from very different directions.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a 2026 policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, a forecasting group focused on advanced AI. Four of its authors also wrote [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), a 2025 scenario about the race toward systems beyond top human expertise. Plan A proposes an international route for governing superintelligence, meaning AI far more capable than the best people across most intellectual work, but its authors are careful: it is a recommendation rather than their best-guess forecast.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) is my book about the passage through advanced automation. Its PDF appeared in my [author-controlled public repository](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) on March 23, 2026, and the edition used here is dated June 24. A [public link to Plan A](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) followed on July 9. Those dates establish a public sequence; they cannot tell us when either idea began in private.

Both works call their proposed payment from AI wealth a Citizen's Dividend. Both also point to the Alaska Permanent Fund, a public savings fund created by constitutional amendment in 1976 to preserve part of the state's oil wealth. It began paying residents annual dividends in 1982, turning a natural resource into a shared financial claim. I think the overlap matters because the two works connect the same economic cause to the same institutional response. Their disagreement matters more: Plan A concentrates on proving what powerful actors are building, while the book concentrates on who owns the productive systems and receives their returns. A third test appears when those questions reach an ordinary institution: can it actually leave its AI supplier?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two maps share an economic diagnosis and a dividend mechanism, but they expose different ways power can escape accountability.</p></div>

## The maps meet in the Void

The [opening map in the June 24 edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment. The Correction is the period when AI makes capital more productive and human labor less valuable. The Void opens when production can recover without restoring jobs, leaving households with less income to buy what the machines produce. Inside that second crucible, the chapter titled Transformation follows the response: a Citizen's Dividend transfers part of the return from AI infrastructure back to the people who sustain demand. The Alignment then asks who owns the machines and who distributes the abundance.

Plan A puts scenario numbers on much of the same chain. By mid-2031, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor, work based mainly on analysis, communication, and professional judgment. As employment falls, personal-income and payroll-tax revenue shrinks. The United States in the scenario therefore sells permits to build new computing capacity and robots, then directs most of that revenue to a Citizen's Dividend projected at $45,000 per adult in 2032 and about $1 million in 2035 ([PDF pages 17–18](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=17)).

The funding mechanisms differ. [The book proposes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=37) a charge for each human-equivalent unit of deployed AI labor, meaning the amount of work a person would otherwise perform. Plan A charges for permission to add new machine capacity. Yet both begin with the same demand problem. As the book puts it, “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Cheap production cannot support an economy if households lose the income needed to buy its output. I think this is meaningful thematic convergence, not one-to-one validation, and it does not depend on accepting Plan A's timetable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Void and Plan A follow automation through lost labor income to a Citizen's Dividend, while proposing different ways to collect the money that funds it.</p></div>

## Verification cannot settle ownership

Here the documents stop mapping the same terrain. Plan A is organized around takeoff, the period when AI-driven research could improve AI capabilities faster than governments can respond. In its scenario, a US-China agreement in 2029 blocks a route to superintelligence by the end of 2030; systems then scale within the human range, pause at top-expert level in 2035, and resume toward superintelligence in 2040. *The Three Crucibles* does not model that technical race or the risk of systems escaping human control. It takes advanced automation as the setting and follows what happens to work, demand, and ownership.

Plan A's main control is verification: evidence that reported chips, data centers, and research on frontier AI, the most capable systems under development, match reality. It proposes declarations for major chip and data-center transactions, tracking for advanced chips, reciprocal inspections of infrastructure, and almost entirely transparent research. These measures use a simple constraint. A large AI project may conceal its purpose, but it still needs visible supplies of computing hardware, electricity, cooling, and construction.

[Plan A's Appendix D](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=45), a scenario analysis of how China might try to evade that inspection regime, imagines a covert data center in tunnels beside the proposed Medog Hydropower Station. Its 500,000 H100-equivalents are a way of expressing computing power in units comparable to Nvidia's H100 AI chip. Within the scenario, the authors put the chance of this project producing AI better than top human experts across domains without detection before 2043 below 10 percent.

The physical logic resembles the Death Star program in *Rogue One*, the 2016 *Star Wars* film. The fictional Empire can hide the purpose of its planet-destroying superweapon, but the project's vast construction and energy needs leave an industrial trail. Plan A makes the same bet about secret AI development: inspectors may miss intent, yet still find the footprint.

The Alignment in *The Three Crucibles* addresses a separate danger. A data center can be declared, inspected, and legal while its control and income remain concentrated. [Distributed ownership in the book](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57) means spreading rights to AI capacity or its returns across many people and institutions instead of a small group. Open infrastructure and open models mean that multiple operators can run the technology. Laws against a private intelligence monopoly would stop a few owners from controlling both the systems on which the economy depends and the wealth those systems produce.

My take is that inspection can make concealed capacity harder to build, while ownership determines where the gains from visible capacity go. I don't know which will face the harder test, and neither document can settle that in advance. I think either control on its own leaves a route back to concentrated power.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A makes secrecy harder by tying declarations to physical evidence. The book asks the question inspection cannot answer: who controls lawful AI capacity and receives what it earns?</p></div>

## The missing test appears at renewal

The control problem becomes concrete at the contract desk. *The Three Crucibles* uses a fictional comparison between two insurers: one keeps 5,000 claims adjusters, while the other deploys a coordinated AI system, cuts headcount by 40 percent, reduces premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. It is a thought experiment rather than a reported company case. Its point is that competitive pressure can force adoption before ownership and governance are settled.

Even an inspected system with broadly shared returns can leave one organization dependent on a single provider. The supplier may control the working setup, data connections, permissions, and tool behavior that keep a service alive. I call the practical test of that dependence exit proof: a bounded handover in which a replacement team must reproduce one working AI service within agreed limits.

At renewal, the chief information officer should require the supplier to fund a 30-day handover of one live workflow to an independent replacement team. The buyer should own the evaluation set, a small collection of past cases whose accepted outcomes define what “working” means. The data export must contain the records needed to run those cases and show where important inputs came from and how they changed along the way.

The supplier must also provide a dated package identifying the model version, the persistent instructions that guide its behavior, its source connections, permissions, connected tools, and the steps needed to rebuild the service. The replacement team may change the model, the instructions, how the system finds source material, or the workflow itself, provided it preserves the agreed business result. Missing the deadline, error limit, or cost ceiling means the test has failed. The contract should then block automatic renewal, keep access available during transition, and require supplier-funded repair and handover support.

Since August 2, 2026, Article 50 of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's law for AI systems, has required disclosure for certain AI interactions and certain AI-generated or manipulated content. That can reveal when AI is involved, but it cannot prove that a working service will survive a supplier change. I think a failed handover is stronger evidence of dependence than a compliant disclosure is evidence of freedom.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Make renewal conditional on a funded, timed handover with buyer-owned test cases and clear limits for cost and errors. If a replacement team cannot reproduce the business result, the buyer's right to leave exists only on paper.</p></div>
