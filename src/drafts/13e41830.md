In 1976, Alaska used a constitutional amendment to put part of its oil revenue into a permanent fund rather than spend it all. The first dividend in 1982 gave eligible residents a direct cash claim on income from that shared resource.

That mechanism reappears in [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/), a 2026 policy scenario from the AI Futures Project. Four of its six authors also wrote *AI 2027*, a 2025 scenario about a rapid race to advanced AI. Plan A is a recommendation, not the authors' best-guess forecast. It proposes a Citizen's Dividend, a recurring cash payment funded by fees on AI infrastructure, and explicitly models it on Alaska.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my book about three stages of AI-driven economic change, reaches the same mechanism under the same name. A [public repository record](https://github.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/commit/ce380abb4f4f13f5fc660a2c2e3a6a75011184a8) places its PDF on my site on March 23, 2026, and the fixed edition cited here is dated June 24; a [public link to *AI 2040*](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) followed on July 9. This establishes public sequence, not private origin or influence.

I think the useful claim is narrower than validation. The two public works follow the same economic chain, then divide over control. Plan A uses verification, meaning declarations and inspections, to expose hidden computing capacity: the chips, data centers, power, and supporting infrastructure used to develop advanced AI. The book asks who owns that infrastructure and its output. I add exit proof, a funded handover that shows whether another team can run a critical workflow before a supplier contract renews.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works reach the same Citizen's Dividend through a shared economic chain. Their public dates establish sequence, while their different control mechanisms make the comparison useful.</p></div>

## The three crucibles meet Plan A

The [fixed June edition](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=6) names the Correction, the Void, and the Alignment. The Correction is the first crash, when AI hype collapses and the market protection keeping less efficient human jobs in place disappears. The Void follows as AI starts producing real value without needing as much human work: the economy recovers, but many jobs do not. Inside the Void, the Transformation chapter connects that loss of labor income to weaker demand: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Its policy response is a [Citizen's Dividend that transfers part of AI infrastructure income to consumers](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=36). The Alignment asks whether AI infrastructure and models will be broadly owned or controlled by a narrow group.

In Plan A's scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, meaning work based on analysis, research, communication, or judgment. As jobs disappear, personal-income and payroll-tax revenue weakens. The scenario then sells tradable permits to build new robots and AI computing capacity, directing most permit fees to a Citizen's Dividend projected at [$45,000 per American adult in 2032](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18).

The book proposes a different funding rule: an AI Labor Equivalence Tax on each unit of machine work measured against comparable human work. The mapping is therefore precise but incomplete. The Correction converges with Plan A's labor diagnosis, the Void and its Transformation chapter converge with the dividend response, and the Alignment converges with the concern about concentrated power. Their speed, tax instrument, and governing machinery differ. I think that is meaningful thematic convergence, not proof that either timeline will happen.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both works connect automated work to weaker wages, taxes, and consumer demand. The book taxes deployed machine labor; Plan A charges for permission to add robots and computing capacity.</p></div>

## Verification does not settle ownership

Plan A centers on takeoff, a feedback loop in which AI automates AI research and speeds the creation of more capable systems. In its scenario, a 2029 agreement between the United States and China avoids a route to superintelligence by the end of 2030. Superintelligence means systems that exceed humans across almost all intellectual work. Development pauses in 2035 at top-expert capability, where AI can match leading human specialists on the relevant intellectual tasks, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040.

The agreement makes major chip and data-center transactions visible, opens infrastructure to reciprocal inspection, and makes almost all AI research public while keeping the use of existing models private. Its covert-project appendix stress-tests that system with a secret Chinese data center built in tunnels beside the proposed Medog Hydropower Station in Tibet. In the scenario, the dam would supply power, while the tunnels make heat harder for satellites to detect. Like *The Matrix*, the 1999 science-fiction film whose virtual world depends on physical machines, advanced AI still leaves a trail of chips, electricity, cooling, construction, and people.

I think that trail makes inspection plausible, but visibility does not settle distribution. Plan A gives each US citizen one share in a corporation that receives part of the permit revenue, creating a claim on income. The [Alignment calls for distributed ownership](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=57) of the infrastructure and models themselves, backed by laws against a private intelligence monopoly. Plan A offers far more detail on treaties and AI safety; the book presses an ownership question that a revenue share only partly answers. I don't know whether Plan A's verification regime could survive sustained US-China rivalry, and its authors do not claim certainty.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification can expose hidden AI development because infrastructure leaves physical evidence. A dividend can spread income, but ownership of the models and machines remains a separate source of power.</p></div>

## Exit proof makes control practical

Even when governments can see capacity and citizens hold revenue claims, an institution can still lose practical choice. A company may own final documents while depending on its AI supplier for usable data, permissions, settings, connected tools, and operating knowledge. Exit proof turns that dependence into a test: can a replacement team run the workflow before renewal?

The book's fictional insurer example makes the pressure concrete. One insurer keeps 5,000 claims adjusters, the people who investigate claims and decide payments. A rival deploys AI, cuts staff by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months ([printed p. 20](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Herbertcuba/herbertcuba-personal-blog/3bd6772f634c1c5dbeef60a71d012532302aae6c/src/static/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf#page=21)). The numbers are illustrative, but the rush they create is familiar: sign first, ask how to leave later.

I would make renewal conditional on a supplier-funded, 30-day handover of one workflow and one complete data export to one replacement team. The handover package should include where the data came from and how it changed, transferable permissions, saved versions of settings and prompts, working connections to other tools, and operating and recovery instructions. The buyer owns the evaluation set, a fixed sample of past cases with accepted outcomes. The replacement may change the model, prompts, way it finds supporting information, or internal workflow, but it must reproduce the business outcome within agreed limits for errors, time, and cost.

The chief information officer owns acceptance. Missing inputs or results outside the limits fail the test, block automatic renewal, and trigger supplier-funded correction and transition support. That consequence matters because a promise that the system can be moved gives the buyer little power unless failure changes the contract.

Since August 2, 2026, [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_50), the European Union's main AI law, has required disclosure in certain AI interactions and marks that software can detect on some AI-generated or manipulated content. Those rules can reveal AI use. They cannot supply the data, permissions, and operating knowledge another team needs.

I think exit proof completes the control picture. Verification governs hidden capacity, ownership allocates the gains, and a successful handover proves that an institution still has bargaining power. A failed handover predicts supplier lock-in, the point at which changing provider has become impractical, even when every transparency requirement has been met.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof makes a supplier fund a bounded handover before renewal. A failed test blocks automatic renewal and starts funded correction and transition support while the buyer still has time to act.</p></div>
