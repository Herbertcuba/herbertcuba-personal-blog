In 1976, Alaska made an unusual deal with itself. The oil at Prudhoe Bay belonged to everyone in the state, so a cut of every barrel went into a permanent fund. Since 1982, every Alaska resident has received a yearly check from it. Fifty years later, the fund is the most boring success story in American policy.

In July, the AI Futures Project published [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/): ninety pages of scenario modeling and policy engineering from the team that wrote [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/). Their proposal for the age of superintelligence: a fixed fraction of compute permit fees flows into a "Compute Dividend Corporation," owned one share per citizen, profits paid out as dividends. Their own words: "This is similar to the setup of the Alaska Permanent Fund, except at a much larger scale." Their 2033 chapter is titled "The Citizen's Dividend."

[The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) carries the same mechanism under the same name, public on my site since March.

The shared name is a footnote. Where their map and mine stop agreeing, that is where the actual decision lives.

<div class="article-tldr">
<span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span>
<p>The AI 2027 team just modeled, in public, the ownership economics I wrote about from the operator side: collapsing labor taxes, rationed compute, a citizen's dividend. Where we part is the control question. They bet on verifying the machines. I bet on settling who owns them. Both maps agree on one practical sentence: the leverage only exists before you sign.</p>
</div>

## The deal

Plan A is the optimistic branch from the people who wrote the pessimistic one.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 is the AI 2027 team's positive scenario: a US-China deal, total research transparency, a controlled scale-up, a pause at top-expert-level AI in 2035. The scenario arithmetic compresses most of the labor transformation into three years.</p></div>

AI 2027 ended in a mad scramble to superintelligence, extinction or irreversible concentration of power. Their footnote one concedes the point: "So far reality is tracking closer to AI 2027 than even we expected."

The scenario draws on direct OpenAI experience and conversations inside the frontier labs. Its spine is a US-China agreement in 2029 to avoid a reckless race. In 2030 the leading US company grudgingly pauses, and total research transparency becomes the price of the deal. The world scales deliberately inside the human range until 2035, pausing at top-expert-level AI, the maximum they believe they can control. In 2040, aligned systems take the torch.

I think the arithmetic is striking even inside the scenario's own hedges. Real GDP growth hits [about 50% in 2032](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf). World compute goes from roughly 20 million H100-equivalents (a standard unit for counting AI computing power) in 2026 to 60 billion by 2034. The AI share of the labor economy climbs from about 20% in 2032 to about 85% by 2035.

Run that last pair again. A three-year window in which paid human work goes from four-fifths of the economy to one-seventh. The plan is for civilization. The arithmetic is for a claims department.

## Where the maps agree

The overlap runs through the whole economic layer of the scenario.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The maps agree on the economics: the tax base collapses as labor is automated, the state ends up rationing compute and robot capacity, and permit fees flow back to citizens as a dividend. AI 2040 reaches this from alignment risk. The Three Crucibles reached it from the enterprise side.</p></div>

Start with the tax base. In the scenario, federal revenue collapses as a fraction of GDP: firms expense the machines that replaced the workers, and taxes on labor income, roughly ten times larger than corporate taxes today, evaporate as headcount shrinks. In my book, the same collapse is the First Crucible: the Correction, the moment Productivity = Capital becomes quarterly reality. Labor solves to zero, and an economy that taxes labor is an economy with no revenue.

Then the fix. Their mechanism is permits. Robot and compute production gets capped, permits are sold to the highest bidder, and the fees become federal revenue: roughly $50 trillion in FY2032, climbing toward [$180 trillion by 2034](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf). A fixed fraction is legally owned by the Compute Dividend Corporation, every citizen holds one share, and the dividend starts at $45,000 per person in 2032 and climbs to about $1M by 2035. In the book, the same step is the Second Crucible: UBI arrives, framed not as welfare but as systemic lubrication, because robots do not buy iPhones. An automated economy without a distribution channel seizes. The dividend is the channel.

Side by side, the overlap looks like this:

| | AI 2040: Plan A (July 2026) | The Three Crucibles (public since March) |
|---|---|---|
| Tax base | Labor taxes evaporate as headcount shrinks | First Crucible: the Correction, labor solves to zero |
| Distribution | Compute Dividend Corporation, one share per citizen, $45,000 to $1M | Second Crucible: the Citizen's Dividend, the channel that keeps an automated economy moving |
| Entry point | Alignment risk, modeled from the lab side | Enterprise reality, written from the operator side |

I think the convergence matters more than either document does on its own. When the people whose job is worrying about misaligned superintelligence independently rebuild the ownership structure you sketched from the boardroom side, that structure is probably carrying real weight.

## Where the maps disagree

The fork is the word "deal."

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 bets on verification: declared compute, inspected datacenters, tracked chips. Its own covert-project appendix shows a defecting state running a crash program with roughly 200 people in the know. The fork is governance versus ownership: police the machines, or settle who owns them.</p></div>

AI 2040's plan rests on verification. Compute gets declared. Datacenters get inspected, because training clusters are visible from space and need conspicuous power infrastructure. Chips get tracked from the handful of fabs that can make them. The whole architecture assumes the chokepoint holds.

Their own appendix stress-tests that assumption, and the stress test is sobering. In the covert-project branch, Chinese intelligence quietly diverts about 500,000 smuggled H100-equivalents, 17% of what smugglers bring into China in 2028. The stockpile ends up in a covert datacenter built in the tunnels next to the Medog hydropower dam in Tibet, a dam with three times the power of Three Gorges. Their best guess is that a project like this could run with only around 200 humans aware of it. A superintelligence program with the headcount of a mid-sized startup.

<div class="scifi">
<span class="scifi__label">Meanwhile, in sci-fi</span>
<p class="scifi__film">2001: A Space Odyssey, Stanley Kubrick (1968)</p>
<p>Early in the film a monolith appears and the hominids around it change. Kubrick never explains the mechanism. He shows the before and the after, and trusts you to see that the tool rewired the user. The datacenter under the Medog dam is that scene rendered in concrete: every governance structure in the scenario exists to prevent the room, and every economic structure in both documents answers what happens when rooms like it run the world. You can police the machine, or you can rewire what its output pays for. The rewiring is the part that lasts.</p>
</div>

Their map says the chokepoint of physical compute is verifiable enough to bet civilization on, provided inspections, buybacks, and satellite monitoring all hold. My map says any chokepoint that profitable gets routed around, and the durable question is who owns the machines. Governance is a bet on process, and process erodes under pressure. Ownership is a bet on what remains when the process fails. When labor no longer buys you a share of the output, ownership is the only remaining claim on it.

I do not know which of us is right about the verification regime. I think the honest read of their covert-project math is that neither do they. They wrote ninety pages on Plan A and then an entire appendix on how it fails.

## The fork at the contract desk

Pull this down from treaty level to where it lands in practice, because the same fork shows up in ordinary procurement.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The governance-versus-ownership fork lands in procurement: what you ask vendors before signing, and what you can still reclaim after. Ask both questions while you can still walk away from the table.</p></div>

In the book I used a small thought experiment. Two insurers, same liquidity squeeze. Company A tries to remain humane, keeps its five thousand claims adjusters, raises premiums to cover costs. Company B forces its agentic swarm into production, software agents doing the adjusting work end to end. Imperfect but functional. Cuts headcount forty percent, drops premiums twenty. Company B crushes Company A in six months. In recession, customers pay for value. Humanity gets priced out.

That is the 20% to 85% labor curve translated into an org chart. The macro numbers in AI 2040 read like science fiction until you put them in a claims department. Then they read like next year's renewal conversation.

The fork shows up at contract scale too. A model two years behind the frontier, running on hardware you own, beats a superintelligence you rent. Under the AI 2040 map, you ask your vendors which treaty obligations their training pipeline is subject to and who attests to it, because attestation claims will start appearing in procurement language, and somebody has to verify the verifier's marketing. Under the ownership map, you ask where the model weights live when the vendor's incentives turn, and what happens to your fine-tunes and eval suites on exit, in what format and under which clause. I think both maps imply the same sentence, and it is the only one in this article I would forward to a board: the leverage only exists before you sign.

On August 2, 2026, the bulk of the EU AI Act starts applying. The obligations for general-purpose models have already been in force for a year, since August 2, 2025. Now the rest of the regime catches up. Provenance, accountability, and reversibility stop being conference words and become clause language. The map you choose decides what you put in those clauses.

---

The two documents tell one story from two directions. The lab side has now modeled, in public, the economics I wrote about from the operator side. The scenario arithmetic is theirs. The fork belongs to everyone reading it.

Fifty years ago, Alaska answered the ownership question before the money got big. The AI version of that conversation is starting now, in treaty language and procurement language at the same time. Whether it gets settled by governance or by ownership is the one variable I would watch.

The window is short. In their scenario, the whole labor transformation happens between one renewal cycle and the one after next. The machines get built either way. Who owns them is still open.
