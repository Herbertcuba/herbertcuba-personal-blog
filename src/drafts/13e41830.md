In 1976, as Prudhoe Bay oil revenue approached, Alaska amended its constitution to save part of its mineral income in a permanent fund. Investment returns financed the first resident dividend in 1982. Fifty years later, that design models how to share AI wealth.

[*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/) uses that precedent in a 2026 policy recommendation from the AI Futures Project, a nonprofit forecasting advanced AI. Four of the five authors of [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), its 2025 rapid-AI scenario, returned. Plan A is not their best-guess forecast.

My book [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/) organizes the transition as the Correction, the Transformation, and the Alignment. It was public on my site before Plan A appeared in July 2026. That establishes publication order, not influence, independent origins, or accuracy. I think the useful result is narrower: two maps reach the same economic fork, then disagree about what control requires.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The works share an economic sequence, but chronology supports comparison, not claims about influence or proof that either timeline is right.</p></div>

## The economic sequence converges

In the Correction, competitive pressure makes AI adoption a survival decision and drives labor's role in production toward zero. Plan A begins further down that road. In its scenario, AI performs a third of all cognitive labor, including analysis, research, and judgment, by mid-2031; as employment falls, personal-income and payroll taxes shrink as a share of the economy.

The Transformation asks how demand survives when paid work no longer supplies most household income. The book puts the mechanism plainly: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” It proposes a Citizen's Dividend, a public claim on the productivity of AI infrastructure. Plan A uses the same label and Alaska precedent. Under Plan A, the US government sells tradable permits for new robots and computing capacity, the chips and facilities used to train and run AI. A growing share of the fees goes to a Compute Dividend Corporation in which every US citizen owns one share. In the scenario, payments rise from [$45,000 per American adult in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf#page=18).

The funding methods differ. *The Three Crucibles* proposes taxing deployed AI work according to the public revenue an equivalent human worker would have generated. Plan A charges for permission to build new robots or computing capacity. I think this is genuine convergence on the economic break and the need for distribution, not validation of either mechanism or schedule.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both connect automated cognitive labor to lower household income and tax revenue, then use a Citizen's Dividend to restore demand; their funding methods differ.</p></div>

## The maps split at Alignment

The Alignment asks whether the owners of AI infrastructure become stewards of abundance or new feudal lords. Plan A approaches that fork through verification: evidence that countries follow agreed limits. In its sequence, a 2029 US-China agreement averts a race to superintelligence by the end of 2030. Here, superintelligence means AI that beats the best humans across fields while also being faster and cheaper. The agreement blocks that early route; a separate 2035 pause holds systems at top-expert AI, roughly the level of the best human specialists, before development resumes in 2040.

Verification depends on declarations of major chip and data-center transactions, reciprocal inspections, and almost entirely transparent AI research. Plan A tests the system with a fictional covert project: roughly 500,000 H100-equivalents, a computing-performance measure based on the H100 processor, are installed in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station. Dam construction provides cover, small nuclear reactors and gas turbines provide interim power, and about 200 people know.

[*The Matrix*](https://www.warnerbros.com/movies/matrix), the 1999 science-fiction film about intelligent machines running a simulated world, helps explain why verification can work. Its signature green code looks weightless, but the machine city behind it still needs chips, power, cooling, and concrete. Those physical traces make inspection possible.

Yet inspection does not decide who owns lawful infrastructure. The book proposes AI hardware owned by individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities, with models that can run locally or on company premises. Plan A gives citizens a share in a dividend corporation, not in the chips, models, or AI companies. I think verification and ownership solve different problems. I don't know which mix survives geopolitical pressure, but visibility can coexist with concentrated ownership.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A uses inspections and research transparency to constrain hidden capacity; the Alignment asks who controls lawful AI and receives its gains.</p></div>

## Control also needs a tested exit

The same distinction appears inside a company. *The Three Crucibles* imagines one insurer keeping 5,000 claims adjusters while a rival uses coordinated AI agents, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. The example is fictional, but adoption can move faster than a buyer's ability to leave its supplier.

My take is that a critical AI contract should renew only after exit proof: a supplier-funded, 30-day handover of one workflow and one usable data extract to a replacement team. The chief information officer owns the renewal decision. The supplier provides decision history, access rights, saved versions of settings and model instructions, software connections, records showing where source information came from and how it changed, plus operating and recovery guidance.

The buyer owns the past cases used for testing. A replacement may change the model, its instructions, how it finds source information, or the workflow itself, but it must reproduce the agreed business result within fixed limits for errors, time, and cost. Failure blocks automatic renewal and triggers funded transition support; a second failure lets the buyer leave without a termination fee.

I think this is the company-level counterpart to Plan A's inspections. Legal disclosure can make a system easier to see, but a failed handover still predicts practical supplier lock-in. If an AI system can be inspected but neither owned broadly nor left in practice, control remains with its builder.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exit proof makes supplier independence a renewal test: if a replacement cannot reproduce the business result within fixed limits, the buyer cannot practically leave.</p></div>
