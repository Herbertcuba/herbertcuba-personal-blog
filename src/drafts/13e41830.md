Buried inside [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) is a fictional company. Every US citizen would own one share in it, and permit fees would supply part of its income. An AI safety plan becomes a draft economic constitution.

Plan A is the AI Futures Project’s 2026 policy recommendation, expressed through a conditional scenario rather than its best-guess forecast. The research nonprofit published [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/) on April 3, 2025; four of that scenario’s five front-page authors return here.

The chronology needs restraint. My site’s repository records the first PDF and link for my book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), on March 23, 2026, and the current-PDF update on June 24; an AI 2040 link appeared on July 9. That proves artifact order, not influence, independent discovery, validation, or predictive success.

I think the comparison matters because both works reach a possible break between machine output and wages, then diverge. Plan A asks how dangerous computing capacity becomes visible; my book asks who owns the assets and income. A buyer faces a third question: can it still leave?

## The economic break is the real convergence

The current PDF of The Three Crucibles opens with The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. Chapters called The Void and The Transformation develop the middle stage, while the landing page calls it The Transformation. Beneath the labels sits one sequence: AI changes labor’s value, opens a gap between production and household demand, then forces a choice about ownership and distribution.

Plan A reaches the same problem through a quantified scenario. It projects AI performing one third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, meaning work based mainly on reasoning and judgment. In 2032, it models roughly 50 percent inflation-adjusted growth while the tax base shrinks relative to the economy. Personal-income and payroll receipts fall as people lose work, while firms deduct new datacenters, factories, and robots from taxable income. Output rises as household and public funding weaken.

[Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Tax_revenue_statistics), the European Union’s statistical office, reports that taxes and social contributions equaled 40.4 percent of EU gross domestic product in 2024, while stable employment and wage growth supported revenue. That confirms the current link, not a future collapse. Governments also tax production, imports, income, and wealth, so the mix can change.

The remedies expose both overlap and disagreement. Plan A sells capacity permits, paid and tradable legal permissions to build scarce robot or computing infrastructure. Its scenario projects $50 trillion in US federal permit revenue in fiscal 2032 and a $45,000 Citizen’s Dividend per adult, in 2025 dollars. These are scenario outputs, not observed results.

[The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/downloads/three-crucibles.pdf) pairs its Citizen’s Dividend with an AI Labor Equivalence Tax: a levy on human-equivalent AI work based on what a person in that role would have contributed to public revenue. Its demand-side explanation is blunt: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” Machine abundance can grow while its customers lose income.

Both works point to the Alaska Permanent Fund, created by a 1976 constitutional amendment to invest part of Alaska’s mineral revenue and support resident dividends, first paid in 1982. I think the shared precedent reveals common economic logic, not influence: when a productive resource displaces the old income base, citizens need a claim on the new one.

<p>Both works ask how purchasing power and public revenue survive when machine output outruns wages. Their charges differ, but both reconnect production to citizens through a dividend. Eurostat shows why the wage link matters today without proving either future.</p>

## Visibility and ownership solve different failures

Plan A begins with takeoff, a feedback loop in which AI automates AI research and speeds its improvement. Its scenario has the United States and China reach an agreement in 2029, pause at top-human-expert capability in 2035, and resume in 2040 toward superintelligence: AI significantly better than the best humans across all tasks, while faster and cheaper.

Restraint depends on compute, the processing capacity used to train and run AI. Major chip transactions would be declared, datacenters inspected, and research disclosed. Developers would publish safety cases, written arguments that a system will not cause irreversible catastrophe, for expert and public criticism. I think this turns restraint from a promise into an evidence problem.

The document stress-tests that method in a fictional branch where China hides processors beside the unfinished Medog Hydropower Station project and falsifies records. The facility still needs power and cooling. Verification can fail, yet industrial capacity leaves physical traces.

Ownership addresses a different failure. Plan A’s proposed Compute Dividend Corporation would hold a fixed share of permit fees, with one equal share for every US citizen. I think that is stronger than a discretionary payment, but it is still a claim on a centrally managed fee stream, not on robots, datacenters, models, or AI companies. My book argues for distributed ownership: individuals, cooperatives, and municipalities directly holding AI infrastructure and its income.

The endpoints sharpen the difference. In Plan A’s 2040 storyline, institutions and equipment are handed to AI systems until humans could no longer shut everything down. The Three Crucibles asks whether people remain stewards of abundance or depend on a narrow owner class. I don’t know which institutional mix could survive a broken agreement or sustained geopolitical pressure. My take is that safety rules and economic rights must be joined because each leaves the other’s failure untouched.

<p>Plan A uses declarations, inspection, and safety cases against hidden capacity and danger. The Three Crucibles pushes asset claims outward. Visibility cannot distribute gains; ownership cannot prove safety.</p>

## A usable exit starts at signing

My book compares two fictional insurers. One keeps 5,000 claims adjusters; the other uses coordinated AI agents, software that carries out claims work, cuts headcount by 40 percent, lowers premiums by 20 percent, and wins within six months. Adoption outruns the buyer’s ability to reverse it.

That is reversibility capture: a supplier retains the data, licences, interfaces, certification, or scarce computing capacity that makes departure expensive despite a portability promise. The countermeasure is exit proof. Before renewal, an independent team must run one critical workflow to an agreed standard without the incumbent’s hidden knowledge.

The leverage only exists before you sign.

For a claims-triage pilot, I would allow 30 days and about 15 people-days across the process owner, a replacement engineer, security, and procurement. The team replays buyer-owned cases, fixed historical inputs and expected outcomes controlled by the buyer. The process owner approves the cases and grader, the rule comparing output with the expected result. It scores three runs with one retry. Even a correct decision fails without an evidence trail because another team cannot audit it.

A workable clause requires test access and supplier cooperation at a pre-agreed cost. The buyer owns the cases and grader; a pass can earn preferred status or better terms. Failure triggers dated remediation plus escrow or portability support. If the supplier still cannot pass, procurement reprices the dependency or does not renew. I think that turns “avoid lock-in” into a decision rule.

The [European Union’s 2024 AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), a risk-based law for AI developers and users, provides a trigger. It became generally applicable on August 2, 2026; rules for providers of general-purpose models, which support many tasks, had applied since August 2, 2025, while some high-risk rules transition later. Legal identifies the system category and whether the buyer is a provider or deployer, the organization using it. The process owner brings intended use and cases, security brings controls and logs, and procurement brings data, licence, cooperation, and termination terms. These records support the test but cannot prove another team can operate it.

The broader thesis needs the same discipline. At a 2032 checkpoint, take the five European economies with the highest official business AI-use rates. If output per worker rises for three years while labor’s income share and employment-linked public receipts hold or rise, the strong thesis fails. If both fall, the break gains evidence without any superintelligence claim.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Exit proof makes reversibility observable before renewal: the buyer controls the test, the supplier cooperates, and failure changes the decision. A public 2032 test disciplines the economic argument. The machines may be built either way; who holds the durable claim is still open.</p>
</div>
