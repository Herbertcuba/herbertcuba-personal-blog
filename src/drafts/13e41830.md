In 1976, Alaska voters made a choice before the state's oil wealth had fully arrived. They created the Alaska Permanent Fund, a state-owned investment fund that saves part of the revenue from a finite natural resource. Since the first payment in 1982, eligible residents have received an annual cash dividend from the fund's earnings.

Fifty years later, that practical idea appears in two speculative maps of an AI economy. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/) uses Alaska as the model for sharing returns from scarce AI infrastructure. So does [my book, *The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), under the same name: the Citizen's Dividend.

A downloadable copy of my book was public on my site on March 23, 2026. AI 2040 was publicly available by July 9. Those dates do not establish influence, and a scenario is not confirming data. They make the independent overlap worth examining.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Alaska turned resource wealth into a lasting public claim. Both AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles ask whether AI productivity needs a similar claim.</p></div>

## What AI 2040 asks us to imagine

AI 2040 is a 90-page scenario from the AI Futures Project, a US research nonprofit. Four of the five authors behind *AI 2027*, its April 2025 scenario about rapid AI development, also appear on the new work. The authors call Plan A “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” which is important: its dates describe a path they want governments to create.

That path begins with a US–China agreement in 2029 that prevents fully automated AI research in 2030. Development continues within the range of human ability, pauses at the level of leading human experts in 2035, and resumes toward superintelligence in 2040. The report uses that term for AI that is broadly better than the best humans while also being faster and cheaper.

The economic mechanism matters more than the headline date. In the scenario, AI performs a third of cognitive labor by mid-2031, so revenue from wages and payroll taxes weakens. Governments cap new compute, meaning the processing capacity used to train and run AI, as well as new robot capacity. They sell tradable permits to add more, then direct part of the fees to a Compute Dividend Corporation in which every US citizen owns one share.

Compliance depends on inspections, chip tracking, and research transparency. In other words, verification means producing evidence that the declared limits are actually being obeyed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A tries to slow the race, preserve visibility into advanced AI development, and turn permits for scarce capacity into income shared with citizens.</p></div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

Before comparing the maps, the book's three stages need to be clear.

**The Correction (2026)** is the break in the old bargain between productivity and paid labor. As AI and machines produce more, the book argues that labor captures less of the gain.

**The Transformation (2027–2032)** is the institutional response. Income and demand need a new foundation, so the book proposes a Citizen's Dividend: a direct transfer from the productivity of AI infrastructure to the people expected to consume what it produces.

**The Alignment (2035+)** is the political test. The same abundance can produce broad stewardship or a new class of feudal owners, depending on who controls the systems and who holds a claim on their output.

This is a philosophical framework rather than a calibrated forecast. Its purpose is to name the transitions that institutions may have to survive.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Correction breaks the labor bargain, the Transformation rebuilds distribution, and the Alignment decides whether AI abundance is shared or concentrated.</p></div>

## Where the maps meet, and where they do not

The first convergence is fiscal. Both maps expect automation to weaken labor income before public institutions have replaced the taxes and consumer demand that depend on it. AI 2040 models the pressure in detail; The Three Crucibles gives it a social shape.

The second is unusually specific. Both arrive at a Citizen's Dividend and both point to Alaska. The phrase itself is older than either work, so I am not claiming to have invented it. What matters is the shared chain of reasoning: machine productivity strains labor income, society still needs purchasing power, and citizens therefore need a direct economic claim.

The third convergence is the struggle over concentrated power. Yet the safeguards differ. Plan A foregrounds limits, inspections, and international coordination. My book foregrounds distributed ownership, AI labor taxation, and the destination of the gains.

Plan A even tests its own control system through a covert Chinese project. Its appendix estimates a chance below 10 percent that such a project could reach AI surpassing leading human experts while remaining undetected before 2043. That low estimate still exposes the boundary: inspection addresses whether actors respect a capacity ceiling, while ownership addresses who benefits below that ceiling. One cannot do the other's job, and The Three Crucibles gives the loss-of-control problem less attention than Plan A does.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The maps share an economic diagnosis and a political fork. Their difference is useful: safe capacity needs verification, while shared prosperity needs ownership and distribution.</p></div>

## Preparation is the point

No one knows what waits between here and 2040. I certainly do not. Plan A's authors do not claim certainty either, and agreement between two speculative works cannot turn uncertainty into fact.

But if Plan A and The Three Crucibles are even directionally right, the road will be bumpy. Automation may move faster than distribution, governance may trail technical capacity, and ownership may concentrate before societies decide what citizens are owed. That is how I want my book to be read: as a preparation document for choices that are still open.

Alaska did not know the exact future value of its oil. It decided who should retain a claim before the full value arrived. AI presents a harder resource and a larger question, but the timing lesson holds: ownership is easiest to shape before abundance becomes power.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Neither map predicts 2040 with certainty. Both suggest that societies should prepare now, while limits, ownership, and the distribution of AI's gains can still be shaped.</p></div>
