A future can fail in the tax code before it fails in the laboratory. [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/), released on July 9, 2026, by the AI Futures Project, is a policy recommendation told through a conditional scenario. The forecasting group previously produced [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), an April 2025 scenario about AI systems accelerating the development of better AI systems. Four of that work’s five authors returned for AI 2040.

Plan A imagines artificial intelligence and robots producing more value while personal-income and payroll-tax receipts erode. Firms keep reinvesting in chips, datacenters, factories, and machines, so corporate taxation also struggles to capture the growth.

That economic break sits at the center of [*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my book about AI-driven changes to work, taxation, ownership, and power. Its public page and downloadable edition were available by March 23, 2026, and the current PDF is dated June 24, 2026. That establishes public chronology, rather than influence, predictive success, or empirical validation.

Plan A’s detailed years and numbers invite overreading. Its authors call it “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction,” designed to test policy rather than state their best guess. I think the independent overlap supports a narrower claim: any advanced-AI strategy is incomplete unless it can verify dangerous capacity and enforce a broad claim on machine-created wealth.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 is a policy scenario, not confirming data. Its value here is that an independent team reaches the same economic problem and makes the missing institutional choices easier to see.</p></div>

## The dates disagree more than the structure

The public page for *The Three Crucibles* names three stages: The Correction in 2026, The Transformation from 2027 to 2032, and The Alignment from 2035 onward. The PDF’s opening map calls the middle stage The Void, then later places transformation within that second Crucible. The underlying sequence is consistent: paid human labor becomes economically marginal, output recovers before jobs do, and the final conflict concerns who owns the productive machines and distributes their gains.

Plan A moves through a different calendar. In its scenario, the United States and China reach a deal in 2029, avoid fully automated AI research in 2030, pause around systems equal to the best human researchers in 2035, and resume progress toward superintelligence in 2040. Fully automated AI research means AI systems performing the work needed to design better AI systems. Plan A defines superintelligence as systems better than the best humans at everything while also being faster and cheaper.

The works begin with different questions. Plan A foregrounds the speed and control of increasingly capable AI, while my framework begins with the economic contract around labor. I think the meaningful convergence is the order of pressures: automation weakens wage income, the old revenue system strains, and ownership becomes a political question.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The timelines are materially different, but both works place an economic break before the final settlement over power and ownership. That structural claim can be tested even if the dramatic 2040 scenario never occurs.</p></div>

## The dividend contains a tax theory

Both works arrive at the same label, “Citizen’s Dividend,” meaning a direct public payment backed by a citizen claim on AI-related revenue or productive assets. Both point to the Alaska Permanent Fund. Alaska created the fund through a 1976 constitutional amendment after revenue from the Prudhoe Bay oil field transformed the state’s finances; it paid its first dividend in 1982. Citizens received a legal claim on resource wealth before the revenue became politically irresistible.

Plan A applies that logic to scarce machine capacity. Its scenario requires permits to build robots and compute, the chips and datacenter processing power used to train and run AI. A cap limits total capacity, while tradable permits allocate it and create public revenue. Under an economics model the authors call uncertain, [the scenario projects](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) $50 trillion in permit fees in fiscal 2032, with an adult dividend rising from $45,000 in 2032 to about $1 million in 2035 (PDF pp. 17–19). These are modeled outcomes, not budget forecasts.

*The Three Crucibles* proposes a different base: distributed AI ownership, meaning access and legal claims beyond a few firms, alongside universal basic income, a regular payment without work conditions, and taxation of AI labor. Its tax principle is that productivity gains should keep financing society when machines replace paid work. The measurement problem is unresolved. A government must decide whether it is taxing an automated task, its output, company revenue, or avoided payroll.

I think this difference matters more than the shared name. Plan A taxes permission to add a constrained input before production. My book taxes the economic substitution after it occurs and seeks broader ownership of the productive infrastructure itself. Permit systems can miss hidden chips or activity moved across borders; labor taxes can fail when firms relabel tasks or cannot attribute output. A dividend without a measurable base is only a promise.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The documents agree that citizens need a claim on AI-created wealth, but they fund it differently. Plan A measures scarce capacity; The Three Crucibles tries to capture displaced labor and ownership, so each mechanism has a different path to evasion.</p></div>

## Control and ownership fail in different ways

Plan A puts verification first. Its international deal requires research transparency, tracked chips, inspected datacenters, and safety cases: documented arguments, backed by evidence, that a proposed AI training or deployment is acceptably safe. These controls are meant to reveal prohibited development early enough for participating governments to stop it.

Plan A also tests its own weakness. Its covert-project appendix imagines China hiding a datacenter in tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station, a vast dam project whose legitimate construction could conceal 500,000 smuggled units of standardized AI-chip capacity. The authors estimate less than a 10 percent chance that the project reaches AI matching the best human experts undetected before 2043. This fictional branch identifies the central failure signal: prohibited capacity can exist while the official record still looks compliant.

*The Three Crucibles* instead calls for open infrastructure, open models, and legal rules against a private intelligence monopoly, but offers no comparable system for controlling dangerous AI development. Plan A includes ownership through its Compute Dividend Corporation, which gives every US citizen one share and pays profits from a rising fraction of permit fees. The divergence lies in what each work tries to measure and enforce first.

My take is that strategy needs a capacity-and-claim test. The capacity side must name what is monitored, where an unauthorized training run can be stopped, who has that authority, and what evidence reveals a bypass. The claim side must state what citizens legally own, who records the right, and what prevents dilution or political capture. I think technical compliance can coexist with concentrated wealth, just as broad ownership can coexist with unsafe AI development.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Verification governs whether dangerous AI capacity can be observed and stopped. Ownership governs who can claim the resulting wealth. A credible settlement needs enforceable answers on both sides.</p></div>

## Convergence should become a test

I don’t know whether the decisive break arrives in the late 2020s, in 2040, or much later. The Plan A authors do not know either, which is why they separate their recommendation from their expected future. I think that uncertainty makes testable institutional commitments more important than confident dates.

The economic claim should weaken if large-scale AI automation leaves wage income and wage-tax revenue broadly intact. The control claim should weaken if chip tracking, inspections, and safety cases cannot detect prohibited development before it matters. The ownership claim should fail if a citizen’s share can be revoked, diluted, or captured while still being called a dividend. These are observable outcomes, not matters of belief.

Publication order makes the convergence worth examining; it does not make it a victory. What matters is whether governments and companies can establish both a real power to stop dangerous capacity and a durable public claim on the value that capacity creates. I think the transition remains ungoverned until both can survive contact with an adversary.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The overlap between AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles is useful only when it produces claims that can fail. If institutions cannot enforce both limits on dangerous capacity and broad claims on machine-created wealth, they have governed only half the transition.</p></div>
