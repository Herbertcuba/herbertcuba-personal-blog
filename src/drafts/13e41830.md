In 1976, Alaska made an unusual deal with itself. The oil at Prudhoe Bay belonged to everyone in the state, so when the oil companies came to drill, a share of the revenue went into a public fund. Since 1982 every Alaska resident has received a yearly check from it. No work requirement. You live there, you own a share of the resource, you get paid.

In July 2026, the AI Futures Project published [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), ninety pages of scenario modeling and policy engineering from the team that wrote [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/). Its economic centerpiece is that same machine at civilizational scale: a Citizen's Dividend funded by fees on compute, held by a Compute Dividend Corporation. Their footnote says it plainly: "This is similar to the setup of the Alaska Permanent Fund, except at a much larger scale." I had published the same term months earlier in [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), public on my site since March 23, 2026. Two maps of the same fire. Both leave the ownership question open, and that opening is where the fight of the next two years happens.

The document earned its audience. 400+ points and 500+ comments on [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48848425) inside a week. George Hotz answered within days with [the hardest published critique it has received](https://geohot.github.io//blog/jekyll/update/2026/07/11/ai-2040.html). The team draws on direct OpenAI experience and conversations inside the frontier labs, and their previous scenario called the current moment better than anyone else in the field. Their own self-assessment is blunter: "So far reality is tracking closer to AI 2027 than even we expected."

## What Plan A says

Plan A is a treaty scenario with an economic engine attached.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The US and China agree in 2029 to avoid a reckless race. The deal triggers a training pause and total research transparency, the world scales to top-expert AI by 2035, pauses there to keep humans in control, then unpauses to superintelligence in 2040. The economy runs on capped compute, permit fees, and a Citizen's Dividend.</p></div>

The timeline, in their scenario. In 2029 the US and China agree to avoid a reckless race to superintelligence. The deal holds off an intelligence explosion that would otherwise arrive by the end of 2030. Between 2030 and 2035 the world scales inside the human range, up to AI roughly as capable as top human experts. In 2035 there is a deliberate pause at that level. In 2040, the world unpauses and scales to superintelligence. Hence the title.

The economics are why I am writing this. All numbers here are in-scenario, their forecast inside their own model. Real GDP growth hits roughly 50% per year by 2032. World compute goes from 20 million H100-equivalents in 2026 to 60 billion by 2034. The AI share of the labor economy climbs from about 20% in 2032 to about 85% by 2035. Under the 2026 tax code, the US tax base collapses as a fraction of GDP, because firms can expense the machines that replaced the workers. So Plan A replaces the dead base with cap-and-trade on compute and robots. Permit fees become the new federal revenue, roughly $50 trillion in FY2032, climbing toward $180 trillion by 2034. Most of it flows to a dividend that starts at $45,000 per person in 2032 and reaches roughly $1 million by 2035.

Translate that curve into one company. In my book I sketched two insurers. Company A keeps its 5,000 claims adjusters and sits near the 20% end of that line. Company B deploys a swarm of claims agents, cuts headcount 40%, drops premiums 20%, and crushes Company A in six months. The advance guard is already visible. In the book I cited Klarna, the Swedish payments company: AI doing the work of 700 full-time employees, headcount halved by 2025. The agent underneath is simple to describe. The model reads the claim, decides what to do, calls a tool, reads the result, repeats. Around the loop sits a verification seam that routes anything above a set amount to a human. That seam is where the remaining jobs live.

The authors are honest about the genre. Plan A calls itself "primarily a recommendation, not a prediction." It is a stress test for five policies: Compute Declaration, Training Pause, Verification Plan, Transparency Plan, Capability Scaling Strategy. Datacenters big enough to be visible from space. Chip fabs concentrated in a handful of countries, which is what makes the accounting exercise thinkable at all.

## Where the maps converge

The overlap is specific enough to check. What survives checking is more than vocabulary.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The convergence runs deeper than vocabulary: same term, same fifty-year-old precedent, same diagnosis of why the tax base dies, same refusal to treat the future as fixed. The funding mechanisms split on one question: does a treaty exist or not.</p></div>

First, the terrain. My book describes the transition as three passages. A Correction breaks the labor economy: Productivity = Capital x Labor loses its labor variable, and what remains is Productivity = Capital. A Transformation rebuilds distribution. An Alignment settles who owns the machines. Plan A's 2027 chapter opens inside the Correction already running. America runs two workforces, 165 million humans alongside millions of AI agents spun up and shut down by the hour. By 2031, in their scenario, a third of all cognitive labor is done by AIs. Their collapse runs on a slower schedule than mine. The shape is the same.

Second, the tell. Their 2033 chapter is literally titled "The Citizen's Dividend." A term I had built a book around, public since March. They cite the same precedent I did: the Alaska Permanent Fund. The term has older roots in UBI discourse. I claim convergence; the word belongs to the commons. When a solo author and a six-author forecasting shop reach for the same word and the same fifty-year-old fund from opposite directions, I think the two documents are triangulating the same structure.

Third, the money and the fork. Both documents see the same hole in the budget: when machines replace payroll, the payroll tax base dies. My book funds the dividend through an AI Labor Equivalence Tax, an instrument a single country can pass on its own. Plan A funds it through cap-and-trade permits, which only work if the Consortium exists to run the market. One mechanism survives a world without a treaty. The other requires the treaty first. Then the endgame. Their foreword frames the whole project as avoiding extinction or irreversible concentration of power, where a tiny group of people ends up controlling the world's only army of superintelligences. That is my Path Two in different vocabulary: stewards of abundance, or new feudal lords, chosen by the owners of the infrastructure. And both documents refuse the forecaster's chair. Theirs is a recommendation. Mine is a preparation manual. Both insist the fork is a choice.

## Where the maps diverge

One question decides which future you get: who owns the intelligence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A bets on governance. I bet on ownership. The treaty's weak seam is the measurement stack it has to invent, and whoever builds it owns the governed world. I call it the verification chokepoint.</p></div>

Plan A's answer is governance. Nations cap compute, verify the cap, pause training when things get dangerous. My book's answer is ownership. Spread the intelligence layer across millions of hands, through open models and hardware people control, and concentration never gets the chance to harden. Same fork. Different lever.

I think ownership is the stronger lever, for one reason. A governed intelligence economy with five owners is a well-run Matrix. The simulation runs smoothly, and nobody inside it owns anything. Plan A's own numbers show the seam. Its enforcement rests on one measurement: by the end of 2029, each side must be confident the other is hiding less than about 1% of AI compute. That confidence needs chips serialized at the fab and devices that attest what workload is running. Pieces of this exist in confidential-computing research and vendor telemetry. Nothing shipped today does it at datacenter scale. The scenario has to invent the measurement stack on a treaty timeline. Whoever builds that stack owns the governed world. I call it the verification chokepoint. Plan A spends ninety pages on who controls the models, and leaves the instrument that measures them to an unnamed consortium. If you build systems for a living, that is the assumption to watch.

To be fair, their covert-project math is the strongest part of the document. I tried to find a hole in it. I came away half-convinced. Their worst case, half a million diverted H100-equivalents and two hundred humans in the loop, still takes until 2043 to reach top-expert AI in their forecast. And George Hotz, in the strongest published critique of the document, went further. He called the consortium world government with sci-fi characteristics, and proposed Plan L: AI aligned to the individual owner instead of a consortium. Plan L is structurally my third pillar, distributed ownership. Plan A's fiercest critic ends where my book does.

I will own my gap too. My book treats loss of control more lightly than it should. They wrote the enforcement mechanics of the transition and I wrote its sociology. I don't know which failure mode is more likely, a deal that collapses or a concentration that never gets challenged.

## What this means for you

If you build with LLMs for a living, this debate prices your skills and picks your employer.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Selling hours is the losing position in both futures. Own assets, and become the person who can verify what the agents did. Start with two vendor questions and one contract clause.</p></div>

I think the boring documents decide more than the scenarios do, so start there. Two questions for your next renewal. What happens to my model access if your licensing terms tighten? Which of my prices only hold while the subsidy war lasts? Then put one line in the next contract: an exit clause that lets you take your fine-tunes and eval suites with you when the terms change. Ownership is a procurement decision before it is a geopolitical one. The same question applies inside your own org: who owns the agents your business units are piloting today, and on whose license do they run? In Europe this stops being theoretical on August 2, 2026, when the EU AI Act becomes fully applicable and provenance and accountability language lands in procurement whether you planned for it or not.

The second move is the verification seam from Company B. That seam is the job. Approval gates and human sign-off are old workflow tooling; what is new is the volume, thousands of agent decisions crossing the seam every day instead of a dozen tickets a week. The threshold keys on claim amount and model confidence, and the seam exists to catch the failure a naive loop misses: an agent that is confidently wrong at volume. Learn evals the way you learned tests, and write the verifier before you trust the agent. The minimal setup is small: one senior engineer owns the eval suite, a domain reviewer sets the routing threshold. A quarter is enough to stand it up. In every scenario on the table, the scarce human is the one who can verify what the machine did. A model two years behind the frontier, running on hardware you own, beats a superintelligence you rent.

Plan A's postscript says the world is asleep at the wheel. I think the wheel is the ownership question, and it gets grabbed in boring places: a clause in a vendor contract, a checkpoint on hardware you own. Alaska answered the question for oil in 1976, before the money got big enough to buy the answer. The window for intelligence is shorter. It starts with the next contract you sign, and the next model you choose to own.
