[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) and my book [The Three Crucibles](/books/the-three-crucibles/) arrive at the same economic fork. Both expect machine labor to weaken a tax system built around human wages, then answer with a Citizen's Dividend modeled on Alaska.

The book has been public on my site since March 23. AI 2040 appeared in July. The phrase "Citizen's Dividend" is older than either work. I think their independent agreement on the whole structure is far more useful than priority over the wording.

One document starts with a fictional insurer, the other with US-China negotiations. They meet when productivity grows faster than human income, then split over control.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI 2040 and The Three Crucibles reach the same economic design from different starting points. Their shared Citizen's Dividend strengthens the diagnosis, while their disagreement exposes the harder problem: who keeps control of machine output?</p></div>

## Two maps reach the same dividend

Start with Company B, the fictional insurer in The Three Crucibles.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The book's insurer scenario and Plan A's national scenario follow the same chain. Automation cuts labor costs, wage-based tax revenue weakens, and part of machine output must return to citizens if demand is going to survive.</p></div>

Company A keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. Company B deploys an agentic swarm, cuts headcount by 40 percent and lowers premiums by 20 percent. In the thought experiment, Company B wins within six months.

That competitive move begins the Correction. The Void follows as machine output rises and wage income loses weight. The Alignment asks whether abundance produces shared stewardship or concentrated power. The book compresses the demand problem into one blunt line: "Robots do not buy iPhones."

Plan A reaches the same pressure through federal finance. Its takeoff scenario assumes AI can speed up AI research, which then speeds up the next round of research. The authors recommend a US-China deal in 2029, a controlled pause, and a later path toward superintelligence in 2040.

The numbers are scenario projections. By mid-2031, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor. Real GDP grows by about 50 percent in 2032. The US sells permits for scarce compute and robot capacity, collecting a projected $50 trillion that year. Each citizen gets one share in a Compute Dividend Corporation. The annual dividend starts at $45,000 and reaches about $1 million by 2035.

Those figures may age quickly. The structure has a better chance of surviving. Alaska created its Permanent Fund in 1976 and paid the first dividend in 1982. Plan A uses permits at the supply constraint. The Three Crucibles taxes AI labor where firms replace human work.

I think the collection point can vary. Company B still creates a demand problem when its savings flow mainly to capital while household wages shrink. A durable economy needs a human claim on machine output.

## The maps split over control

The agreement ends when each document explains how power stays accountable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A tries to make frontier AI visible through declarations, inspections and public research. The Three Crucibles puts more weight on distributed ownership. Visibility can reveal a breach, while ownership determines who can act after the breach is found.</p></div>

Plan A asks major datacenter owners and suppliers to declare large transactions. Inspectors check the numbers. Almost all AI research becomes public. Capability pauses remain reversible, and frontier work is spread across regulated actors.

Then the report attacks its own design. In its covert-project scenario, China diverts about 500,000 smuggled H100-equivalent chips into tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station. Around 200 people may know. The scenario estimates that the chance of reaching Top-Expert-Dominating AI undetected stays below 10 percent until 2043.

The appendix shows verification under pressure. Declared compute can remain visible while hidden capacity grows.

The Three Crucibles leans harder on distributed AI ownership. I read that as three separate rights. A dividend shares economic returns. Operational portability lets Company B move its claims process. Democratic control decides who may set the rules for frontier systems. One right cannot stand in for the others.

This is the *Alien* problem. The Nostromo crew discovers Special Order 937, but discovery comes after the company has already set the mission's priorities. The evidence changes what the crew knows. Their ability to change the system remains limited.

George Hotz attacks Plan A from the physical side. He argues that fabs and supply chains slow any hard takeoff, then proposes local AI aligned to the individual owner. I don't know which pressure breaks Plan A first. I think the report's answer is stronger on loss of control, while the book is stronger on ownership after control fails.

## Turn ownership into a claim replay

Company B gives a junior AI engineer a small place to test this civilizational argument.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Ownership becomes concrete when Company B can replay representative claims with a fallback model. ReAct describes the agent's tool-use loop. LangGraph, TypedDict and StateGraph make the workflow and its human boundary visible.</p></div>

Suppose Company B's claim agent uses ReAct, short for reasoning and acting. The model reads a policy, calls a claims tool, reads the result and chooses its next action. That loop continues until the agent proposes an outcome or sends the claim to a person.

LangGraph is a Python library for workflows that branch or repeat. A `StateGraph` connects each step as a node and uses edges to choose the next step. The nodes share one state record. A `TypedDict` tells Python's static type checker which fields should exist in that record. Python leaves runtime validation to other code.

An illustrative state for Company B could look like this:

```python
from typing import Literal, TypedDict

class ClaimState(TypedDict):
    claim_id: str
    policy_version: str
    proposed_outcome: Literal["pay", "review", "deny"]
    requires_human: bool
    model_id: str
    tool_trace: list[dict]
```

A `StateGraph` can route `requires_human=True` to a review node. The `tool_trace` field can hold the tool calls and returned values used during a replay. This snippet only defines the state shape. The model call, tools, routing code and runtime checks still have to be built and tested.

Before Company B signs with a model provider, it can save representative past claims after legal and privacy review. Each case needs an allowed outcome and a human escalation rule. The team runs them against the current model and a named fallback. If the fallback turns an allowed payment or review into an unsupported denial, the exit test fails.

I think this is the engineering form of ownership. The company keeps its policy logic, prompts, evals and traces in an exportable bundle. A provider can improve the model. Company B can still prove that its claims process survives a provider change.

## Put the exit test before the contract

Portability only creates leverage while Company B can still choose its supplier terms.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Run the fallback replay before signature and again at renewal. Engineering owns the test, architecture owns the export format, and procurement records the exit path. A failed replay blocks the deal unless a named executive accepts the risk.</p></div>

Closed and open models require different contracts. In a typical closed API arrangement, Company B may keep its policy data, prompts and evals while the provider keeps the model weights and serving system under the signed terms. With open weights, self-hosting depends on the model's actual license, the available infrastructure and the company's ability to operate it.

The decision rule can stay simple. Engineering runs the claims set before signature and at renewal. Architecture checks the export bundle. Procurement and legal record the approved EU or EEA data path, notice periods and deletion terms. The AI platform budget pays for the fallback run. A failure pauses signing until the defect is fixed or an executive owner accepts it in writing.

On August 2, 2026, the bulk of the EU AI Act started applying. The obligations for general-purpose AI models have applied since August 2, 2025. Regulation adds pressure for evidence, but Company B still needs its own proof of exit.

I think junior engineers should treat that proof as production work. A pilot shows that today's provider can process the claim. A replay shows whether the business can keep processing claims after the provider changes the deal. That is [reversibility as an engineering constraint](/posts/when-software-becomes-disposable/) and it gives [the agent layer a named owner](/posts/who-owns-the-agent-layer/).

AI 2040 gives governments a plan for visibility and shared returns. The Three Crucibles adds ownership. Company B turns both maps into evidence an engineering team can collect before signing.

The machines will be built either way. Ownership is the decision still in human hands.
