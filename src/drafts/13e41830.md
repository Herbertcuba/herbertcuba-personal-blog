[AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf) calls its 2033 chapter "The Citizen's Dividend." My book [The Three Crucibles](/books/the-three-crucibles/) uses the same name and the same Alaska oil-dividend precedent. The book has been public on my site since March 23. AI 2040 appeared in July.

The phrase predates both documents. I think the stronger signal is the structure. Four of the five authors behind [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/) returned for AI 2040. Their route starts with national policy. The Three Crucibles starts with a damaged-car claim.

The routes converge on economics and split on accountability when verification fails.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Both documents connect machine labor to a Citizen's Dividend. Wage-funded demand and public revenue weaken as machines do more work.</p></div>

## One damaged-car claim contains the economic problem

A claims department makes the national argument small enough to inspect.

In the book's thought experiment, Company A keeps 5,000 claims adjusters. Company B uses an agentic system, cuts headcount by 40 percent and premiums by 20 percent, then wins within six months.

The First Crucible, the Correction, forces Company A to respond. The Second, the Void, opens as machine output rises while wage income weakens. "Robots do not buy iPhones." The Third, the Alignment, asks who owns the machines and who receives their output.

Plan A reaches the same pressure through federal revenue. It says personal income and payroll taxes brought in roughly ten times more federal revenue than corporate taxes in 2026. When salaries disappear, those taxes go too. The report says the tax base would collapse as a share of GDP without reform.

I think this overlap survives a slower AI timeline. Company B needs an agent that handles enough claims at a lower cost. Once one insurer proves the model, price pressure carries the Correction through the market.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Three Crucibles starts with one insurer. AI 2040 carries the effect into the federal tax base. In both, rising machine output weakens wages, demand, and public revenue.</p></div>

## The dividend is the strongest convergence

Plan A turns Company B's missing payroll into a new source of public income.

Its scenario begins with a US-China deal in 2029. Takeoff means AI speeds up its own research, allowing capability to improve far faster. The deal prevents a 2030 jump to fully automated AI research. Countries pause at top-expert AI in 2035, then move toward superintelligence in 2040. The authors call Plan A a recommendation and its economic model uncertain.

Inside the scenario, AI performs one-third of cognitive labor by mid-2031. Real GDP grows about 50 percent in 2032. The US caps advanced compute and robot production, then collects a projected $50 trillion from permits that year.

Part of that money goes into a Compute Dividend Corporation. Every US citizen receives one share. The projected annual dividend starts at $45,000 per person in 2032 and reaches about $1 million in 2035.

Alaska established its Permanent Fund in 1976 and paid the first dividend in 1982. Both documents use that precedent. AI 2040 charges for scarce compute and robots before production. The Three Crucibles taxes AI labor or output as it enters the economy.

I think the dollar amounts will age faster than this structure. Company B still needs a way to return part of its machine output to people whose wages once supported demand and public services. The collection point can move. The claim on the output has to exist.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A sells permits for scarce compute and robots, then gives every citizen a share in the resulting fund. The Three Crucibles taxes AI work as it enters production. The funding mechanisms differ, while both preserve demand through a Citizen's Dividend.</p></div>

## Verification and ownership cover different failures

Plan A gives verification its strongest possible case.

Datacenter owners and suppliers declare large purchases and sales. Governments inspect infrastructure. Total research transparency exposes training methods. The plan spreads access, keeps pauses reversible, and gives citizens an economic share. It goes far beyond a chip registry.

The report then tests its design. In a covert branch, China diverts about 500,000 smuggled H100-equivalents into tunnels beside the Medog Hydropower Station. Around 200 people may know. In the scenario, the chance of reaching top-expert AI while hidden stays below 10 percent.

A low probability still defines a failure mode. Verification determines what authorities can see and constrain. Ownership determines who keeps a claim on the output and who has an exit when the constraint fails.

The split is testable. If transparency and citizen shares created a reliable exit from concentrated suppliers, ownership would add little. Plan A gets closer through diffuse access. With a closed model, the supplier still controls the weights, license, and serving infrastructure.

The damaged-car claim makes the rights easier to separate. A dividend shares economic returns. Portability protects the claims process. Direct control covers access to the model itself. Democratic control needs more than any one of them.

In *Alien*, the Nostromo crew discovers Ash's orders after the company has decided whose interests count. The crew gains evidence while Weyland-Yutani keeps control. Company B can face the same structure with lower stakes.

[George Hotz's critique](https://geohot.github.io/blog/jekyll/update/2026/07/11/ai-2040.html) challenges hard takeoff through physical limits and puts AI closer to the individual owner. I don't know which layer breaks first. I think verification and ownership cover different failures, and each remains incomplete alone.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A combines inspections, research transparency, broad access, reversible pauses, and citizen shares. Its covert branch still leaves room for hidden compute, while closed providers create a smaller version of the control problem inside companies. Verification shows what is running; ownership preserves a claim and an exit.</p></div>

## The same fork appears in a LangGraph workflow

Company B can test the policy argument on its next damaged-car claim.

Suppose the claim agent uses ReAct. In this loop, a model chooses an action, calls a tool, reads the result, and chooses again. Here, tools read the policy and request a repair estimate. A denial or large payment moves to human review.

LangGraph is a Python library for building that loop as a graph. A `StateGraph` connects Python functions and routes work between them. Each function updates shared state. A `TypedDict` tells Python's type checker which fields that state should contain. Runtime validation needs separate code.

```python
from typing import TypedDict
from langgraph.graph import StateGraph

class ClaimState(TypedDict):
    claim_id: str
    repair_estimate: float
    proposed_outcome: str
    model_id: str
    tool_trace: list[dict]
    requires_human: bool

def human_gate(state: ClaimState):
    needs_review = (
        state["proposed_outcome"] == "deny"
        or state["repair_estimate"] > 5_000
    )
    return {"requires_human": needs_review}

graph = StateGraph(ClaimState)
graph.add_node("estimate", estimate_damage)
graph.add_node("gate", human_gate)
graph.add_edge("estimate", "gate")
```

`estimate_damage` adds the amount and model ID. The gate records the human boundary. `tool_trace` keeps the tool calls and results needed for replay.

An eval is a repeatable test of agent behavior. One illustrative eval says a covered €2,400 repair may end in `pay` or `review`. If the fallback returns `deny`, the migration fails. The saved state, trace, and eval suite must reproduce an allowed outcome under the named fallback.

That replay turns [reversibility into an engineering constraint](/posts/reversibility-is-the-new-correctness/). Company B can own its policy logic, prompts, evals, traces, and data rights. A closed provider is unlikely to transfer its weights. An open-weight model may allow self-hosting under its license. [The agent layer still needs an owner](/posts/who-owns-the-agent-layer/), even when the graph is portable.

Engineering runs the fallback on representative claims before signature and at renewal. Architecture owns the export format. Procurement and legal own the exit terms. A failed replay blocks renewal until it is fixed or the executive owner accepts the risk. The contract states the EU or EEA data path and how customer data exits.

The bulk of the EU AI Act started applying on August 2, 2026. General-purpose AI obligations have applied since August 2, 2025. European teams now need model identity, evidence, and exit terms.

I think junior AI engineers should treat the replay as production work. A pilot proves one provider can run the claim today. An exit test proves the company can still run it after the terms change. The machines will be built either way. Who keeps the right to their output is still open.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>ReAct describes the agent loop, while LangGraph makes its state and approval boundary explicit. Portable state, saved traces, and a fallback eval give Company B an exit it can test. The contract has force only when the same damaged-car claim still reaches an allowed outcome after the provider changes.</p></div>
