In 1976, Alaska’s voters faced a basic question: what should a place do with extraordinary wealth from a resource that will not last forever? They created the [Alaska Permanent Fund](https://apfc.org/about/history/), a public investment fund that receives at least 25 percent of certain mineral revenues. Since the first payment in 1982, eligible residents have received an annual cash dividend funded by its earnings.

Oil and AI are very different resources, but the institutional idea travels. A society can give citizens a direct stake in a productive asset before most of its value accumulates elsewhere.

That is why Alaska appears in [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/), a 2026 policy scenario from the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit focused on advanced AI. Plan A uses the fund as a precedent for returning part of AI’s economic output to citizens. My 2026 speculative book, [The Three Crucibles](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), reaches the same question from another direction. The resemblance cannot prove what comes next, but it does reveal where pressure may build.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Alaska turned finite resource income into a lasting public stake. Both Plan A and The Three Crucibles ask whether AI wealth will require a similar decision.</p>

## What Plan A actually proposes

[Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), published on July 9, 2026, calls itself “primarily a recommendation, not a prediction.” Four of its six authors also wrote scenario material for [AI 2027](https://ai-2027.com/), their 2025 study of rapid AI development and the risk of losing control. The new document describes the route they believe governments should try to create.

That route starts with a US-China agreement in 2029, before the report says AI research could become fully automated in 2030. Development stays within the broad range of human ability until 2035, pauses around top-human-expert level, and proceeds in 2040 to superintelligence. The report defines that as AI far better than the best humans at essentially every task while also being faster and cheaper.

The mechanism is control over compute, meaning the advanced chips and data centers used to train and run AI. Plan A proposes tracked chips, inspected facilities and shared information so countries can check that agreed limits are being kept. It also imagines permits for scarce compute and robots. Fees would fund a corporation with one share per citizen, which would pay a Citizen’s Dividend. In the scenario, AI performs a third of thinking and knowledge work by mid-2031, weakening labor-tax revenue as production accelerates.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A recommends slowing and checking advanced AI development, then using the value created by controlled compute and robots to fund a public dividend.</p>
</div>

## The three crucibles in plain language

The first crucible is **The Correction**. AI lets firms produce more with less human labor, weakening the familiar connection between employment, income and consumer demand.

The second is **The Void**, the transformation period after the old arrangement starts failing but before a stable replacement exists. A Citizen’s Dividend keeps people involved in the economy when wages no longer carry the whole load.

The third is **The Alignment**. Once machine production becomes central, society must decide whether AI infrastructure supports broad stewardship of abundance or concentrates power among a small number of owners.

Plan A follows much of this sequence: its shrinking tax base resembles the Correction, its Citizen’s Dividend and Alaska precedent address the Void, and its concern about extreme concentration belongs to the Alignment. The funding differs. My book points toward taxing AI labor and spreading ownership, while Plan A relies more heavily on permits created by international limits.

Social dividends and public investment funds are older than either work. The interesting overlap is the order of events. My book was available on March 23, 2026; Plan A followed in July. Both put distribution and power inside the AI transition rather than leaving them as cleanup after the technology arrives.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The works converge on a sequence: labor’s role weakens, distribution has to change, and ownership becomes a political question. The overlap is structural, not a claim that public dividends are new.</p>
</div>

## The disagreement is about where trust belongs

Plan A centers verification: governments declare their compute, inspectors check facilities, and transparency makes secret development harder. The report tests the limits of this approach in a covert-project appendix. It estimates below a 10 percent chance that a hidden project could reach AI able to dominate top experts without detection by 2043.

The Three Crucibles centers ownership. Verification can reveal a broken limit, but it does not give citizens a claim on lawful AI production. Ownership can spread the upside, but it cannot stop an unsafe race by itself. A workable settlement may need both, and neither document proves that its preferred institutions will survive geopolitical pressure.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Plan A asks how the world can check AI development. The Three Crucibles asks who holds the resulting wealth and power. Solving one problem does not settle the other.</p>
</div>

## Preparation without prophecy

No one knows whether advanced AI will follow these dates, stall on physical limits or take a route neither work anticipates. Yet the difficult part may begin before any final breakthrough, as institutions built around human work come under strain while ownership and governance remain unsettled.

If Plan A and the crucibles are roughly right, we have a bumpy road ahead. The Three Crucibles is meant as a preparation document for that road. Its purpose is to make the choices visible while they are still choices.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Neither work is a prophecy. Their value lies in preparing us for a plausible transition in which technological capacity moves faster than the social contract around it.</p>
