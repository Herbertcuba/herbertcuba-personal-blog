Agreement between forecasts is usually overrated. Two authors can inherit the same assumptions and reproduce the same mistake. The more interesting case is when they begin with different problems and still encounter the same constraint.

That is why I paid attention to [*AI 2040: Plan A*](https://ai-2040.com/AI-2040.pdf), a 2026 policy recommendation presented as a conditional scenario by the AI Futures Project, a research nonprofit focused on advanced AI. Four of its six authors also helped write [*AI 2027*](https://ai-2027.com/), the group's April 2025 scenario about a rapid race toward systems beyond human capability. Their new work asks how the United States, China, and other states might avoid that race through shared limits and inspection.

[*The Three Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my framework for the economic and political passage through advanced automation, had been public on my site since March 23, 2026. A public link to Plan A appeared on July 9. That chronology establishes only that my framework was not written in response to Plan A. It does not turn their scenario into evidence that my dates are right.

I think the overlap matters for a narrower reason. Both works are forced to move the economic unit of account from labor to infrastructure. Once machines perform a large share of valuable work, the decisive questions become who may operate the productive capacity, how society taxes it, and who owns a claim on what it produces.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A is a policy scenario, not confirmation of my forecast. Its importance is that a separate team reaches the same structural bottleneck: an AI economy eventually has to govern and distribute the output of productive infrastructure rather than rely mainly on wages.</p></div>

## Both maps move the tax base

The current PDF of *The Three Crucibles* names its stages The Correction, The Void, and The Alignment. The book's landing page still calls the second Crucible The Transformation; in the current framework map, The Transformation is a chapter inside The Void. The Correction is a collapse in AI hype and the financial protection around inefficient work. The Void follows when business output recovers without a comparable recovery in knowledge work. The Alignment is the political choice between broad stewardship of abundance and concentrated ownership.

Plan A has no close equivalent to The Correction. It begins with geopolitical competition, imagines a United States–China agreement in 2029, and avoids fully automated AI research in 2030. It then holds systems within the range of human ability until a 2035 pause at “top-expert” AI, before a proposed move to superintelligence in 2040. Superintelligence means systems that are faster and significantly better than the best humans across virtually every field.

The economic middle is where the maps meet. In Plan A's scenario, AI performs one third of all cognitive labor by mid-2031. Cognitive labor means paid work centered on information, analysis, communication, and judgment rather than physical action. As that work moves to machines, payroll and personal-income taxes fall. Companies meanwhile reinvest revenue in datacenters and robots, whose costs reduce taxable profit. The report's conclusion is blunt: without reform, the tax base collapses as a share of the economy.

Plan A answers with tradable permits, government-issued rights to build limited amounts of computing capacity or robots. Its scenario has the United States collect $50 trillion in permit fees in fiscal year 2032, then distribute much of the revenue through a Citizen's Dividend that begins at $45,000 per adult and approaches $1 million by 2035. These are projections from an uncertain economic model, not observed outcomes.

My book reaches the same demand problem in simpler language: “Robots do not buy iPhones.” It proposes AI labor taxation and a Citizen's Dividend so that productivity can keep funding the people whose spending sustains the economy. Both works also invoke the Alaska Permanent Fund, created by Alaska voters in 1976 to reserve part of the state's mineral wealth and used to pay its first resident dividend in 1982. I think that shared precedent is more revealing than the shared label. Both maps treat automated production as a resource from which citizens need an enduring claim.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The strongest convergence is an accounting mechanism: automation weakens taxes on labor, investment can suppress taxable corporate profit, and governments seek revenue from scarce productive capacity instead. Both works then turn that revenue into a citizen dividend tied to a shared asset.</p></div>

## Convergence does not confirm the clock

The dates remain sharply different. My First Crucible places a market correction in 2026, while Plan A does not make a financial crash part of its sequence. Plan A's explosive economic phase comes later and depends on an international agreement that its own authors recommend but do not expect as their best-guess future.

The matching middle therefore cannot validate either calendar. Nor can the phrase “Citizen's Dividend” carry that burden; the term predates both works. What the timing and specificity show is that two projects with different starting points encountered the same chain: less human labor income, a weaker conventional tax base, a new levy on AI capacity, and a legal route back to citizens.

My take is that this convergence validates the question, not the answer. The question is whether the institutions built for an economy of human earners can survive an economy in which ownership does more of the distributing than employment. If labor income and labor-tax receipts remain robust while capable AI spreads through the 2030s, the shared diagnosis weakens. If public revenue begins migrating toward compute, robots, or AI output, it strengthens.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The two works disagree on the correction, the pace, and the political path. Their overlap becomes useful only when stated as a falsifiable institutional claim: watch whether income and taxation move from human work toward ownership and control of AI capacity.</p></div>

## Verification cannot substitute for ownership

Plan A's central control is total research transparency: frontier AI projects share research details, while major datacenters receive monitoring devices that verify whether chips are being used only for approved tasks. This is compute verification, the process of checking where advanced processors are and what kind of work they perform.

The report stress-tests that idea in a scenario appendix. It imagines China withholding part of a smuggled chip stockpile and building hidden capacity while inspectors watch declared datacenters. Its analysts estimate that the chance of such a project reaching top-expert AI undetected remains below ten percent until 2043. That is a scenario estimate, not evidence that verification will work, but it makes the residual risk visible.

This branch exposes the strength and limit of Plan A's bet. Verification can make cheating harder and give countries a reason to trust a shared cap. Yet a perfectly counted processor still belongs to someone, and inspection alone says nothing about who receives its income.

*The Three Crucibles* makes distributed AI ownership one of its three final pillars, alongside basic income and taxation of AI labor. Distributed ownership means spreading control of AI infrastructure and models among individuals, cooperatives, municipalities, and other institutions rather than leaving the intelligence layer with a few firms. That can spread economic power, but it does not solve Plan A's central problem: how to prevent highly capable systems from escaping human control.

I don't know what balance of inspection, international trust, and distributed ownership could survive severe geopolitical pressure. I think the honest conclusion is that each work supplies a missing layer for the other. Verification governs what capacity may do. Ownership determines who benefits when it does it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Plan A addresses hidden capacity and loss of control; The Three Crucibles addresses concentrated claims on productive output. Inspection cannot distribute wealth, while distributed ownership cannot by itself make superhuman systems safe.</p></div>

## The forecast to watch is institutional

The most useful signals will arrive before anyone can settle the argument about superintelligence. Fiscal policy will reveal whether governments still see labor as the durable tax base. Dividend design will reveal whether citizens receive temporary support or a legal share of AI-generated income. Governance rules will show whether transparency and ownership are being designed together or treated as substitutes.

The sequence matters. Measurement tells the state what productive capacity exists. Tax rules determine how much of its value becomes public revenue, and ownership rules determine whether citizens hold a lasting claim or depend on payments that can be withdrawn. I think the transition becomes politically durable only when those three layers reinforce one another.

The timetable may be wrong, the growth figures may be wrong, and the proposed international agreement may never exist. But if AI keeps shifting productive power away from human labor, a society that verifies the machines without distributing claims on their output will have solved only half the problem.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The decisive forecast is not a year for superintelligence. It is whether ownership law, tax policy, and technical verification evolve fast enough to preserve a human claim on machine output, because controlled intelligence without distributed economic power is still concentrated power.</p></div>
