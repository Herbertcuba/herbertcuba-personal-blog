AI makes execution cheaper before it makes an organization simpler. A company can draft proposals, classify requests, and prepare decisions faster while preserving every approval, meeting, and reporting line around that work. The output accelerates, but the institution stays where it was.

An AI-first company goes further. It designs a workflow around AI agents, software that can pursue a defined goal through several actions and approved tools, while people remain responsible for the consequences. I think the central design question is therefore about permission: once an agent can do and coordinate more of the work, who may decide, who must intervene, and who answers when the result is wrong?

[Arvind KC joined OpenAI as Chief People Officer in February 2026](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/). OpenAI is an AI research and product company that builds systems able to reason, write, and use software tools, so its people leader has a useful view of how advanced AI changes roles. A few months after joining, Arvind shared [ten reflections on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7485407006204493825/), the professional network, including *“Every role is a technical role”* and a description of OpenAI as *“designed for bottom-up innovation.”*

Benjamin Simkin offers a view from outside an AI developer. His independently published May 2026 book, [*The AI First Company*](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5), is a field manual for business owners who want to rebuild workflows around AI. Simkin calls the common alternative the *Retrofit Trap*: adding AI to parts of an inherited process while leaving its queues, handoffs, and authority untouched.

Neither account proves that every organization should flatten. Together, however, they expose the mechanism that matters. When agents remove routine execution and information relay, some hierarchy may lose its purpose, but authority itself does not disappear. The new org chart should show where permission and responsibility now live.

## The org chart is a permission system

Most organization charts look like maps of people, yet their deeper function is to distribute permission. They show who can commit money, change a policy, accept risk, settle a conflict, or stop work. Those permissions are often bundled with routine relay work, such as collecting status, chasing approvals, and translating updates between teams.

AI agents can take on much of that relay when the route is predictable. They can assemble evidence, apply an agreed rule, send a standard response, and direct an unusual case to the right person. Managers still coach people, allocate scarce resources, resolve competing goals, and accept responsibility for decisions whose consequences reach beyond one workflow. Removing the relay does not remove those obligations.

Consider an illustrative customer-refund process. An agent could verify the order, check the current policy, identify the payment method, and approve an ordinary refund within a company-set limit. A suspected fraud case, a threat of legal action, or a request that conflicts with another policy would move to a named person with authority to decide. If finance, legal, and customer service review every routine refund anyway, the agent has accelerated preparation while leaving coordination intact.

This is where *bottom-up innovation* becomes concrete. The phrase means that people close to the work can improve it and decide within known limits instead of sending every choice upward. Those limits are *decision rights*: explicit rules about who may make a decision, what evidence they need, and when the case must move to someone else. I think a company becomes meaningfully flatter when these rights let ordinary work stay close to its context without turning exceptional work into an ownerless queue.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents can remove predictable information relay, while human authority remains necessary for conflict, scarce resources, and consequential exceptions. A thinner structure works when decision rights keep ordinary choices local and give unusual cases a named owner.</p></div>

## Technical reasoning belongs in every role

Arvind's statement that *“every role is a technical role”* can sound like a demand for universal coding skills. Its practical meaning is broader and more useful. Technical reasoning is the ability to judge what a system can do, what evidence it used, where it tends to fail, and how those limits affect a business decision.

The customer-service leader in the refund example does not need to build the agent. That leader does need to know whether it checked the current policy, whether its evidence is recorded, which data it was allowed to access, and what makes it stop rather than act. Without that understanding, local authority is mostly theatre because every uncertain case must return to a central AI team.

That return path is the organizational version of Simkin's Retrofit Trap. The interface looks new, but the old dependency survives behind it. Shared technical reasoning changes the structure because commercial, operational, and creative leaders can judge systems at the level of their own decisions. Software engineering remains specialist work; informed authority no longer belongs to specialists alone.

My take is that technical reasoning will become a normal part of management in the same way financial reasoning already is. A leader does not need to prepare every account to understand margin, cash, and investment. In the same way, a leader can own an AI-enabled workflow without writing its code, provided that the leader can question its evidence, recognize its limits, and act when it fails.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical reasoning means understanding an AI system well enough to make sound decisions around it, rather than becoming a software engineer. When that ability spreads beyond a central technical team, authority can move closer to the work without creating a new approval queue.</p></div>

## Measure whether coordination actually declined

A flatter structure should follow operating evidence, not an announcement. Before changing roles, leaders can record how a workflow performs today: elapsed time, the number of human handoffs, the cost of errors and rework, how long exceptions wait, and how many hours owners spend resolving them. The point is to establish whether the redesign removes work or merely moves it to less visible places.

The first trial can run in *shadow mode*, which means the agent proposes actions while people continue to make the real decisions. Comparing those proposals with human choices reveals weak evidence, unclear ownership, and exceptions that the original process concealed. It also gives the team a chance to set stop conditions before the agent receives permission to act.

Hidden coordination often appears after the headline measure improves. Turnaround time may fall while domain owners spend more evenings resolving unusual cases, or while issues crossing finance and customer service wait longer for someone with sufficient authority. If that happens, the organization has created a faster normal path by overloading its human edge. The productivity chart looks healthy because the displaced work is being counted in the wrong place.

I don't know a universal ratio that tells a board when a management layer has become unnecessary. Different work carries different consequences, and a fixed span of control would pretend otherwise. I think the defensible rule is simpler: combine or remove a role only after the workflow shows a sustained drop in routine relay, without a rise in error cost, exception age, or hidden owner time. If the evidence fails that test, improve the workflow and keep the authority the business still needs.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Baseline the workflow, test the agent in shadow mode, and measure errors, exceptions, handoffs, and owner time together. A layer has become optional only when coordination falls without being displaced into slower escalations or invisible human effort.</p></div>

## A thinner hierarchy changes the career bargain

Arvind's eighth reflection says every role should carry the suffix *“agent manager.”* I read this as a capability rather than a new title. An agent manager defines the goal, sets boundaries, evaluates the result, and improves the system when repeated failures reveal a weak rule or missing context.

This shift creates a human problem that efficiency language tends to hide. Traditional career ladders often connect progress to a larger team, a wider reporting span, and more approval power. When agents reduce routine supervision and relay work, some of those steps disappear. Calling the company flat does not replace the status, pay progression, and opportunity that the old pyramid offered.

The support specialist in the refund workflow might grow into a *domain owner*, the person accountable for the end-to-end outcome in that area. That could mean authority to change standards, pause the agent, resolve common exceptions, and influence the policies causing repeated failure. The destination is credible only when development, decision rights, and compensation move with the responsibility.

Some people will move toward deeper expertise, coaching, system design, or domain ownership. Some jobs will contain too little necessary work once execution and relay have changed. I think leaders damage trust when they present that transition as empowerment while leaving people with a larger burden and a smaller future. The honest work is to identify which paths are real, fund the learning they require, and say plainly where no durable path exists.

A single workflow is enough to begin. Redesign it, test its boundaries, watch where human judgment remains necessary, and change the surrounding roles only when the evidence holds. The org chart can then become a record of how the company actually works rather than a picture of its AI ambition.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>When agents reduce routine coordination, companies must replace managerial status with paid paths to deeper expertise, wider judgment, and responsible system ownership. A thinner hierarchy is credible only when authority and human progression are redesigned together; execution may become cheap, but responsibility does not.</p></div>
