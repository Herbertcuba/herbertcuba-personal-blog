The org chart should be the last thing an AI-first company changes. Agents may clear queues, prepare decisions, and keep systems updated, but a management role contains more than the movement of information. It may also contain coaching, conflict resolution, resource allocation, and authority over risk. Delete the role too early and those responsibilities return as private messages, crowded calendars, and decisions that belong to whoever happens to answer first.

An AI-first company designs work around what artificial intelligence can reliably do instead of attaching AI to an unchanged human process. An [AI agent](https://openai.com/index/introducing-chatgpt-agent/) is software that can pursue a defined goal through several steps and approved tools. It might collect evidence, compare it with policy, update a business system, or send an unusual case to a person. The important distinction is that completing and routing work does not make the software accountable for its consequences.

[Arvind KC joined OpenAI as Chief People Officer in February 2026](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/). OpenAI is an AI research and product company developing systems that can write, reason, and use software tools, so its senior people leader has a relevant view of how advanced AI changes jobs. A few months after joining, KC shared [ten reflections on LinkedIn, the professional network](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7485407006204493825/). They included “Every role is a technical role,” the description of OpenAI as “designed for bottom-up innovation,” and the idea that every role also carries the suffix “agent manager.”

Benjamin Simkin approaches the same question from the owner’s side. His independently published May 2026 book, [*The AI First Company*](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5), is a field manual for business owners redesigning work around AI. Simkin calls the weaker approach the *Retrofit Trap*: adding AI around an old workflow while preserving the handoffs, queues, and authority that shaped it.

Neither account proves that a flat structure fits every business. I think their shared value is more precise. They show why the shape of a company can change after agents absorb routine execution and coordination, and why that change fails when leaders confuse one removable part of management with the whole job.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI agents can remove the routine movement of work without removing coaching, conflict, resource choices, or accountability. The organization should change only after leaders know which parts of management have truly disappeared and where the rest will live.</p></div>

## A management layer is a bundle of jobs

Imagine a supplier-onboarding process. An agent gathers registration documents, checks required fields, compares contract terms with company policy, and prepares an ordinary case for approval. It can also keep procurement, finance, legal, and security informed without a manager carrying the status from one function to another. This is an illustrative workflow, not a reported deployment, but it shows the kind of relay work that software can plausibly absorb.

The same workflow also produces cases that cannot be settled by routing. A supplier may need access to sensitive customer information, demand an unusual liability clause, or become essential to a product launch. Someone still has to weigh speed against exposure, resolve disagreement between functions, and accept the result. The agent can assemble the decision; it cannot inherit the company’s authority merely because it assembled the file.

That distinction changes how a leader should inspect a layer. Look at the work inside the role and separate repeated collection and routing from judgment, development, and conflict. I think a layer becomes a candidate for removal only when its relay work has ended and each remaining responsibility has a credible owner. Otherwise the company has hidden the management work without reducing it.

The warning signs are ordinary. Cases begin waiting in informal review queues while senior leaders spend more time reconstructing context. Experienced employees become unofficial coordinators because they know whom to ask, so a flatter chart may look efficient while the actual workflow becomes more dependent on favors and memory.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Management combines routine relay work with human responsibilities that automation may leave intact. A layer is genuinely removable only when the relay has ended and coaching, conflict, resource allocation, and risk each have somewhere credible to go.</p></div>

## Technical roles begin with consequences

KC’s phrase “Every role is a technical role” can sound like a universal coding requirement. I think it is better understood as a standard of judgment. Anyone who owns a business outcome needs enough knowledge of the system producing it to question the evidence, recognize the failures relevant to the job, and stop the process when the risk crosses an agreed boundary.

In the supplier workflow, a procurement owner does not need to build the model. That person does need to understand where the agent found its evidence, which records may be incomplete, and what conditions require legal or security review. The lawyer, security specialist, and AI engineer will ask different questions because they own different consequences. Technical depth varies; accountability does not disappear.

KC’s “agent manager” extends that responsibility. *Agent management* means setting the goal, limiting what the software may read or change, inspecting its evidence, handling failures, and improving the workflow when the same exception returns. A central AI team can provide secure infrastructure, shared controls, and specialist help. If every uncertain decision goes back to that team, however, the company has replaced an old management queue with a technical one.

This is also what bottom-up innovation requires in practice. The phrase means people close to the work can improve a process and decide within known limits instead of sending every change upward. Those limits matter. Local freedom works when people understand both the technology and the consequences attached to their decisions.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A role becomes technical at the point where its owner must judge an AI system’s evidence and consequences. Central experts protect shared boundaries, while people near the work need enough understanding to act responsibly within them.</p></div>

## Flatness changes the career promise

Management layers do more than organize work. They also carry status, higher pay, broader influence, coaching responsibility, and an understandable next step. When routine coordination shrinks, part of that career structure shrinks with it. A company cannot call the change empowering while leaving employees with fewer credible ways to grow.

One possible route is *domain ownership*: responsibility for an end-to-end business outcome, including authority to change standards, pause an agent, and address recurring exceptions at their cause. A supplier coordinator who once chased documents might develop into an owner of supplier integrity across the full workflow. This is a possible role design rather than a promised promotion. It requires deeper knowledge, protected learning time, real authority, and pay that reflects the wider consequences of the job.

Other people may advance through expert practice, system design, or coaching without collecting more direct reports. Some positions will still disappear because too little necessary work remains. I don’t know a clean formula that makes this transition painless. I think leaders owe people clarity about which responsibilities are ending, which are moving, and what someone must learn to qualify for the work that remains.

The cost of redesign therefore includes training, temporary overlap, and honest treatment of people for whom no credible destination exists. Those are architecture costs, just like software and integration. Leaving them out does not make the new company leaner; it transfers the bill to employees.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A flatter company removes part of the old career ladder along with coordination work. It needs real progression through domain ownership, expertise, system design, or coaching, backed by learning time, authority, and pay, while speaking plainly about roles that will end.</p></div>

## Let the work redraw the chart

The redesign can begin with one workflow. Record the ordinary path, the actions an agent may take, the evidence a person needs, and the conditions that stop automation. Then write the *decision rights*: who may decide, within which limits, and where an exception goes when it crosses them. This reveals the future organization in the work before anyone moves a reporting line.

Run the workflow long enough to see where people still have to relay context or hunt for permission. Watch the age of serious exceptions, the time senior owners spend settling cross-functional cases, and the amount of informal supervision that appears around the system. There is no universal threshold, but the direction should be clear: routine cases move with less human relay while difficult cases reach qualified owners without losing context.

Simkin offers a useful clean-slate question: “If I were building this business today, from scratch, knowing what AI can do, would it look like this?” Applied to a workflow, that question forces a company to examine each approval and handoff instead of preserving it through habit. Applied only to the org chart, it can become permission to cut before the new decision system exists.

My take is that a company earns a flatter structure when the work no longer needs the old relays, the people at the edges can judge the systems they use, and responsibility remains visible when something goes wrong. Until then, the pyramid may be inefficient, but deleting it is cosmetic. The org chart should become the record of a redesign that already works.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Redesign one workflow first: define the agent’s actions, the evidence, the stop conditions, and the owners of exceptions. When routine coordination has truly left the work and necessary judgment has a credible home, the chart can change as evidence rather than aspiration.</p></div>
