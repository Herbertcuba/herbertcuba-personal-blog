The case for removing management layers in an AI-first company sounds simple. Agents can complete more work, so fewer people should be needed to organize it. The conclusion may be right, but it arrives before the important question: what kind of work has actually disappeared?

An AI-first company designs workflows around the capabilities of artificial intelligence instead of attaching AI to an unchanged human process. An AI agent is software that can pursue a defined goal across several steps and approved tools. It might gather evidence, prepare a decision, update a business system, or route an unusual case. Those abilities can reduce execution without changing who waits for whom, who settles a conflict, or who carries the risk when the normal path fails.

[Arvind KC joined OpenAI as Chief People Officer in February 2026](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/). OpenAI is an AI research and product company developing systems that can write, reason, and use software tools, so its senior people leader has a relevant view of how advanced AI changes jobs. A few months after joining, KC shared [ten reflections on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7485407006204493825/), including “Every role is a technical role” and the claim that OpenAI is “designed for bottom-up innovation.”

Benjamin Simkin approaches the same subject from the owner’s side. His independently published May 2026 book, [*The AI First Company*](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5), is a field manual for business owners rebuilding work around AI. Simkin calls the weaker approach the *Retrofit Trap*: putting AI around the edges of an old workflow while keeping its queues, handoffs, and authority intact.

Neither account proves that every company should become flat. I think their value lies in the mechanism they reveal. A flatter structure becomes possible when technical judgment moves into ordinary roles, routine coordination leaves the workflow, and human authority becomes clearer rather than merely less visible. The org chart should record that change after it happens.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI can make tasks faster without changing the organization around them. A company earns a flatter structure only when the workflow needs fewer human relays, people close to the work can judge the system, and exceptions still reach someone with authority.</p></div>

## Technical judgment moves closer to the work

KC’s statement that every role is technical can sound like a demand for universal coding skills. I think the more useful reading is about judgment. Anyone who owns a business outcome now needs enough understanding of the system producing it to examine the evidence, recognize likely failure modes, and decide when its output should be stopped.

Consider an illustrative maintenance workflow in a factory. An agent could review sensor alerts, check the equipment manual, compare the signal with recent repairs, and propose a service window. The maintenance leader does not need to build the model. Yet that leader cannot truly own equipment reliability without knowing whether the sensor data is complete, whether the manual is current, and which warning signs make an automated recommendation unsafe.

KC’s eighth reflection gives every role another suffix: “agent manager.” I interpret *agent management* as setting the goal, limiting what the agent may access or change, inspecting its evidence, handling failures, and improving the workflow when the same exception returns. Domain knowledge remains central because the agent cannot decide what counts as an acceptable business outcome. It applies that knowledge at greater speed and scale.

A central AI team still has important work. It can provide secure infrastructure, shared controls, evaluation methods, and specialist help. But if every uncertain decision returns to that team, a new technical queue replaces the old one. I think judgment has to sit near the business context, while central specialists protect the boundaries that individual teams should not set alone.

This does not give every employee the same technical depth. It gives each role enough depth for the consequences it owns. The maintenance leader, security specialist, finance director, and AI engineer will ask different questions, but none can treat the system as a sealed box and remain accountable for its output.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A role becomes technical when its owner can question an AI system’s evidence, understand the failures relevant to the job, and stop the system when necessary. Central experts support that judgment, but business accountability cannot be sent back to a technical help desk.</p></div>

## Coordination has to disappear before a layer does

A management role usually combines several kinds of work. Managers coach people, resolve competing priorities, allocate scarce resources, accept risk, collect updates, and route decisions. AI agents may absorb the collection and routing while leaving coaching, conflict, and accountability fully intact. Treating that partial change as the disappearance of management confuses a task with a function.

The factory example makes the distinction visible. Routine maintenance might move from detection to diagnosis, parts availability, and scheduling without a manager carrying status between teams. A damaged safety control is different. That case may affect production, worker safety, insurance, and capital spending, so it needs a direct route to people authorized to weigh those consequences.

Those routes depend on *decision rights*: written boundaries that state who may decide, what evidence they need, and which conditions require escalation. When the boundaries are clear, ordinary work can move horizontally and serious exceptions can rise without touring the hierarchy first. When they are vague, experienced employees become unofficial coordinators who spend their time finding context and permission.

This is the practical meaning of KC’s phrase *bottom-up innovation*. People near the work can improve a process and decide within known limits instead of sending each change upward. The result may look untidy because authority is spread across more places. Useful freedom still has edges, especially when a decision creates material financial exposure, crosses business areas, or changes the risk accepted by the company.

I don’t know a universal span of control that settles when a layer can go. The answer depends on the cost of failure, the variety of unusual cases, and the amount of judgment the work still needs. I think leaders should watch where cases wait, how long serious exceptions remain unresolved, how much time senior owners spend chasing cross-functional decisions, and whether informal supervision rises as formal supervision falls.

My take is that flattening should proceed workflow by workflow. First prove that routine cases move without human relay and that exceptions reach qualified owners quickly. Then change reporting lines. Reversing that order produces a lean diagram while the removed layer survives as hidden work in private messages, review queues, and overloaded calendars.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A reporting layer becomes removable when its routine relay work has ended, while coaching, conflict resolution, and risk still have clear owners. If employees must recreate coordination informally, the hierarchy has moved out of sight rather than disappeared.</p></div>

## A flatter structure needs a new career bargain

Management layers carry more than coordination. They also provide coaching, status, higher pay, broader influence, and a visible next step. When routine relay work shrinks, part of that career system shrinks with it. Calling the company flatter does not tell an employee how greater skill or responsibility will be recognized.

One possible destination is *domain ownership*: responsibility for an end-to-end business outcome, with authority to change standards, pause an agent, resolve recurring exceptions, and address their causes. In the factory example, a maintenance coordinator who once chased updates might grow into an equipment-reliability owner who sets intervention rules, reviews severe failures, and can stop the automated workflow. That is an illustrative path, not an automatic promotion. It requires technical learning, protected time, real decision rights, and pay that reflects the wider consequences of the role.

Other people may progress through deeper expertise, system design, or coaching without managing a larger team. Some positions will still disappear because too little necessary work remains. Leaders should say so plainly. Otherwise “empowerment” becomes a pleasant description for concentrating judgment in fewer hands while everyone else loses the ladder they were told to climb.

I think the people transition belongs inside the architecture decision, before a layer is removed. Leaders should know which responsibilities have ended, which have moved, who will carry them, and how employees can qualify for the work that remains. The cost of redesign includes training, temporary overlap, manual exception capacity, and honest treatment of people for whom no credible destination exists.

That is the harder standard for an AI-first company. Agents can make routine execution abundant, but responsibility remains scarce because it requires context, judgment, and a willingness to carry consequences. A company deserves fewer layers only when the work needs fewer relays and the people carrying more judgment receive the authority, skill, and future that judgment demands.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Removing management layers also removes part of the career ladder. A responsible redesign creates credible paths into domain ownership, expertise, system design, or coaching, backed by decision rights, learning time, and pay, while speaking honestly about roles that will end.</p></div>
