AI can flatten the flow of work long before it makes a flat company safe. An agent may prepare decisions, carry context between functions, and keep a process moving without human reminders. Yet a manager often does several other jobs at once: coaching people, settling conflict, allocating scarce resources, and accepting risk. If the box disappears while those responsibilities remain, the company has changed its diagram more than its operating system.

An AI-first company designs work around what artificial intelligence can reliably do, rather than attaching AI to an otherwise unchanged process. An [AI agent](https://openai.com/index/introducing-chatgpt-agent/) is software that can pursue a defined goal through several steps and approved tools. It can gather evidence, take permitted actions, and send unusual cases to a person. It cannot become accountable for the business consequence simply because it moved the case forward.

Two recent sources make the structural question unusually clear. [Arvind KC joined OpenAI as Chief People Officer in February 2026](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/). OpenAI is an AI research and product company developing systems that can write, reason, and use software tools, which makes its senior people leader a useful observer of how advanced AI changes jobs. A few months later, KC published [ten reflections on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7485407006204493825/), including “Every role is a technical role,” a description of OpenAI as “designed for bottom-up innovation,” and the idea that every role also carries the suffix “agent manager.”

Benjamin Simkin comes at the same issue from the business owner’s side. His independently published May 2026 book, [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5), is a field manual for owners redesigning companies around AI. He calls the weaker approach the *Retrofit Trap*: adding AI around an old human workflow while preserving the handoffs, queues, and authority structure that made it slow.

Neither source proves that flatter structures suit every company. I think they offer something more useful than a universal prescription: a mechanism we can examine. When agents remove routine execution and the relay work around it, some management layers may become thinner. Whether they can disappear depends on where judgment, authority, and responsibility go next.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI can remove the movement of work without removing the human obligations bundled into management. A flatter company becomes credible only when leaders can show where judgment, authority, coaching, and accountability will live.</p></div>

## Agents reduce traffic before they reduce responsibility

Consider an illustrative customer-renewal workflow. An agent could collect contract terms, product usage, support history, and payment status, then prepare a renewal recommendation. It could update the customer system and route an ordinary case to the account owner without a manager asking four teams for status. That removes real work, including much of the coordination that once made the process legible.

The same workflow will eventually produce a case that does not fit. A strategic customer may demand an unusual liability clause, a large discount, or access that creates a security concern. The agent can assemble the relevant evidence, but someone still has to weigh revenue against exposure and decide which risk the company will accept. Moving information and holding authority are different functions.

This is why a management layer must be inspected as a bundle of jobs rather than a single cost. Repeated collection, status reporting, and routing are strong candidates for automation. Coaching, conflict resolution, resource choices, and responsibility for difficult outcomes may remain. I think a layer becomes removable only when its routine traffic has ended and every remaining obligation has a named, capable owner.

Otherwise the coordination has merely moved out of sight. Senior specialists start rebuilding context themselves, employees create informal approval networks, and the fastest response becomes the default decision. The official hierarchy is flatter, but the real hierarchy survives as access to a few overloaded people.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents can absorb collection, routing, and status work while leaving judgment and people responsibilities intact. Removing a layer too early pushes management into informal channels and concentrates hidden power around whoever still has context.</p></div>

## A technical role can answer for the system

KC’s line “Every role is a technical role” is easy to misread as a demand that everyone learn to code. I think the better interpretation is *technical reasoning*: enough understanding of the system behind a business outcome to question its evidence, recognize the failures that matter, and stop it when the risk crosses an agreed limit. The required depth changes by role because the consequences change by role.

In the renewal example, the account owner does not need to build the model. That person does need to know which records shaped the recommendation, where the evidence may be stale, and when legal, finance, or security must become involved. The specialists will ask different questions, but none can treat the agent’s output as someone else’s technical problem.

KC’s “agent manager” adds an operating responsibility to that literacy. *Agent management* means setting a goal, limiting what the software may read or change, checking the evidence behind its actions, and improving the workflow when the same failure returns. A central AI team can provide secure tools and shared controls. It cannot own every decision without becoming a new queue between the technology and the people carrying the business consequences.

This also explains bottom-up innovation, KC’s term for improvements initiated by people close to the work rather than handed down through several approval layers. Local freedom is useful only inside clear limits. People at the edge need enough technical understanding to act, and *decision rights*, the explicit rules for who may decide what and within which boundaries, must give that action legitimate authority.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A role becomes technical when its owner must understand and challenge the system producing the result. Agent management combines that literacy with clear limits, while decision rights let people near the work act without sending every uncertain case back to a central team.</p></div>

## Evidence has to earn the flatter structure

Simkin asks a useful clean-slate question: “If I were building this business today, from scratch, knowing what AI can do, would it look like this?” Applied to a workflow, it exposes approvals and handoffs that survive mainly through habit. Applied straight to the organization chart, it can become an excuse to cut before a working decision system exists.

The safer sequence begins with the work. Map one workflow from request to outcome, state what the agent may do, and define which conditions require a person. Then assign each exception to someone with the knowledge and authority to resolve it. Run the new design alongside the existing process until leaders can see whether ordinary cases move with less human relay and difficult cases arrive with their context intact.

The measurements do not need to be exotic. Watch how often a person must reconstruct the case, how long serious exceptions wait, how much senior time the workflow consumes, and whether the same manual rescue repeats. My prediction is that premature flattening will first appear as rising expert load and growing informal coordination even while the headline output looks healthy. Automation can increase completed volume at the same time that it makes the remaining human work harder.

There is no universal threshold because a marketing approval and a security incident carry different costs. The evidence should still point in one direction: routine coordination is falling, exceptions are reaching qualified owners faster, and the organization is not recreating the removed layer through favors and emergency meetings. If those conditions fail, the workflow needs more design before the company needs fewer managers.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Start with a workflow, define the agent’s limits and the owners of exceptions, then measure where human coordination reappears. A flatter structure is earned when routine relay falls without increasing expert overload, delayed exceptions, or informal supervision.</p></div>

## Flatness rewrites the career agreement

Management layers have never served only the workflow. They also carry status, pay progression, wider influence, and an understandable next step. When agents reduce coordination-heavy roles, they shorten that ladder. A company cannot describe the change as empowerment if employees receive more responsibility but fewer credible ways to advance.

One possible destination is *domain ownership*: responsibility for an end-to-end business outcome, with authority to change standards, pause the agent, and address recurring exceptions at their cause. In the renewal workflow, a coordinator who once chased updates might grow into an owner of renewal integrity across sales, finance, legal, and security. That is a possible role design, not an automatic promotion. It requires deeper knowledge, protected learning time, real decision rights, and compensation that reflects the wider consequence of the work.

Other people may progress through expert practice, system design, or coaching without collecting direct reports. Some positions will still end because too little necessary work remains. I don’t know a clean formula that makes that transition painless. I think leaders owe people a clear account of which responsibilities are disappearing, which are moving, and what someone must learn to qualify for the work that remains.

Training, temporary overlap, and fair support for people whose roles end belong in the cost of the redesign. Excluding them does not create a leaner transformation. It transfers the cost from the program budget to the people who made the previous system work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A flatter organization also removes part of the old career system. Credible alternatives require domain authority, expert and coaching paths, protected learning time, and honest treatment of people whose work does not survive the redesign.</p></div>

## Let the chart record the operating reality

KC writes that a company designed for bottom-up innovation may look chaotic. Some of that visible disorder is healthy because decisions happen close to the work instead of waiting for permission from above. But missing ownership can look chaotic too. The difference is whether people know the limits of their authority, whether important actions can be traced, and whether an exception has a clear destination.

That distinction matters because a clean chart can hide slow work, while a distributed company can look untidy and still be controlled. Control does not require every decision to travel upward. It requires visible responsibility, shared boundaries, and a reliable way to intervene when local action creates wider risk.

My take is that leaders should treat the organization chart as the record of an operating change, not the instrument that creates it. Redesign the workflow, teach people to judge the systems they use, place authority beside the consequences, and build a career structure for the work that remains. Then the reporting lines can follow.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The flatter chart should document a reality already proven in the work: coordination has genuinely declined, authority remains visible, and people can still grow. Until those conditions exist, deleting a layer only makes the organization’s missing design harder to see.</p></div>
