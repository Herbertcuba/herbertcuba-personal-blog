A workflow can become faster while the company around it becomes more fragile. Routine cases move automatically, the dashboard improves, and senior people quietly spend more time rescuing the difficult cases. Remove a management layer at that moment and the hierarchy does not vanish. It returns as an informal queue around the few people who still understand the risk.

I use *AI-first* for a business that rebuilds a process around the dependable abilities and known limits of artificial intelligence. By [AI agent](https://openai.com/index/introducing-chatgpt-agent/), I mean a software system assigned an outcome, a narrow toolset, and room to choose its next step inside stated boundaries. Such a system can move a case forward. It still cannot accept business liability, care for an employee, or answer for a harmful decision.

In February 2026, [OpenAI appointed Arvind KC as Chief People Officer](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/). The company researches and builds AI systems that can write, reason, and operate software, which places its people chief close to jobs being redesigned around those abilities. A few months into the role, KC wrote on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7485407006204493825/) that “Every role is a technical role,” that OpenAI is “designed for bottom-up innovation,” and that every role carries the suffix “agent manager.”

KC’s “technical role” is best understood as a duty to know enough about the system behind a decision to question its evidence and stop unsafe output. Bottom-up innovation, in this context, gives the person who sees a problem enough scope to improve the process without climbing several approval rungs. Agent management means setting the software’s goal and limits, then judging what it produces.

Author Benjamin Simkin writes for owners rather than AI researchers. [His May 2026 independently published book, *The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5), is a 35-chapter guide to rebuilding an existing business around AI. Simkin calls it the *Retrofit Trap* when a company adds AI to an old process but leaves its handoffs and decision structure intact. He asks owners to imagine rebuilding today with current AI capabilities rather than treating an inherited process as fixed. I think the question is valuable, but the organization chart should be the last place leaders apply it.

The sharper question is whether a company can prove that its human system still works after agents absorb part of the flow. That proof must cover difficult decisions, senior capacity, coaching, conflict, and career movement. Otherwise a flatter chart is only a faster way to hide unfinished management work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI can speed up work without making the surrounding organization ready to flatten. Reporting lines should change only after leaders prove that authority, expert attention, people responsibilities, and career progression still work under pressure.</p></div>

## Workflow speed is only the first result

A useful Nordic anchor comes from [Klarna, the Swedish digital bank and payments provider](https://www.klarna.com/international/about-us/). In February 2024, Klarna [reported](https://www.klarna.com/international/press/klarna-ai-assistant-handles-two-thirds-of-customer-service-chats-in-its-first-month/) that its OpenAI-powered assistant had handled 2.3 million conversations in one month, equal to two-thirds of its customer-service chats. The company said repeat inquiries fell by 25 percent, average resolution time dropped from 11 minutes to less than two, customer satisfaction matched human agents, and the work was equivalent to 700 full-time agents.

Those are meaningful workflow results, especially because they describe volume, speed, repeat demand, and customer response rather than activity inside the AI tool. They are also company-reported figures from the first month. They do not tell us whether managers spent less time coordinating, whether specialists received harder cases with enough context, or whether the workforce design remained effective over time. Klarna demonstrates that AI can compress a large service flow. It does not prove that a management layer can safely disappear.

That gap is where *expert load* matters: the time qualified people spend reviewing, correcting, and deciding cases that require real judgment. An automated service may close more conversations while fraud, regulatory, or vulnerable-customer cases pile up around a smaller group. I think this is the earliest useful warning. If expert load rises faster than routine relay falls, the system has concentrated complexity rather than removed it.

Managers also carry duties that do not appear in a service metric. They coach, settle disagreements, allocate scarce attention, explain decisions, and answer for outcomes. A workflow result can justify changing some of that work, but it cannot make the duties disappear. The same is true of careers: removing a layer also removes some routes to higher pay, broader influence, and structured development.

A counterexample therefore limits the whole argument. If an agent improves speed and volume but senior rescue work grows, serious exceptions age, or employees lose credible support and progression, then AI has not earned a flatter company. I think leaders should be willing to accept that result even when the headline numbers look excellent.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Klarna’s reported results show that AI can compress a major customer-service workflow, but workflow performance is not evidence of safe organizational flattening. Rising expert load, delayed high-risk cases, or weakened people support would disprove the case for removing a layer.</p></div>

## Use a test that can say no

A strategic claim becomes useful when a team can fail it. I would use an **earned-layer test** with five conditions, all measured against the existing workflow:

1. **Routine relay falls.** People spend less time collecting updates, copying context between systems, and chasing ordinary approvals.
2. **Serious exceptions move faster.** A case outside policy reaches a qualified person with the evidence needed to decide, and the decision can be traced afterward.
3. **Expert load stays within capacity.** Senior review time, exception age, and repeated manual rescue remain below limits agreed before the trial.
4. **Every remaining management duty has an owner.** Coaching, conflict resolution, resource choices, risk acceptance, and performance decisions have named, capable owners.
5. **Career movement remains credible.** Employees can see how responsibility, skill, influence, and pay can grow when the old ladder becomes shorter.

The workflow passes only when all five conditions hold. A team should stop or roll back, restoring the previous process, if any of four conditions appears: a severe exception requires a decision beyond the authority delegated to the workflow or its named owner; the expert queue breaches its agreed capacity; the same failure repeatedly needs manual repair; or a management duty remains without an owner. The thresholds will differ between customer service and financial crime review, where suspicious transactions face stricter checks, because the consequences differ. The decision rule should not.

I think this test improves the conversation because it separates two questions that executives often merge. One asks whether AI can perform enough of the work. The other asks whether the organization can still make sound decisions and support people with one less layer. Only the second answer can justify changing the chart.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The earned-layer test requires falling relay work, faster qualified exceptions, bounded expert load, owners for every residual duty, and credible career movement. Any failed condition is a reason to keep the structure and redesign the workflow.</p></div>

## Run the trial so you can reverse it

The safest starting point is a deterministic workflow, a fixed sequence in which the same known condition should trigger the same action. Ordinary routing, required checks, and hard policy limits belong there. An *agent loop*, in which software chooses its next approved step as it works, is justified only where uncertainty requires gathering evidence, choosing among tools, or preparing a judgment for a person. I think that distinction prevents leaders from turning stable business rules into unpredictable AI behavior for no reason.

A practical trial needs a written envelope. For example, give one workflow owner six to eight weeks and a capped budget that includes software, security review, human review, training, and temporary overlap with the existing process. Record the baseline for relay time, exception age, expert hours, cost, and the relevant customer result, such as resolution time or repeat contact, before the trial begins. Reserve enough qualified people to handle the expected exception queue, then define the point at which excess demand stops the trial rather than becoming unpaid overtime.

The system boundary should use *least privilege*, meaning the agent receives only the data access and actions needed for this workflow. A service agent might issue a standard refund within an approved limit, but a fraud flag or policy exception must block that action and send the case to a named owner. An *audit trail* should record the evidence, software actions, case-status changes, and human approvals so a failed case can be replayed. The rollback path should restore the previous process if the AI model, outside supplier, or connection to another system becomes unavailable or behaves outside the agreed limits.

People enter the design before the trial changes their jobs. The affected team, HR, and any relevant workforce or union representatives should review the management duties that remain, training promise, workload limits, and stop conditions while reversal is still possible. This is also when the full transition cost becomes visible. Software is only one line; review capacity, security work, learning time, temporary overlap, and support for roles that may end belong in the same decision.

A reversible trial creates real commitment because it makes success and failure observable. It also protects the company from confusing enthusiasm with evidence. If leaders cannot state who may stop the system, how the old process returns, and who carries each difficult case in the meantime, they are not ready to use the trial as an argument for structural change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Begin with fixed rules, use an agent only where uncertainty earns it, and test within a fixed time, cost, permission, and review boundary. Trace every consequential action, involve affected employees early, and preserve a tested route back to the previous process.</p></div>

## A shorter hierarchy needs a new promise

Once coordination work genuinely falls, some roles will change and some may end. The responsible move is to design the next career system before deleting the old one. Otherwise specialists inherit more judgment and informal coaching while the company removes the title, authority, learning time, and pay that once came with those duties.

A flatter company can still offer growth through deeper expert practice, responsibility for a complete business result, system design, or formal coaching. Each route needs published expectations and compensation tied to the consequence of the work, not simply a new label. I think the earned-layer test should fail if employees cannot see how to qualify for these paths or if the redesign depends on invisible people work.

I don’t know a formula that makes every transition painless. Some employees will retrain, some will move, and some will leave because the necessary work is no longer there. Leaders can still be precise about what is disappearing, fund the learning required for the work that remains, and support people whose roles end instead of excluding those costs from the AI business case.

My take is that the reporting structure should be the final output of this work. Prove the workflow and its authority boundaries, then show that expert capacity and career commitments hold. Only then does a missing layer reflect changed work instead of abandoned responsibility.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A flatter company owes employees credible routes to deeper expertise, ownership of a complete business result, system design, coaching, and fair transition support. AI has earned a new org chart only when the work is safer, the authority is visible, and the people carrying the change can still build a future inside it.</p></div>
