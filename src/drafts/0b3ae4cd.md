An AI-first company is not simply a company in which everyone uses AI. It is a company that redesigns work around AI agents, software that can pursue a defined goal through several actions and approved tools, while people remain accountable for the outcome. Once those agents begin to handle both execution and routine coordination, the shape of the organization can change. The org chart should be the result of that change, not its opening move.

That distinction matters because faster work can leave the old company intact. A team may produce reports in minutes and still wait through the same approvals, status meetings, and handoffs. In that case, AI has accelerated tasks without removing the structure built to coordinate them. I think many “AI-first” reorganizations will fail here: they will cut a layer before proving that the work performed by that layer has disappeared.

[Arvind KC joined OpenAI as Chief People Officer in February 2026](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/). OpenAI is an AI research and product company building systems that can reason, write, and use software tools, which makes its people leader's view of changing roles especially relevant. A few months after joining, Arvind published [ten reflections on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7485407006204493825/), including two claims that belong together: *“Every role is a technical role,”* and OpenAI is *“designed for bottom-up innovation.”*

Benjamin Simkin approaches the same question from outside the frontier labs. His independently published May 2026 book [*The AI First Company*](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5) is a field manual for owners rebuilding companies around AI. Its central warning is what he calls the Retrofit Trap: adding AI to the edges of an inherited process while preserving its queues, handoffs, and authority. Neither source proves that every company should become flat. Together they point to a more useful claim: when the work changes, authority has to move with it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI can change organizational structure only when it removes the execution and routine coordination that made the old structure necessary. Faster tasks are insufficient; leaders need evidence that work, authority, and accountability have moved together.</p></div>

## Technical work spreads beyond technical teams

Arvind's phrase *“every role is a technical role”* does not mean everyone becomes a software engineer. It means that anyone making a business decision must understand what the systems involved can do, where they fail, and how those limits affect the choice at hand. Technical reasoning becomes part of commercial, financial, operational, and creative judgment even when coding remains specialist work.

Consider a commercial lead reviewing an AI-generated proposal. The important skill is not writing the model that drafted it. The lead needs to know which claims came from approved evidence, what customer context the system could access, and which commitments require a person to decide. Without that understanding, the apparent speed of the tool merely brings uncertainty closer to the customer.

This is why the Retrofit Trap is organizational as well as technical. If a business team cannot judge the system, it sends every uncertain case back to a central technical group. The queue survives under a new name, and the supposed decentralization creates another service desk. I think shared technical reasoning matters because local authority is credible only when the person holding it can recognize the system's boundaries.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A technical role is a role whose owner can reason about the systems shaping their decisions. Coding can remain specialized, but business authority cannot move closer to the work unless people there can judge evidence, limits, and failure.</p></div>

## Separate relay work from human coordination

A hierarchy does more than pass information around. Managers coach people, resolve conflict, allocate scarce resources, maintain accountability, and make trade-offs between competing goals. Those responsibilities do not vanish because an agent can summarize a meeting. The part that may disappear is routine relay work: gathering status, assigning known tasks, translating updates, chasing standard approvals, and sending predictable exceptions to the right place.

The difference gives leaders a cleaner way to inspect a role. Ask which activities exist because information has to travel, and which exist because people genuinely disagree about priorities or consequences. An agent may take the first category when the route is clear and observable. The second category still needs someone with authority, because software cannot accept responsibility for the trade-off.

A regional pricing exception makes the boundary visible. An agent could gather the account history, current margin rules, delivery capacity, and contract terms, then prepare a recommendation. If the request crosses an agreed discount limit or creates tension between a revenue target and a legal obligation, the case needs a named human owner. When finance, sales, legal, and operations review every ordinary request anyway, the agent has removed very little.

This is where Arvind's description of *bottom-up innovation* becomes practical. It means people close to the work can improve the process and decide within stated limits, rather than sending every choice upward. Those limits are decision rights: explicit rules about who may decide what, what evidence they need, and when the issue must move elsewhere. I think a flatter company becomes possible at that boundary, where routine relay fades but consequential conflict remains clearly owned.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents can absorb predictable relay work, while coaching, accountability, conflict, and cross-business trade-offs remain human. A thinner hierarchy is justified only where routine handoffs fall and consequential decisions still have named owners.</p></div>

## Design the organization around exceptions

The normal path through a workflow tells you how much can be automated. Its exceptions tell you what the organization must become. Each exception reveals a boundary between domains, a missing decision right, or a consequence that no local system should settle alone. The hidden org chart is therefore easier to see in the queue of unusual cases than in the boxes on a slide.

For the pricing workflow, the design should begin with the cases the agent may never settle: a contract change, an offer below the margin floor, a commitment outside delivery capacity, or a request involving restricted customer data. Each boundary needs an owner, an escalation route, and a stop condition. A *domain owner* is the person accountable for the end-to-end result in one coherent area of work, including the agent's permissions and the unresolved cases it produces.

Simkin's clean-slate question is useful here: *“If I were building this business today, from scratch, knowing what AI can do, would it look like this?”* Applied honestly, the answer may still include finance, legal, sales, and operations as homes for expertise. What changes is their interaction. Shared functions set rules that protect the whole company, while domain owners make ordinary decisions inside those rules without collecting ceremonial approval from every function.

There is no universal ratio that tells a leader when to remove a layer. I don't know one, and the work varies too much for a credible fixed number. The evidence should come from the workflow itself: fewer human touches, shorter queues, stable or lower error costs, and no rise in the time owners spend resolving exceptions. If those measures worsen, coordination has moved rather than disappeared.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exceptions expose the authority an AI-enabled workflow still needs. Design each boundary around a named owner, an escalation route, and a stop condition, then retain the organizational layers required by the measured exception load.</p></div>

## A flatter company needs a different career system

Arvind's eighth reflection says every role should carry the suffix *“agent manager.”* I read that as a capability rather than a title: a person sets the standard, directs agents within limits, evaluates the result, and improves the system from its failures. The value of the role moves from completing each task to extending sound judgment across more work.

That shift creates a career problem. Traditional pyramids often connect advancement to a larger team, more approvals, and a wider reporting span. If routine coordination shrinks, those familiar steps shrink with it. Calling the company flat does not solve the loss of status, pay progression, or opportunity for people whose careers were built around becoming managers.

A pricing coordinator, for example, might grow into ownership of the commercial decision system. That is a credible destination only if the person receives authority to change standards, stop the agent, resolve ordinary exceptions, and influence the policies that create recurring failures. A new label without decision rights or pay is simply extra work disguised as transformation.

I think this human architecture deserves the same care as the workflow. Some managers will spend less time relaying information and more time coaching, designing systems, or owning domains. Some roles will no longer contain enough necessary work to survive. My take is that leaders should say this plainly, fund the transition where a real destination exists, and build progression around depth of expertise, scope of judgment, and the quality of systems a person can responsibly own.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>When routine coordination stops being the main route to advancement, career progression must reward deeper expertise, wider judgment, and responsible system ownership. New roles become real only when authority, development, and pay move with the title.</p></div>

## Let the workflow earn the structure

The practical starting point is one workflow, not a company-wide declaration. Map its repeated execution, routine relay work, human judgment, and cross-domain conflicts. Record how long the work takes, how many people touch it, how exceptions age, how much review it creates, and what a serious error costs before an agent enters the process.

Then let the agent operate in shadow mode, a trial in which it proposes actions while people still execute them. This gives the team evidence about where the system is reliable and where its confidence is misleading. It also exposes hidden work, because every override, unclear owner, and unresolved exception becomes visible before reporting lines change.

Only after the redesigned workflow performs better should leaders remove a handoff or combine responsibilities. I think boards should treat the org chart as a claim supported by operating evidence, not as a promise attached to an AI budget. If a layer still performs necessary coaching, conflict resolution, or exception ownership, keep it and improve the work around it.

The deeper opportunity is still substantial. Agents can compress execution and routine relay, giving skilled people more room for design, judgment, and relationships. But authority does not evaporate when software takes over the route between decisions. AI-first architecture begins when the company makes that authority visible, puts it in capable hands, and rebuilds human progress around the work that remains.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Test one workflow before changing the company around it. When measured execution, handoffs, review load, exceptions, and error costs improve together, the structure can follow; AI-first architecture begins only when work, authority, and human progression change together.</p></div>
