I tried to draw the next buying journey for a Nordic heat-pump company. The customer asks an AI agent whether the company serves their address and what installation costs. It checks the answer and books an approved slot. The homepage never opens.

This is the transition I think many companies are missing. The website can remain useful for people while losing its role as the main hub for company knowledge.

<div class="article-tldr">
<span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span>
<p>Search is answering before the click, and agents are beginning to act. A verified knowledge catalog becomes the durable asset behind the interface. Structured content makes it usable.</p>
</div>

## Search is becoming a conversation

The click is losing its role as the default handoff.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Google increasingly answers questions inside Search. Agents extend that change by using company information to complete a task for the customer.</p></div>

A 2024 SparkToro and Datos study found that 58.5% of US Google searches ended without a click. The EU figure was 59.7%. Pew Research later found that people clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits with an AI summary, compared with 15% when no summary appeared. Sources inside the summary received clicks in 1% of visits.

Risher attributes the line "2027 is the year of the agents" to Sundar Pichai. The exact wording remains unverified. In April 2026, Pichai described Search becoming an "agent manager."

An AI agent pursues a goal through tools and checks each result. Google launched its Agent2Agent protocol, A2A, at Cloud Next in April 2025 with more than 50 partners. A2A lets agents exchange information and coordinate work. ServiceTitan already ships AI booking assistants for field service.

For our heat-pump company, this means the customer's agent can compare service coverage and request a slot. I think rankings still matter, but mention rate and AI share of voice now tell you whether the company enters the conversation at all.

## A knowledge catalog is an official answer set

Risher's phrase is useful when we define it carefully.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog is the verified company information an agent is allowed to use. Each answer has a defined shape, a named owner, and a review date.</p></div>

Risher describes a knowledge catalog as a verified dataset about a business that can be deployed through an agent. I found no Google product with that name, so I use Risher's term.

For the heat-pump company, one entry might look like this:

```yaml
service: heat_pump_installation
region: SE-C
price_from_sek: 89000
standard_installation_included: true
valid_until: 2026-12-31
answer_owner: pricing@company.example
```

The same entry can feed the website and a controlled agent interface. Risher calls content "training data for the future internet." In engineering terms, much of this will be retrieval at runtime: the agent fetches a current answer instead of learning it during model training.

I don't know whether the label will last. The need for verified answers already exists.

## Structured content is the control surface

Structured content gives code clear fields instead of paragraphs to interpret.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Named fields let an agent validate an answer before acting on it. Expiry rules and version history also give the team a safe way to reject stale data or restore an earlier catalog release.</p></div>

For a junior AI engineer, structured content means agreed keys and types. `price_from_sek` is a number, while `valid_until` is a date. The agent does less guessing.

The heat-pump agent might use ReAct, a reasoning-and-action loop: choose a step, call a tool, inspect the result, then repeat. LangGraph builds stateful agent workflows as graphs. Its `StateGraph` defines the steps and transitions. A Python `TypedDict` describes the keys carried in state, such as the address and approved catalog entry. It helps the type checker, while runtime validation still needs a schema check.

When `valid_until` has passed, the catalog step returns "price unavailable" and routes the request to a person. A test set of common customer questions should block a release when expected answers change. I think this verification loop is where structured content becomes production infrastructure.

## Headless CMS gives you a head start

A headless CMS stores content separately from the page that displays it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Teams with clean content models already have much of the catalog's raw material. Their CMS can serve the website today and a controlled agent interface tomorrow.</p></div>

Modern headless CMS platforms store reusable entries behind APIs. If the heat-pump company's price and service coverage are real fields, the team can add agent access without extracting facts from page copy first.

In my experience, the hard part is deciding what a field means. Does `price_from_sek` cover labor? Who changes the region when a crew reaches capacity? Technology cannot settle those questions.

I think many companies bought headless CMS for frontend freedom and accidentally prepared for the agent web. I made the earlier case in [The quiet truth about headless CMS](/posts/the-quiet-truth-about-headless-cms/).

## The Digital Singularity is arriving below the screen

Generative UI, or GenUI, builds an interface when the request arrives.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A generated interface may live for one task. The verified knowledge below it can serve every interface that follows.</p></div>

In [The Digital Singularity Shift](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/), I argued that interfaces would be assembled around each person's intent. Gemini 3's generative interfaces and Google's A2UI standard now point in that direction. The knowledge layer outlives each generated screen.

<div class="scifi">
<span class="scifi__label">Meanwhile, in science fiction</span>
<p class="scifi__film">Star Trek (2009)</p>
<p>The Enterprise crew asks the computer for a route and gets an answer built for that moment. The screen can change because the ship's trusted systems hold the knowledge. GenUI follows the same shape: temporary interface, persistent source.</p>
</div>

I don't know whether the website loses hub status in three years or seven. I think the direction is clear enough to move the asset below the screen now.

## Answer ownership turns data into authority

An agent answer can become a promise that a customer acts on.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every catalog field needs an accountable owner and a revocation path. Narrow approval scopes make that responsibility possible to accept.</p></div>

Call this answer ownership, or <em>svarsägarskap</em> in Swedish. The board-level test is blunt: Who signs what our agent says?

Pricing owns the amount, while operations owns service coverage. Warranty wording follows the existing legal approval path. People may resist because signing a field sounds like unlimited liability. Give each approval a defined use and an expiry date, and the responsibility becomes bounded.

For teams connecting EU AI Act controls with GDPR work, the catalog can hold approval evidence and show who can revoke an answer. Delegated authority needs an off switch.

## Choose a partner that can remain independent

The team that built your content platform may already know where the useful knowledge lives.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Start with a consultancy that understands your content model and can work across platforms. Keep the verified dataset portable and agent access reversible.</p></div>

My take: ask the consultancy that implemented your CMS to map the first catalog slice. Valtech is one example. Independent Contentful, Sanity, and Optimizely partners can also do this work.

Platform expertise helps. Financial dependence on another product license can pull the architecture toward a module before the knowledge model is clear. Ask how the partner gets paid.

Put two questions into procurement: Where does the verified dataset live? Who can export it and revoke agent access? Those answers reveal whether you own the catalog in practice.

## Build the first catalog slice now

Begin with the twenty questions customers ask before they buy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Choose one valuable domain and assign an owner to every answer. Expand only after its read-only interface passes a monthly test.</p></div>

For the heat-pump company, the first slice is service coverage and pricing. The CMS supplies the fields through a read-only tool. Answer owners approve the test set before release.

Run the twenty-question test monthly with the major assistants. Record the companies they mention and the facts they use. Log failures separately. A spreadsheet is enough to begin, while AI visibility tools can automate the work later.

Add mention rate and AI share of voice beside rankings in the leadership report. That gives the pilot a business measure: whether an agent can find a usable company answer and whether the company can prove where it came from.

Your website can keep serving people. Your knowledge catalog will carry the answer when no screen appears.
