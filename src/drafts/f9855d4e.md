Your website will remain useful, but its privileged role as the center of your digital business is weakening. Customers can get an answer without visiting it, receive an interface created for a specific request and, in some situations, let software take the next step for them.

I think traffic loss is the visible consequence. The strategic problem is the **answer-control gap**: the distance between an answer your company has approved and the answer a customer receives from an interface, search service or AI assistant.

That gap has two parts. You can control answers on interfaces you operate; you can only monitor and influence what outside AI services say. The executive question is whether your current systems can keep approved knowledge accurate, traceable and within scope across the first group while making reliable source material available to the second. If they can, avoid another platform. If they cannot, start building a knowledge-catalog capability.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The website is becoming one interface among many. Leaders need to separate the answers their company controls from the external answers it can only influence.</p></div>

## The answer is leaving the page

[SparkToro](https://sparktoro.com/blog/in-2026-less-than-one-third-of-google-searches-still-send-a-click/), an audience-research company, estimated that 68.01% of Google searches from January through April 2026 ended without a click. Its analysis used a US desktop-and-mobile browser panel supplied by Similarweb, a company that measures digital behavior. A **zero-click search** ends without the user opening a result, whether the user found an answer, abandoned the session or changed the query.

The panel excluded Google's mobile app, and its coverage differs from SparkToro's [2024 European Union estimate](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/) of 59.7%, so the two figures are not a clean trend line. Only 0.34% of searches in the 2026 panel reached AI Mode, Google's conversational search interface. Zero-click behavior is widespread, but this panel does not prove widespread agent adoption.

AI answers still change behavior where they appear. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), a nonpartisan US research organization, examined March 2025 browsing activity from 900 American adults. A traditional result received a click in 8% of Google visits with an AI summary and 15% of visits without one. A source inside the summary received a click in only 1% of visits where a summary appeared.

The interface is changing as well. A **generative user interface**, or GenUI, creates a screen for a particular request instead of presenting the same fixed page to everyone. Google demonstrated this with its Gemini 3 AI model in [November 2025](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/). When the screen can be assembled at the moment of need, the finished webpage becomes one possible presentation of the underlying knowledge.

An **AI agent** is software that carries out several steps toward a goal. On [April 9, 2025](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/), Google announced the open **Agent2Agent protocol**, or A2A, with more than 50 technology partners. It gives separately built agents a common way to exchange information and coordinate actions, while each participating organization retains its own access rules. The launch proves that a coordination standard exists; it does not prove that agent-to-agent commerce is already normal.

I don't know how quickly people will trust agents with expensive, sensitive or regulated decisions. I think the useful conclusion is narrower: discovery is moving from page to answer, while software is beginning to move from answer to action. Both shifts reduce the website's claim to be the single hub.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Search often ends without a visit, generated interfaces can replace fixed pages, and agents can now coordinate through an open protocol. The direction is visible even though broad agent adoption remains unproven.</p></div>

## What a knowledge catalog adds

Phil Risher, owner of [Phlash Consulting](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo), a marketing firm for local-service businesses, described a **knowledge catalog** in June 2026 as “a verified data set of information about your business.” Google Cloud, Google's enterprise cloud business, introduced [a narrower data product with the same name](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/data-analytics/introducing-the-google-cloud-knowledge-catalog) in April 2026. No verified Google source defines Risher's broader model this way, so treat the name as a useful synthesis rather than an established product category.

A company knowledge catalog is a governed set of approved claims: facts, pricing rules, policies, credentials and answers, each connected to its source, owner, market, effective date and permitted use. It is an operating model before it is a product. The point is to preserve not only what the company says, but where that statement is valid and who can change it.

Most large organizations already own parts of this capability. A content management system, or CMS, manages editorial material; a product information management system keeps product attributes; a data catalog helps people find datasets; a knowledge graph maps relationships; and retrieval software selects material for an AI answer. The knowledge catalog should not replace these systems. It adds a common contract for which claims are approved and which limits must travel with them.

Consider a hypothetical record: “Consumer returns are accepted within 30 days.” Its source is policy version four, its owner is commerce operations, its market is Sweden, its effective date is September 1, 2026, and its permitted use is public consumer support. If an agent asks about Norway or a business contract, this record must not be stretched into an answer. Refusing an out-of-scope request is part of quality.

That distinction also sets the boundary of control. A catalog can directly govern the answers supplied by your website, app, service desk or company agent. Publishing the same claims in a clear, machine-readable form may help an external AI service find better evidence, but it cannot force that service to use the evidence or repeat it correctly.

**Structured content** makes this possible by storing meaning in named, reusable fields rather than burying it inside finished page copy. It is broader than webpage structured data, often called schema markup, which adds machine-readable labels to information on a page. In [May 2025 guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search), Google Search Central, the company's documentation for website owners, said its systems consider structured data and urged publishers to create unique, non-commodity content. Clear fields help machines read the material; distinctive company knowledge makes the material worth reading.

A **headless CMS** stores content separately from website design and sends it to different interfaces through software connections. I think an organization with mature content models, named owners and dependable update processes has a substantial head start because much of the catalog's raw material already exists. That is an architectural judgment, not a measured market result. A headless CMS filled with generic or poorly governed content only distributes ambiguity faster.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog connects each approved claim to its source, owner and limits. It coordinates existing systems rather than replacing them, and structured content gives the model reusable raw material.</p></div>

## The Digital Singularity shifts the scarce asset

In [*The Digital Singularity Shift*](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/), my 2024 book about AI making functional differences easier to copy, I argue that meaning, story and belief become the remaining battleground. The book did not predict a particular Google protocol. Its underlying mechanism, however, is now reaching the interface itself.

GenUI makes presentation easier to reproduce because software can assemble a useful screen for each request. AI-mediated search then decides which knowledge appears inside that screen, while agents may act on the answer. I think the next fault line is therefore authority: which source a system can trust, which scope applies and who is accountable when the answer changes.

A beautiful website still matters when a person chooses to visit. Its strategic value changes when presentation becomes temporary and the same approved claim must survive in search, an assistant, a sales tool and an interface that did not exist when the claim was written.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>As AI makes interfaces easier to generate, verified meaning and accountable authority become scarcer than presentation. That is the knowledge-catalog opportunity.</p></div>

The mapping is the separation between interface and authority. A spoken request may replace a menu, but the ship's computer still needs current records, clear access rules and limits on what it may do. A knowledge catalog plays that role for company-approved answers; it does not give the company command of computers outside its control.

## Prove the case before buying the platform

The evidence behind this article contains no measured Swedish or Norwegian enterprise case with a published baseline, cost and outcome. The US search data does not fill that gap. My take is to treat the knowledge catalog as a testable Nordic business hypothesis and make the first pilot produce the missing evidence.

Use six weeks as a planning box, not as a promise of transformation. That is enough time to record a baseline, change one controlled source and repeat the test, yet short enough to stop before procurement turns it into a platform program. Choose one policy family, one market and one interface you own. Before changing anything, run 100 predefined questions through the current setup, including paraphrases, outdated wording, wrong-market requests and requests that exceed the user's authority.

For the owned interface, I would set three starting limits: every answer must point to an approved record, no wrong-market or unauthorized answer is accepted, and an approved change must appear within one business day. Allow no more than five other exceptions per 100 questions. These are pilot limits, not a universal standard; a regulated process may require zero exceptions. Scale only if the current setup misses the limits and the pilot then meets them.

Test external AI services separately because you do not control them. Capture a baseline with 50 predefined customer questions across two named services, make the approved source public and machine-readable, then repeat the test weekly for 12 weeks. Record answer accuracy, citations and **mention rate**, the percentage of those questions whose answers name your company. Add **AI share of voice**, your share of mentions across a fixed group of competitors, alongside rankings rather than replacing them. For a first test, predeclare success as at least a ten-percentage-point increase in answers that both name the company and state the tested claim correctly, sustained across three weekly checks. If that does not happen, reject the external-influence claim; the case for consistency on owned interfaces must stand on its own.

I think a small test needs a hard economic boundary. For this first scope, I would cap internal work at 60 hours in total and external fees at €25,000, with no new software license. That is a decision envelope, not a consultancy-market benchmark. Stop if the current systems already pass, if the pilot breaches the cap or if a critical scope error remains. Rollback means disconnecting the pilot delivery path and returning the interface to its previous source while keeping the test record.

Name a permanent business owner, delivery owner and risk owner before the pilot starts. If the work scales, the business owner chairs a monthly answer-control review; claims are checked whenever a source policy changes and at least quarterly; the risk owner can block unsafe use; and the business owner can retire expired claims. Funding that service is part of the scale decision, not an afterthought.

Start with the independent consultancy that already understands your content platform. [Valtech](https://www.valtech.com/), a global business-transformation agency, and agency or solution partners working with [Contentful](https://www.contentful.com/partners/), [Sanity](https://www.sanity.io/agency-partners) or [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/partners/), three headless-CMS vendors, are examples rather than endorsements. A platform vendor may bring useful product expertise, but a supplier paid to resell licenses has a different incentive. Require disclosure of resale income, full export rights and a written reason for every proposed new platform.

Publish the baseline, final result, internal hours and external cost. That is how a Nordic company turns a borrowed forecast into local evidence. I think leaders should begin now, but the first goal is not to own another system. It is to know whether the company can keep an approved answer intact when the interface changes.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Run separate, bounded tests for owned-answer control and external visibility, with explicit limits, cost, ownership and rollback. Build only when existing systems fail and the pilot proves the capability can close the gap.</p></div>
