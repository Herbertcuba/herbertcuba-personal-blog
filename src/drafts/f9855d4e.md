Your website is on its way to becoming irrelevant.

I do not mean that websites will disappear. Companies will still need places where they control the brand, explain complex offers and build direct customer relationships. The part becoming irrelevant is the website's old job as the compulsory center of discovery, comparison and action.

The familiar journey began with a search, continued through a click and ended on a page the company had designed. AI is weakening that sequence at both ends. Search engines can answer before the click, while AI agents can coordinate tasks beyond the page. I think this changes the investment question: the durable asset is no longer just the interface people see, but the company knowledge that many different systems can use.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The website will survive as an important channel, but it is losing its position as the required route into a business. Investment should follow the company knowledge that can remain useful when discovery and action happen elsewhere.</p></div>

## Search is no longer a reliable doorway

The loss of the click began before today's AI boom. A [2024 study by marketing research firm SparkToro, based on browsing data from analytics provider Datos](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/), estimated that 58.5% of US Google searches and 59.7% of EU searches ended without a click. That figure includes people who stopped searching and people who changed their query, so it does not prove that AI caused the behavior. It does show that a website visit was already far from guaranteed.

AI-generated summaries make the change easier to see. In March 2025, [Pew Research Center, an independent US research organization, examined the Google browsing activity of 900 American adults](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/). People clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits when Google displayed an AI summary, compared with 15% when it did not. A source named inside the summary received a click in only 1% of visits.

The point is not that keywords have vanished. It is that customers can ask a full question and receive a composed answer before meeting any of the companies behind it. Pew found that about 60% of searches beginning with words such as who, what, when and why produced an AI summary. I think business leaders should treat that as a market signal: a company can shape a customer's decision without ever hosting the moment when that decision takes form.

Now the technology is moving from answers toward delegated action. An AI agent is software that receives a goal and can carry out several steps for a person or organization within the access it has been given. At Google Cloud Next '25, Google's annual enterprise technology conference, the company announced [Agent2Agent, or A2A](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/). This open protocol gives agents a common way to exchange information and coordinate actions across different business applications.

A2A does not establish whether a company fact is current, nor does communication between agents grant them unlimited authority. Identity, access, records and reversibility remain separate business decisions. I don't know when customers will routinely delegate a major purchase to an agent because payment, liability, trust and habit still create real friction. Yet even partial adoption matters: once an agent can compare options or arrange the next step, the website is no longer the only place where a commercial journey can move forward.

This also changes measurement. Search ranking remains useful, but leaders increasingly need to know whether AI answers mention the company and how often it appears relative to competitors. [Semrush, a search analytics company, analyzed 200,000 keywords in September 2024](https://www.semrush.com/blog/ai-overviews-study/) and found that a top-ten traditional ranking did not ensure inclusion in a Google AI Overview. Mention rate and AI share of voice are still developing measures, but they address a question rankings alone cannot answer: is the business present in the answer the customer actually receives?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Zero-click behavior made the website optional, AI summaries can remove the need to visit it, and agents can carry a journey into action. The business therefore needs visibility and reliable information inside AI-mediated decisions, not only strong positions in a list of links.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog gives agents something reliable to use

In a June 2026 video, [Phil Risher, a marketing consultant at Phlash Consulting](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo), described a knowledge catalog as a verified set of information about a business that can be deployed through an agent. The phrase is useful, although it is not an official Google product or technical standard. I use it to describe a strategic asset the company itself must own.

A knowledge catalog is a collection of business information arranged so software can use it without guessing what each item means. It could include services, product limits, pricing rules, policies, locations, credentials and the questions customers repeatedly ask. The information is machine-readable, which simply means that its meaning is expressed through consistent labels and fields rather than left inside the visual layout of a finished page.

Consider a warranty statement. On a normal web page, the duration, eligible products and regional exceptions may be mixed into several paragraphs. In a knowledge catalog, those elements can be stored separately, connected to the approved policy and given a review date. A website can still present them as polished prose, while an agent can use the same information to answer a narrow customer question without inventing the missing conditions.

Verification is what turns this from tidier content into a business control. For every consequential statement, I would want a named source, an accountable owner, a period of validity and rules for where it may be used. If the supporting policy expires, the statement can be withheld until someone reviews it. A conversational interface may present an answer or initiate an action; the catalog is the governed information layer behind what the organization is prepared to let that interface say or use.

Risher calls content “training data for the future internet.” That is a useful strategic image, but the immediate mechanism will often be retrieval: an AI system looks up relevant information while preparing an answer rather than retraining its underlying model. The company does not need to teach every AI system everything it knows. It needs to make the right knowledge easy to find, hard to misunderstand and possible to withdraw.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog is the company-owned information layer beneath websites and agents. Its value comes from combining reusable business facts with clear meaning, evidence, ownership and limits on use.</p></div>

## Structured content creates a head start

Structured content stores an idea in named parts instead of treating an entire page as one block of text. A service record might separate the service name, eligible area, price rule and last review date. Because each part retains its meaning, the same record can support a website, a customer-service tool or an agent without being copied and rewritten for every channel.

This is also the direction in Google's own guidance. In May 2025, [Google Search Central, the company's official guidance service for website owners](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search/), said that structured data helps share content information in a machine-readable form its systems can consider. The guidance also emphasized original, useful information. Structure makes knowledge easier to consume, but it cannot make a generic claim distinctive or an outdated claim true.

Organizations with a well-run headless content management system have a practical head start. A headless CMS separates reusable content from the design of one website and supplies that content to different channels through software connections. If the organization already models product facts, assigns approval rights and publishes the same record in several places, it has some of the raw material for a knowledge catalog. The platform name alone proves nothing; a headless CMS filled with unstructured page copy is still a collection of pages.

The platform market is already responding. [Sanity, a structured-content platform, introduced Agent Actions in May 2025](https://www.sanity.io/blog/agent-actions-ai-building-blocks-for-structured-content), giving AI systems ways to work with content models and workflows. [Contentful, another enterprise content platform, made its hosted connection for AI assistants and agents generally available in July 2026](https://www.contentful.com/blog/remote-mcp-now-generally-available). These are vendor announcements rather than neutral proof of adoption, but they show what the content-platform industry is preparing for: software consumers as well as human readers.

This makes the consultancy that built the current content platform a sensible place to look for relevant knowledge. A global digital consultancy such as [Valtech](https://www.valtech.com/), or experienced partners working with Contentful, Sanity or Optimizely, may already understand the company's content model and approval process. I think the important filter is commercial independence, not a familiar logo. Leaders should know who owns the resulting knowledge model, whether the information can be exported and whether the advice remains sound if the platform changes. Deep product expertise is valuable; an incentive to make one license the permanent center of the solution is not the same thing.

The near-term investment case is therefore more durable than another broad website redesign. A redesigned page improves one interface. Verified structured knowledge can improve that page while also serving search systems, employee tools, customer-service channels and agents. It gives the business options before anyone can say which agent or interface will win.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structured content lets one approved business fact travel without losing its meaning. A mature headless CMS and an independent consultancy can provide a head start, provided the company retains ownership and freedom to change platforms.</p></div>

## Generative UI makes the investment logic clear

*[The Digital Singularity Shift](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/)*, my 2024 book about business as AI makes digital creation abundant, argued that functional features become easier to reproduce and that value moves toward the meaning beneath them. Generative user interfaces now give that argument a visible technical form. A generative UI is a screen assembled for a particular request instead of a fixed page designed in advance for every possible visitor.

In November 2025, [Google introduced experiments in Gemini 3 that generated an interface when the user submitted a prompt](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/). In April 2026, Google released [A2UI v0.9](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-v0-9-generative-ui/), a proposed set of rules through which agents can request suitable interface elements while an application keeps control of its approved design components. The screen can adapt to the task without giving the agent unrestricted control over how the company presents itself.

The closest science-fiction comparison is the holodeck in *Star Trek: First Contact*, the 1996 film in which a computer creates a setting around the immediate mission. Generative UI points toward a modest digital version of that idea: a customer may receive a comparison, form or dashboard assembled for one moment. The scenery changes, so the stable value must sit beneath it in the facts, rules and permissions that shape what appears.

Design, accessibility and brand will still matter. They may matter even more when a company must express itself across interfaces it did not fully design. But I think the most important investment now is the knowledge catalog, because generated screens increase the number of places where company information can appear and the speed at which a weak claim can travel.

The website becomes one expression of the business rather than its digital center. Generative UI will make expressions cheaper and more temporary. The company that owns trusted, structured knowledge can remain useful across all of them, even when it no longer owns the screen.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Generative UI turns the interface into a temporary response to a customer's task, which is the shift anticipated in <em>The Digital Singularity Shift</em>. The investment that matters now is the verified knowledge catalog beneath those changing screens.</p></div>
