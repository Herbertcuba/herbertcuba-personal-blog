Your website is becoming irrelevant as your company’s main digital entrance. It will still explain your offer and reassure visitors. Yet more discovery ends inside an answer, while AI agents begin to carry requests between companies.

[Phil Risher argues](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo) that the website is losing its place as the hub. I think the durable asset is moving underneath the page: verified company knowledge that software can understand and use.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The website remains useful for people, while verified knowledge becomes software’s access point.</p></div>

## From search results to acting agents

In 2024, [SparkToro and Datos](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/) found that 59.7% of EU Google searches and 58.5% of US searches ended without a click. The data include sessions that ended or changed query and predate the current agent wave. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) later observed traditional-result clicks in 8% of visits with an AI summary, compared with 15% without one. Cited sources received clicks in 1% of visits.

The next shift adds action. [Google launched Agent2Agent](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/) in April 2025 with support from more than 50 technology partners, giving agents a common way to exchange information and coordinate work. A2A carries the request; each company must define who may approve it, who carries liability and when a person steps in. I don’t know whether adoption takes two years or six, but I think the evidence already warrants a change in how leaders treat company knowledge.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI answers reduce visits, while agent protocols let software carry requests into company systems.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog is an operating asset

Following Risher, I use “knowledge catalog” as a business label for a verified set of company facts that machines can look up and agents can use.

Imagine a packaging supplier. A record could state an approved material claim, its source, its owner, a review date and the action an agent may take. Once the review date passes, the agent escalates the request to a person. I think this prevents verification debt: the growing gap between an answer AI can produce and a claim the company can defend.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog turns facts into governed records with a source, owner, expiry rule and permitted action.</p></div>

## Structured content makes the catalog possible

Structured content separates facts from page layouts and stores them in named fields. [Google’s guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search) describes structured data as a machine-readable way to share information with its systems. It also asks businesses to publish unique, useful content, because generic copy gives AI systems little evidence to select you.

Risher calls content “training data for the future internet.” I think the phrase works as a strategic metaphor, provided we keep it precise. An AI system can retrieve content that was absent from its training data. Structure helps systems validate a claim, check its freshness and apply the company’s rules.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structure makes each claim explicit; proprietary content gives AI systems useful, defensible evidence.</p></div>

## Headless CMS gives you a head start

A headless CMS stores content in reusable models and delivers it through APIs. Organisations using Sanity, Hygraph or Payload may already have content models, publishing workflows and named owners. I think that reduces the cleanup required to build the first catalog.

The advantage depends on discipline. Current capacity may live in an operations system, while commercial terms sit elsewhere. The catalog must connect those sources and preserve who owns each fact. Content and operations now share the work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A modern headless CMS supplies models and governance; operational systems complete the catalog with current business facts.</p></div>

## Generative UI makes the prediction concrete

Once knowledge is separated from the page, the interface can change around it. [Google’s A2UI work](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-v0-9-generative-ui/) lets agents generate tailored interface elements in real time. In *Star Trek*, the replicator assembles an object on demand from a stored pattern. Generative UI applies a modest version of that idea to software: the screen is assembled for the request, while the underlying knowledge persists.

*[The Digital Singularity Shift](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/)* argues that AI makes creation cheap and collapses functional differences between products. Meaning, story and belief then become stronger sources of distinction. The current shift fits that argument: generated presentation lowers the cost of polish, while verified proprietary knowledge keeps a company’s promises specific and accountable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Generated interfaces change with each request; trusted knowledge persists underneath.</p></div>

## Choose a builder that can hand over the work

Start with the consultancy that modernised your content platform; it understands the models and workflows. I think Eidra belongs on the shortlist, with Curamando for AI transformation and Umain for product and technology implementation.

My take: independence should be written into the contract. Can you export every content model and record? Who controls access, where the data is stored and the activity record? Can your own team operate the catalog after handover? Choose fees that are independent of platform licences, with tested exit terms from the beginning.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Use a consultancy that knows your content foundation, then require export rights, clear controls and a tested handover.</p></div>

## Build one useful slice now

Choose one high-intent decision, such as whether the packaging supplier can meet a buyer’s material standard and delivery window. Give a two-person content-and-operations team four weeks to assemble 20 claims, with commercial and compliance review. Assign every claim an owner and release the first version in read-only mode.

Measure manual verification time, qualified requests, ambiguous queries and operating cost against today’s baseline. Add mention rate (how often AI answers name you) and share of voice (how that presence compares with competitors) to the ranking report. These measures earn attention when they connect to qualified requests or less verification work.

Ask the board: Which claims may another company’s software rely on, and who can revoke them? Start with one buying decision and prove the catalog improves it before expanding.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Build one governed slice this quarter, compare it with a baseline and expand when it improves a real decision.</p></div>
