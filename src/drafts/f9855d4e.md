Your website is becoming irrelevant to a growing share of customer decisions. It will still explain products, support customers and close business, but the answer that shapes a decision may now appear in Google, an AI assistant or an agent before the customer sees a page.

The next digital failure is therefore bigger than lost traffic. It is **answer drift**: the same company giving different answers because approved knowledge changes faster than its interfaces. I think leaders should measure the resulting **answer-control gap**, the distance between the fact the company approved and the answer a customer receives.

Test it over six weeks. Measure how long one important fact takes to reach every interface you own and how often fixed questions produce a stale, conflicting or unauthorized answer. If current systems already stay within an agreed limit, build no new layer. If they cannot, a knowledge catalog is missing business infrastructure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The website is becoming one interface among many. The strategic issue is whether approved company knowledge remains accurate and within scope when the interface changes.</p></div>

## The next failure is answer drift

The movement away from website visits is measurable, although the numbers need care. [SparkToro, an audience-research company, and Similarweb, a digital-behavior measurement company](https://sparktoro.com/blog/in-2026-less-than-one-third-of-google-searches-still-send-a-click/), reported in June 2026 that 68.01% of Google searches in Similarweb's US desktop-and-mobile web panel ended without a click during January through April. A **zero-click search** ends before the user opens a result.

The study excludes Google's mobile app and combines desktop and mobile behavior through a weighted estimate. Its authors warn that this panel is not directly comparable with the 2024 panel from Datos, a browsing-data provider, which estimated that 59.7% of European Union Google searches ended without a click. The older figure gives European orientation, not a clean trend line.

Only 0.34% of searches in the early-2026 panel reached AI Mode, Google's conversational search interface, so zero-click behavior is not proof that agents have taken over. Yet AI answers deepen the pattern. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), a nonpartisan American research organization, observed 900 US adults in March 2025. People clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits with an AI summary and 15% without one; cited sources received a click in only 1% of visits with a summary. Pew also found summaries on 60% of queries beginning with “who,” “what,” “when” or “why,” showing how question-shaped search can become an answer before it becomes a visit.

The interface is changing too. A **generative user interface**, or GenUI, assembles a screen for the request instead of choosing a finished page. Google demonstrated this with its Gemini 3 AI model in [November 2025](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/) and released version 0.9 of its Agent-to-User Interface project, A2UI, in [April 2026](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-v0-9-generative-ui/). A2UI lets an AI system describe an interface while the receiving application renders approved components.

An **AI agent** is software that works through several steps toward a goal, such as comparing options and requesting a booking. At Google Cloud Next, its enterprise technology conference, on [April 9, 2025](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/), Google announced the Agent2Agent protocol, or **A2A**, with more than 50 technology partners. A protocol is an agreed communication method; A2A lets separately built agents exchange information and coordinate work. It does not give an outside agent permission to use your data or commit your company.

I don't know how quickly customers will trust agents with expensive or regulated decisions. I think the direction is clear enough to act on because generated interfaces and coordinated agents remove the webpage from more of the journey. The next thing to break is the connection between an approved fact and the answer built from it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Zero-click behavior is already widespread, while direct use of Google's AI Mode was still small in early 2026. Agent adoption remains uncertain, but search answers, generated interfaces and A2A are separating customer journeys from fixed pages.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog is an answer contract

[Phil Risher of Phlash Consulting](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo), a digital-marketing firm serving local-service businesses, described a **knowledge catalog** in June 2026 as “a verified data set of information about your business.” I think that is a useful starting point, but the term is not a Google standard or an established product category. For an enterprise, it should mean the vendor-neutral operating contract that connects an approved claim to its source, owner, scope, effective dates and permitted use.

This does not replace existing systems. A product information management system stores specifications, a content management system holds published explanations, a data catalog inventories datasets and a knowledge graph records relationships. The knowledge catalog adds value only when those parts do not already preserve the context needed to deliver one governed answer across several interfaces.

The boundaries need to be explicit:

| Layer | Responsibility | Accountable owner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source systems | Hold the authoritative product, policy or content record | Business owner |
| Knowledge catalog | Index the approved claim, its source, scope, version and status | Knowledge owner |
| Identity and policy | Decide who may retrieve which fields and request which actions | Security and risk owner |
| Serving interface | Retrieve the approved record and present it on a site, assistant or agent | Technology owner |
| Audit trail | Record the version, access decision and output for review and rollback | Control owner |

The catalog does not authenticate a user, enforce policy or render an answer. Its job is to stop knowledge from losing meaning as it crosses systems.

Consider a manufacturer with evidence that one current product model no longer contains a restricted chemical. The source holds the laboratory report; the catalog carries the model, market, dates, approval and allowed use. The policy layer can let an assistant answer a buyer while refusing to issue a regulatory certificate or extend the claim to an older product. The audit trail records the version used.

A fact can be correct and still be unsafe when software loses its boundaries. **Update lag** measures the time from an approved source change to a consistent answer across owned interfaces. **Exception rate** measures the share of a fixed test set that produces a stale, conflicting, out-of-market or unauthorized answer. These are proposed management measures, not industry standards, but they make the answer-control gap visible enough to govern.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog is the cross-system contract around an approved claim, not a replacement for existing content and data platforms. It closes the answer-control gap only when ownership, permissions, delivery and audit remain separate and explicit.</p></div>

## Structured content turns prediction into infrastructure

**Structured content** stores meaning in named, reusable fields instead of burying it inside finished page copy. In the manufacturer example, the claim, product model, market, evidence and approval status remain separate. The same approved record can then serve a website, customer assistant or internal agent without each channel guessing which sentence contains the rule.

This is broader than **web structured data**, also called schema markup, which adds standard machine-readable labels to information visible on a webpage. In [May 2025 guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search), Google Search Central, the company's documentation for website owners, said its systems consider structured data and urged publishers to create unique, non-commodity content. In plain language, machines need clear labels, but the underlying knowledge must also be useful and difficult for a competitor to reproduce.

Risher calls content “training data for the future internet.” Some AI systems learn from large collections while others retrieve current records, so the phrase is not a literal description of every system. The strategic point survives: company knowledge is becoming source material for interfaces the company did not design.

A **headless content management system**, or headless CMS, stores content separately from website design and makes it available through software interfaces. I think mature content models, named owners and reliable connections provide a substantial head start. That is an architectural judgment, not a measured market advantage; a modern platform filled with duplicate claims offers very little.

This is where the shift meets [*The Digital Singularity Shift*](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/), my 2024 book about AI making functional differences easier to copy. Its thesis is that “meaning, story, and belief” become the remaining battleground. It did not predict A2A by name. The connection is structural: generated interfaces make presentation easier to reproduce, while distinctive knowledge and the trust attached to it remain harder to copy. Structured content lets that value travel without becoming generic.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structured content preserves the meaning, origin and limits of knowledge beyond one page. A mature headless CMS supplies useful raw material, but only distinctive knowledge and clear ownership turn that material into an advantage.</p></div>

## Invest in proof, not another platform

The evidence assembled for this article contains no measured Swedish or Norwegian enterprise case with a published baseline and outcome. American search behavior and vendor releases show direction, not what your organization will achieve. My take is to create local evidence before approving a large program.

Use one six-week charter that a sponsor can forward without explanation:

| Decision | Pilot rule |
| --- | --- |
| Scope | One high-risk answer, one market and one interface the company owns |
| Ownership | A permanent business owner approves the claim and funding; content, technology and risk owners keep their separate decision rights |
| Review | Meet weekly during the pilot, then set a risk-based maintenance cadence and retire a claim immediately when its source or approval expires |
| Test | Record the baseline, then use fixed ordinary and adversarial questions covering stale, conflicting, out-of-market and unauthorized claims |
| Limit | Fix the internal hours, external fee ceiling and scale-or-stop date before work begins |
| Pass gate | Improve update time, reduce correction effort, make no unauthorized commitment in the test set and complete a tested rollback |

The sample will not establish a universal failure rate, and public AI services should not decide the pass gate because they sit outside company control. Monitor them instead. **Mention rate** is the share of fixed questions whose answers name the company; **AI share of voice** is the company's share of mentions across a fixed competitor set. Keep rankings, but pair these observations with corrections, escalations or qualified inquiries.

The consultancy that built your content platform may be the best place to start because it knows the content model and integrations. Choose it for headless-CMS implementation skill and knowledge-governance capability, not a partner badge. Require clear data-location terms, access controls, full export rights, fees separated from platform incentives and a tested handover. A supplier rewarded for adding licenses has a different objective from a company testing whether another platform is necessary.

The falsifier is simple. If existing systems can move an approved fact across owned interfaces within the target time and preserve its limits, formalize that model and stop. Stop again if the pilot cannot improve the baseline within six weeks. But if it exposes an answer-control gap, build the knowledge catalog before generated interfaces turn inconsistent knowledge into inconsistent company behavior.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Run a bounded six-week pilot, keep permanent ownership with the business and select help through an evidence-based, vendor-neutral checklist. Build no new layer when existing systems already close the gap. When they do not, the durable investment is the governed knowledge behind every interface.</p></div>
