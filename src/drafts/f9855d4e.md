A customer can now receive an AI-generated answer about your price, warranty or delivery terms without visiting the page where your company approved the wording. An AI agent, software that can pursue a goal and take permitted steps for a person, may then use that answer to prepare a purchase in another interface.

The website will remain valuable for explanation, persuasion and direct customer service. But I think leaders should stop treating it as the compulsory hub for every digital journey. The more important question is becoming: who owns the commercial truth when another system answers first?

I call the required capability **claim control**. A business claim is any statement that can shape a customer decision, such as a price, product condition or return right. Claim control means knowing where that statement came from, where it applies, who approved it, how software may use it and how company-controlled systems will correct it. A **knowledge catalog** is the operating model I propose for putting that control into practice.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Customers can receive company information from artificial-intelligence software without opening the approved webpage. Leaders therefore need a controlled catalog that keeps each important business statement connected to its source, owner, scope, permitted use and correction route.</p></div>

## Search is becoming an answer and an action

The change begins before artificial intelligence enters the picture. In a July 2024 clickstream study, [SparkToro, an audience-research company, and Datos, a browsing-data provider](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/), estimated that 58.5% of measured Google searches in the United States and 59.7% in the European Union ended without a click. Those figures include people who ended a session or changed their query, so they say nothing about whether artificial intelligence caused the result or whether the searcher left satisfied. What they do establish is that a search no longer guarantees a website visit.

Artificial-intelligence summaries provide more direct evidence. [Pew Research Center, a nonpartisan US research organization, studied the Google browsing activity of 900 American adults in March 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/). People clicked a traditional result during 8% of visits when Google displayed an AI summary, compared with 15% when it did not. They clicked a source cited inside the summary during only 1% of visits with such a summary. Pew also found that about 60% of searches beginning with question words produced an AI summary. I think that pattern is consistent with discovery becoming more conversational, and I would treat it as a strong signal about question-shaped searches rather than proof of a completed historical move away from keywords.

The next step is action. At Google Cloud Next '25, Google's annual conference for business technology, the company launched [Agent2Agent, or A2A](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/), with support from more than 50 technology partners. A2A is an open set of rules that lets independently built AI agents exchange information and coordinate work across business applications. A customer's agent could ask a supplier's agent about availability and prepare an order for human approval, for example. And this is no longer only conference material: by June 30, 2026, Google was already publishing product updates for Gemini Spark, a personal AI agent designed to browse, research and help complete bookings on a user's behalf.

I don't know which protocol or customer habit will dominate, especially for expensive or high-risk decisions. A2A gives systems a route to communicate, but it does not prove that a supplier's information is true or grant an agent commercial authority. The strategic risk is therefore larger than lost traffic: company information can shape a decision before the customer reaches a company-controlled screen.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Observed browsing data shows that a search can end without a website visit, while artificial-intelligence summaries are associated with fewer clicks on traditional results. Open communication rules now also let independently built AI programs coordinate permitted work across business systems, without certifying the truth of the information they exchange.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog keeps the conditions attached

In a June 2026 video, [Phil Risher of Phlash Consulting, a marketing consultancy for service businesses](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo), describes a knowledge catalog as verified company information that can be made available through an agent. I think the phrase is useful, but the Google sources behind this article do not identify an official Google product or standard with that name. Here, it means a company-owned collection of approved knowledge arranged so people and software can use it without guessing what a statement means.

The catalog can contain product details, service areas, pricing rules, policies, credentials and answers to real customer questions. An important statement should carry its approved source, accountable owner, applicable market and product, review date, current status and limits on use. The sentence alone is not enough because those conditions determine whether it is true.

A public Nordic policy difference makes the problem concrete. [Konsumentverket, Sweden's national consumer agency, says on a page reviewed in November 2025](https://www.konsumentverket.se/konsumentratt-process/reklamera-vara/) that consumers in Sweden have three years to complain about a faulty product. [Forbrukerrådet, Norway's consumer council](https://www.forbrukerradet.no/forside/feil-ved-vare/), says the Norwegian period is two or five years depending on how long the product is meant to last. If a Nordic retailer gave the generic answer "you have three years" to a Norwegian customer, it would release a valid Swedish rule into the wrong market. The Norwegian market owner should be able to block that answer until the correct condition and source are attached.

A compact catalog record could make the boundary visible:

| Field | Illustrative value |
| --- | --- |
| Statement | A consumer may complain about a faulty product within three years |
| Market | Sweden only |
| Approved source | Current Swedish consumer policy and internal legal approval |
| Accountable owner | Nordic customer-policy director |
| Review rule | Recheck after a legal or policy change |
| Permitted use | May answer an information request; refunds require separate approval |

The last row matters because permission to read a policy does not include permission to use personal data, promise a refund or change an order. Each action needs its own limited authority and an accountable human decision.

Risher calls content "training data for the future internet." That phrase works for me as a strategic metaphor. In practice, many AI systems look up current company information while composing an answer instead of permanently adding every statement to the underlying model. Either way, the company needs evidence of which source was used and a reliable way to replace it in the systems it controls.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog is a company-owned collection of approved business information, not an official Google standard in the sources used here. It keeps every important statement connected to its evidence, local conditions, accountable owner and action limits so that a correct fact does not become a false promise when reused.</p></div>

## Structured content creates usable raw material

**Structured content** stores information in named, reusable parts instead of leaving it only inside the layout of a finished page. A return policy becomes connected information about the market, eligible product, time limit, approving source and review date. Because those parts retain their meaning, one approved record can serve a website, a customer-service tool or an agent without being rewritten for each interface.

Structured content is related to **structured data**, but the terms describe different layers. In Google Search, structured data is standardized code attached to a webpage so a machine can identify what the page contains. Structured content is the underlying model of the business information. [Google Search Central, Google's official guidance service for website owners, said in May 2025](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search/) that structured data helps share information in a machine-readable form its systems can consider. The same guidance emphasized original, useful content because structure cannot make an expired policy current or generic copy distinctive.

A modern **headless content management system**, usually called a headless CMS, keeps reusable content separate from one website's design and supplies it to different channels through software connections. If your company already models products, policies, markets and approvals this way, it may hold much of the raw material for a knowledge catalog. I think that is a practical advantage: the work begins with governed fields and relationships instead of extracting facts from page-shaped copy. The architectural label alone proves little, however, when ownership is unclear or the information cannot be exported cleanly.

For a large organization, the catalog should connect authoritative systems instead of replacing them. Shared customer and supplier identities may remain in a master-data system, which keeps core business entities consistent across the company. Product specifications may remain in a product-information system, which maintains detailed product attributes. Live stock and prices stay in operational systems. The catalog can present approved views of those sources while regional teams retain authority over local conditions.

A leader with an existing data-governance stack may reasonably object that this sounds like the company **data catalog** under a new name. A data catalog indexes internal data so that analysts and engineers can find and trust it; a knowledge catalog publishes approved business statements outward, with permissions for how people and AI systems may use them. If your organization already runs a data catalog or a product-information system with a documented history of where each value came from, treat the knowledge catalog as the publication and permission layer on top of it. A technical reader may add that the result resembles a product-information system with recorded data lineage, and that is a fair description of the plumbing. What changes is the outward audience and the attached permissions.

This gives leaders a useful investment rule. Extend the current content system when it already supports reusable fields, market scope, approval history and usable exports. Add separate catalog capacity when important statements depend on several authoritative systems, change in real time or need different access rights. As a rough threshold, the case for dedicated capacity strengthens when one answer must stay consistent across more than two authoritative systems, or when the conditions behind it change weekly instead of once a year. Either way, the content model needs identity controls, access policies and answer monitoring; structured content supplies the raw material, and the agent system still has to be designed.

Supplier roadmaps point in the same direction, with one caveat: a roadmap shows what a supplier is building and says little about what buyers have adopted. [Sanity, a structured-content platform, announced tools for AI-assisted structured-content workflows in May 2025](https://www.sanity.io/blog/agent-actions-ai-building-blocks-for-structured-content). [Contentful, another content platform, made its hosted Model Context Protocol connection generally available in July 2026](https://www.contentful.com/blog/remote-mcp-now-generally-available). The Model Context Protocol is a standard route for connecting AI assistants to external information and tools; in business terms, it is one way to give an assistant controlled access to approved company information instead of letting it scrape whatever it finds. Whether any given implementation is useful or safe remains a separate question.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structured content keeps approved business information in reusable parts whose meaning survives beyond one page. A content management system that already separates those parts from website design may provide useful raw material, while a data catalog or product-information system can supply the governed history underneath. Ownership, access rules, live-data connections and answer monitoring still have to be designed.</p></div>

## Generated interfaces change the investment logic

I read this shift through the argument in *[The Digital Singularity Shift: The Rise of Metamodern Narratives in Business](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/)*, my 2024 book about artificial intelligence making digital functions easier to reproduce and increasing the value of the meaning beneath them. A **generative user interface**, often shortened to GenUI, is a screen assembled by artificial intelligence for the request at hand instead of selected from one fixed path designed in advance.

In November 2025, [Gemini 3, an artificial-intelligence model from Google, introduced experiments that generated a custom interface from a user's prompt](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/). Five months later, Google released version 0.9 of [A2UI, an open specification for agent-driven interfaces](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-v0-9-generative-ui/). It lets an agent describe the interface elements needed for a task while the receiving application controls how they look and behave. One customer may receive a comparison table while another receives a booking form, each assembled for the immediate request.

This does not remove the need for design; companies still need recognition, trust and accessibility in the experiences they control. I think leaders should nevertheless assign more strategic weight to the governed meaning beneath the screen, because the same information can now appear through interfaces the company did not design. That forecast follows from the mechanism above, and measured market outcomes do not exist yet.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A generative user interface is a screen assembled by artificial intelligence for a specific request. As one body of company knowledge appears through many possible screens, leaders have reason to invest more in the dependable meaning, ownership and authority underneath the presentation.</p></div>

## Meanwhile in sci-fi

There is an older fictional version of this idea, and it is useful because it shows both the promise and the failure mode.

<div class="scifi"><span class="scifi__label">Meanwhile in sci-fi</span><p class="scifi__film">Star Trek: The Next Generation (1987)</p><p>The holodeck in this television series is a room that assembles a responsive environment around each person who enters it, drawing entirely on the ship's computer and its stored rules. When those rules are accurate and bounded, the illusion serves the crew; when a stored parameter is wrong or a limit is overridden, the same room becomes dangerous. A generative user interface is a modest business version of the holodeck: the screen is assembled per request, and it is only as trustworthy as the governed knowledge and permissions underneath it. That is exactly why the knowledge catalog, not the generated surface, is the asset worth owning.</p></div>

In the series, nobody asks whether the holodeck can render a convincing scene; they ask whether the underlying instructions can be trusted. Leaders should ask the same question about generated interfaces.

## Test the model before buying a platform

Do not begin with another platform purchase. My take is to start with a 30-day exercise around ten high-volume or high-risk statements, such as delivery promises, return rights or product eligibility. A proposed minimum is one pilot lead with about two days a week protected, four or five specialists contributing a few hours each week, and an executive sponsor joining a short weekly decision meeting. Altogether that is roughly three to four person-weeks over 30 days by my estimate; adjust the shape to your organization.

Agree the internal coalition before the statements get owners. Commercial leadership should stand behind the promises, legal behind the evidence, customer service behind the real customer questions and regional market leads behind local conditions. Without that agreement, a catalog record is only a document. In most organizations the harder part is political, because legal and regional market owners rarely share a weekly working rhythm with digital teams. The sponsor's short weekly meeting exists mainly to create that rhythm until the records make it habitual.

Make the decision rights explicit:

| Role | Work and authority |
| --- | --- |
| Business owner | Approves what is true and where it applies; remains accountable for the statement |
| Information maintainer | Keeps the record, source and review date current; runs correction tests |
| Local market owner | Confirms local scope and can block release when a regional rule is unclear |
| Legal or compliance specialist | Reviews evidence and sensitive commitments; can require the statement to remain unavailable |
| Security and technology specialist | Controls system access and tests that reading information cannot become an unauthorized action |
| Executive sponsor | Resolves ownership conflicts and decides whether to scale, pause or stop |

First record how long it takes to find the authority behind each statement, how many versions conflict and how many lack an owner. Then create the ten catalog records, connect them to one controlled assistant and test correction outside production. For this pilot, I would set a service goal of removing an expired answer from the company website and controlled assistant within four business hours of an approved change. The board-ready measure is the time from a changed business promise to a corrected answer in every channel the company controls.

The timing also has a regulatory side. [The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act, the EU law on artificial intelligence that entered into force in August 2024](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), phases in its obligations through 2028. Following the AI Omnibus, an amendment package that entered into force in July 2026 and simplified parts of the Act, the main high-risk duties apply from December 2027, and from August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products. Those duties require human oversight, record-keeping and accuracy controls for higher-risk AI uses. Europe's data-protection rules already give people the right to have inaccurate personal data corrected. A governed catalog does not by itself make a company compliant, but owners, review dates and correction tests are exactly the habits these frameworks expect. For the pilot's business case, that is a second return on the same work.

That boundary must remain visible. A company can test its website, assistant, service tool and stored copies used to speed responses. It cannot guarantee immediate removal from a public Google summary, a customer's saved chat or an external AI service that has already copied an answer. If internal correction becomes faster while customer-facing answers remain persistently inconsistent, the catalog is better content maintenance, but it has not yet become the strategic asset described here.

Test failure as deliberately as success. Ask the assistant for an expired statement and confirm that it refuses or retrieves the replacement. Give it a Norwegian customer with a Swedish condition and check that it does not mix the policies. Then place a malicious instruction in an outside source and verify that this **prompt injection**, an attempt to manipulate AI software through untrusted text, cannot increase access or turn a policy lookup into an unauthorized refund.

Visibility metrics can guide the next decision when they are tied to accuracy. **Mention rate** measures how often a fixed set of AI answers names the company. **AI share of voice** compares those mentions with a defined group of competitors. For a mid-size Nordic company, a workable version is five fixed customer questions about one important product category, asked monthly in the AI assistants its customers actually use: is the company named, does the answer match the current approved statement, and how does that compare with three named competitors? If the company is absent from accurate answers, leaders may prioritize missing evidence. If it is mentioned often with an expired policy, correction comes first. Attention without accuracy increases the risk.

At day 30, require three deliverables: a register of the ten statements with owners and sources, a record of the correction and permission tests, and a recommendation for the next investment. Scale when every statement has an accountable owner, the correction goal holds in controlled systems and the permission tests pass. Pause when evidence or regional authority remains disputed. Stop or redesign the approach when stale answers repeatedly survive or the assistant crosses from reading to acting without approval. If the test scales, the continuing cost is mostly people, with licenses the smaller share. I would plan for two or three people owning records and reviews as a defined part of their roles, plus connection and monitoring work, and budget it as content operations until the evidence justifies anything larger.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Start with a staffed 30-day test of ten important business statements, backed by commercial, legal, service and regional leaders. Measure correction time in company-controlled channels, test expired and wrong-market information, prevent unauthorized action, and finish with documented evidence for a decision to scale, pause or redesign the investment.</p></div>

## Choose a partner that can recommend less technology

Only after the operating model is clear should the supplier question enter. The consultancy that modeled and connected your current content may be a sensible candidate because it already understands the information structure. [Valtech, a global digital consultancy](https://www.valtech.com/), and implementation firms in the partner networks of Contentful, Sanity and [Optimizely, a digital-experience platform](https://www.optimizely.com/partners), are examples to evaluate, and naming them is no endorsement.

Platform knowledge has real value, and a partner badge does not guarantee independence. Ask whether the consultancy is paid only to solve your information problem or also benefits when you buy more licenses. Require it to explain when the existing content system is enough and when another connection, policy or monitoring layer is justified. A credible partner should be able to recommend less technology when that is the better answer.

Put control into the contract. Your company should own the information model, receive usable exports, decide where data is stored and processed, restrict access for every person and AI system, and get a tested handover if the relationship ends. Reversibility is the real test of a sound catalog decision: when the company owns the model and the exports, it can move the catalog to another partner or another platform without rebuilding the knowledge. Then begin with the business promises that cause the most harm when they are wrong. The page may no longer carry the whole customer journey, but the truth must never travel without its conditions.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The consultancy that built a modern content platform may be well placed to extend it, provided its incentives and contract preserve company ownership, usable exports, access control and a tested handover. Define the knowledge and decision rights first, then start building the catalog before more software answers and acts with company information.</p></div>
