Your website is becoming optional in some of the moments that shape a purchase. A customer can receive a comparison, policy answer or recommendation inside Google or an AI assistant before your homepage loads. The site will remain useful, but it is losing its old privilege as the default front door.

A [July 2025 study by Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), a nonpartisan US research organization, tracked the March browsing behavior of 900 adults. People clicked a traditional search result in 8% of visits when Google displayed an AI summary, compared with 15% when it did not; they clicked a source cited inside the summary in just 1% of visits. The observational study cannot prove that the summary caused every missing click, but it exposes the management problem: your company can be considered without receiving a visit.

I think the strategic measure hiding here is **correction latency**, the time between a business fact changing and every relevant interface using the approved new version. An **answer supply chain** is the combination of systems, owners and rules that moves that fact from its source to a website, assistant or agent. Its control point is a **knowledge catalog**, a governed collection of company claims that machines can find and people can correct.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The website is becoming one interface among many. Leaders now need to manage how quickly a verified answer reaches every interface that may speak for the company.</p></div>

## The first answer is leaving your website

The movement started before the current wave of AI. A [2024 analysis by audience-research company SparkToro and Datos, which provides records of browsing behavior](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/), found that 58.5% of US Google searches and 59.7% of EU searches ended without a click. Those figures included sessions that ended and searches that were rewritten, so they should not be presented as an AI effect. They establish something more basic: a search journey often ends before the open web receives a visitor.

The interface is now changing too. A **generative user interface**, or GenUI, is a screen assembled for a particular request rather than selected from a fixed set of pages. Google demonstrated this in its [November 2025 Gemini 3 AI-model release](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/), and in April 2026 it published [A2UI v0.9](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-v0-9-generative-ui/), a public technical format through which an AI system can describe the interface it needs while the receiving application renders approved components. These releases prove capability, not mass adoption, but they show why the prebuilt page is no longer the only possible surface.

The next change is from answers to action. An **AI agent** is software that can pursue a goal through several steps, such as gathering options and coordinating a request, instead of returning only a block of text. At Google Cloud Next ’25, Google’s annual cloud conference, the company announced the open [Agent2Agent protocol, or A2A](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/), with support and contributions from more than 50 technology partners. A protocol is simply a shared set of rules. A2A lets participating agents communicate, exchange information securely and coordinate actions across business applications.

That does not make every claim true or every transaction authorized. A purchasing assistant may ask a supplier agent about availability, but the surrounding business systems still decide whether it may reveal a contract price, accept a delivery date or place an order. I don’t know how quickly customers will delegate everyday purchases because liability, fraud and habit remain real barriers. The direction is clear enough, though: company knowledge must work even when the company did not design the interface around it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Search can finish without a click, GenUI can assemble the screen on demand, and A2A can connect agents across systems. Reliable knowledge and authority to act therefore have to be managed separately beneath the interface.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog is a control layer

In a June 2026 video, [Phil Risher of Phlash Consulting](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo) describes a knowledge catalog as “a verified data set of information about your business.” It is a useful label, but the official Google material reviewed for this article does not define a Google product or standard by that name. I use it for an operating model: approved business claims, linked to their sources and made available in a form software can query.

The catalog should not become another master database. A product information management system, or PIM, remains the source for product attributes. Master-data management keeps important shared records, such as customer or product identities, consistent across systems. An enterprise data catalog tells teams which datasets exist and who owns them; a content management system manages publishable explanations; and a knowledge graph can map relationships between facts. The knowledge catalog should reuse those capabilities and expose the approved answer, not copy every underlying record into a parallel set of controls.

Each catalog claim needs enough context to survive outside its original page: the approved wording, supporting source, accountable owner, market or audience, effective date, next review and permitted use. A negotiated discount can be correct inside one customer agreement and still become an unauthorized public offer. I call that failure **promise drift**: a valid fact loses its boundary and turns into a commitment nobody approved.

The operating model should be equally explicit. An executive sponsor owns the business scope, the people responsible for each subject approve the claims, the content and data teams publish them, and legal or security owners set the access and action limits. Conflicts need a named decision owner, while every change needs an audit trail and a rollback path. European companies can put this work inside existing [General Data Protection Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) controls for personal data and their [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) governance for AI systems instead of creating a parallel approval structure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog connects approved claims to existing systems of record, owners and permission rules. Its job is to prevent stale information and promise drift without duplicating the company’s data estate.</p></div>

## Structured content creates the head start

**Structured content** stores meaning in named, reusable fields rather than burying it inside a finished page. A policy record, for example, can hold its rule, market, exceptions, source and review date separately from the design used to display it. **Web structured data**, often called schema markup, is narrower: it adds standardized machine-readable labels to page content for systems such as search engines. Structured data has broader meanings elsewhere in an enterprise, so the page-markup technique should not be confused with the underlying content model.

Google’s [May 2025 guidance for AI experiences in Search](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search) says structured data shares content information in a machine-readable form that its systems consider, while also telling publishers to create unique, non-commodity material. Both parts matter. Structure without proprietary knowledge produces well-packaged sameness; distinctive knowledge locked inside page copy remains difficult to reuse and govern.

A **headless content management system** stores content separately from page design and supplies it to different channels through software interfaces. I think an organization with clean content models, clear ownership and working interfaces has a meaningful architectural head start because much of the catalog’s raw material is already separated from presentation. That is an inference from the architecture, not a measured market advantage. A headless platform filled with duplicated, generic or stale content solves very little.

The platform market is already moving in this direction. Enterprise content providers Sanity, Contentful and Optimizely all introduced features that connect AI agents to managed content during 2025 and 2026. Their roadmaps are vendor evidence rather than neutral proof of demand, but they confirm that the content layer is being prepared for machine consumers as well as human readers.

This is also where the shift meets [*The Digital Singularity Shift*](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/), my 2024 book about AI making functional differentiation easier to reproduce and moving durable value toward meaning, story and belief. I think generated interfaces illustrate part of that mechanism without proving the whole thesis. If a usable screen can be assembled for each request, polished presentation becomes easier to copy. Verified operating knowledge, distinctive judgment and earned trust remain harder.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structured content makes approved knowledge reusable beyond a page, and a well-governed headless CMS can provide the starting material. The advantage comes from proprietary, current knowledge, not from the platform label alone.</p></div>

The mapping is the disappearing front door. Once an assistant assembles the interaction around a person’s intent, the visible page no longer controls the sequence. For a business, the catalog must supply both the fact and its boundary beneath whatever interface appears, or convenience will accelerate promise drift.

## Make the first investment falsifiable

My take is that the first return will appear in lower correction latency and fewer contradictory answers before it appears in fully autonomous commerce. That prediction can be tested inside one organization. If a governed catalog cannot improve the speed and consistency of answers against today’s baseline, it has failed its first business case and should not receive an enterprise rollout.

Run a 12-week test in one market, for one product or service, using 25 commercially important customer questions. Before building anything, record the answers currently given by the website, support material, sales material, an owned assistant if one exists, and a small set of public AI services. Keep the questions fixed; record the service, model version when available, date and market; and repeat each public-AI question three times each week so response variation is visible rather than hidden.

Set the decision gate before a supplier begins. One reasonable bar is that at least 24 of the 25 questions produce the approved answer across owned interfaces, those interfaces make no unauthorized offer, and the time required to correct an answer falls by at least half. Keep traditional search rankings in the dashboard, but add **mention rate**, the share of tested AI responses that name the company, and **AI share of voice**, the company’s share of mentions among a fixed competitor set. These are visibility measures for this test, not universal standards and not proof of revenue.

The sponsor should own the budget and scale decision. Subject owners approve facts, the content-platform lead implements the records, and legal or security reviewers can reject unsafe data or permissions. A weekly decision meeting should resolve conflicting answers. The team and supplier should log actual hours and cost so the maintenance burden is part of the result rather than an estimate invented after the pilot.

The same consultancy that built the content platform may be the fastest credible partner because it already understands the models and integrations. Valtech, a global experience and transformation firm, and agencies in the Contentful, Sanity or Optimizely partner networks are possible places to look, not automatic endorsements. A partner badge proves a relationship with a platform; it does not prove independence, quality or fit.

Ask each bidder to disclose revenue or resale incentives tied to the recommended platform, show how records and content models can be exported, state who owns the test set and governance model, and demonstrate how access can be revoked or a change rolled back. Platform expertise has value. Undisclosed dependence is the problem.

I think starting now means selecting the first 25 questions and naming their owners before buying another AI product. Scale when accuracy, correction speed and logged cost pass the agreed gate; pause if access, audit or rollback fails; and stop if the answer supply chain performs no better than the pages it was meant to improve. On a web increasingly mediated by agents, the company that corrects the truth fastest is the company that is hardest to misrepresent.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Use a 12-week, 25-question test with fixed prompts, named owners, repeatable measurements and a pre-agreed scale, pause or stop decision. Buy independence and portability, then expand only when correction speed improves without creating unauthorized promises.</p></div>
