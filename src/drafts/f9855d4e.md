Your website is becoming irrelevant to a growing share of customer decisions.

It is not disappearing. People will still visit websites to understand complex offers, complete transactions and build a direct relationship with a company. What is disappearing is the guarantee that discovery must lead to a click and that the company gets to design every step that follows.

That distinction changes what the durable digital asset is. I think many leadership teams still treat the finished page as the asset and the information inside it as material for filling the page. As AI systems move between the customer and the company, that order reverses: the interface becomes temporary, while the company’s verified knowledge has to survive every interface.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The website will remain useful, but it is losing its position as the required entrance to a business. The durable asset is the company knowledge that can still be understood and trusted when the customer journey happens somewhere else.</p></div>

## Google is replacing the click with an answer and an action

The movement away from websites began before the current wave of artificial intelligence. In 2024, [SparkToro, an audience-research company, and Datos, a provider of browsing data](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/), estimated that 58.5% of US Google searches and 59.7% of EU searches ended without a click. Those figures include sessions that ended and searches that were reformulated, so they do not show that AI caused the behavior. They show that even the old search model often failed to deliver a website visit.

AI-generated summaries make the change easier to see. [Pew Research Center, a nonpartisan US research organization, examined the Google browsing activity of 900 American adults in March 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/). People clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits when Google displayed an AI summary, compared with 15% when no summary appeared. They clicked a source cited inside the summary in only 1% of visits. About 60% of searches phrased as questions produced a summary, which helps explain the shift from entering keywords to holding something closer to a conversation.

The next step goes beyond answering. An AI agent is software that receives a goal and permission to carry out several steps for a person or organization. At Google Cloud Next ’25, the company’s annual enterprise technology conference, Google launched [Agent2Agent, or A2A](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/), with support from more than 50 technology partners. A2A is a shared set of rules that allows independently built agents to exchange information and coordinate work across business systems. A procurement agent could, for example, ask a supplier’s agent about availability and terms before preparing an order.

That possibility should not be confused with full autonomy. A2A helps agents communicate; it does not decide whether a fact is true, grant permission to spend money or transfer responsibility away from the business. I don’t know how quickly customers will delegate high-stakes purchases, because trust, liability, payment and habit remain real barriers. Yet an agent only needs to compare options, exclude a supplier or arrange the next step to change the commercial journey before a website appears.

The old visibility dashboard therefore tells an incomplete story. Rankings still matter, but I think leaders will increasingly need mention rate, which shows how often AI answers name the company, and AI share of voice, which compares that visibility with competitors. These measures are still young and the vendors promoting them benefit from the shift. The useful question underneath them is sound: when an AI system answers in your category, does your company enter the answer at all?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Search already sends fewer journeys to websites, AI summaries reduce clicks further, and agents can move a task forward across company systems. Visibility increasingly depends on being present in the answer and usable in the action, not only on ranking in a list of links.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog gives software something it can trust

In a June 2026 video, [Phil Risher of marketing firm Phlash Consulting](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo) uses the phrase “knowledge catalog” for a verified set of business information that can be deployed through an agent. Available Google documentation does not establish that phrase as the name of an official Google product or standard. I think it is still a useful name for a company-owned body of knowledge arranged so software can use it without guessing what a claim means, where it applies or who approved it.

The word *catalog* matters because this is more than a collection of pages. It contains the approved facts behind products and services, along with their conditions, sources and responsible owners. It can also hold the questions customers actually ask and the policies that determine what the company may promise. The aim is not to publish more content. It is to make the company’s existing knowledge clear enough to find, verify, update and withdraw.

Consider a delivery promise. On a conventional website, “delivery within 24 hours” may appear in campaign copy, a help article and several country pages, each with slightly different conditions. In a knowledge catalog, the promise is connected to the products and markets it covers, the policy that authorizes it, the person responsible for it and the date it must be reviewed. If an agent does not know the customer’s market, it should ask rather than quietly apply a US promise in Sweden.

That small example reveals the larger business risk. A wrong sentence on one page can be corrected on that page. A wrong claim used by search summaries, service assistants and outside agents can spread across the whole customer journey, while the company may not know which old source produced it. My take is that traceable claims become more valuable as interfaces multiply, because the ability to correct commercial misinformation depends on knowing where every answer came from.

Risher describes content as “training data for the future internet.” The strategic direction is right, but the near-term mechanism will often be retrieval rather than model training. Retrieval means an AI system looks up the latest approved information while forming an answer; it does not permanently teach that information to the underlying model. In both cases, the requirement is the same: machines need company knowledge in a form that retains its meaning and authority outside the page where it first appeared.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog is a governed source of company facts, not another pile of pages. It connects each important claim to its meaning, conditions, approved source and owner so that an answer can be used, challenged and corrected.</p></div>

## Structured content turns a CMS into a head start

Structured content stores an idea in named parts instead of locking it inside the layout of a finished page. The delivery promise, for example, becomes separate but connected information about time, market, eligible product, source policy and review date. Because those parts keep their meaning, one approved record can feed a website, a service tool or an agent without being copied into several conflicting versions.

Google itself points in this direction. In May 2025, [Google Search Central, its official guidance service for website owners](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search/), said that structured data helps companies share information in a machine-readable form its systems can consider. Machine-readable simply means software can identify what each part represents instead of inferring meaning from the visual arrangement of a page. The same guidance emphasized original, useful content, because structure can make a claim easier to handle but cannot make a generic claim distinctive or a stale claim true.

This is why organizations with a well-run headless content management system have a practical advantage. A headless CMS separates reusable content from the design of one website, then supplies it to websites, apps and internal tools through software connections. If a company has already modeled its products, policies and approval flows this way, it owns much of the raw material for a knowledge catalog. A modern platform filled with page-shaped copy does not offer the same head start.

The content-platform market is already adapting. [Sanity](https://www.sanity.io/blog/agent-actions-ai-building-blocks-for-structured-content) and [Contentful](https://www.contentful.com/blog/remote-mcp-now-generally-available) have released ways for authorized AI assistants and agents to work with structured content, while [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/agents/competitive-ai-share-of-voice-agent) now offers measurement of brand visibility in AI answers. These companies are selling into the trend, so their announcements are not neutral proof. Taken together, however, their roadmaps show that content platforms are being rebuilt for consumers other than human website visitors.

The same consultancy that modeled and connected a company’s current content may be well placed to extend that foundation. Valtech, a global digital consultancy, is one example; independent implementation firms in the Contentful, Sanity and Optimizely partner networks are others. They do the practical work of modeling, moving, governing and connecting content rather than merely selling a platform license. I think independence matters because a company invested in one platform has a reason to make that platform the answer. Independence does not guarantee good advice, but it reduces that conflict and makes it easier for the client to retain ownership of the knowledge model and the freedom to move its information later.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structured content lets an approved fact travel without losing its meaning. A mature headless CMS provides useful raw material, and an independent content consultancy can extend it without turning the knowledge catalog into a permanent dependency on one platform.</p></div>

## Generative interfaces make the deeper shift visible

*[The Digital Singularity Shift](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/)*, my 2024 business book about AI making digital creation abundant, describes a world in which functional digital features become easier to reproduce and value moves toward the meaning underneath them. AI answers and acting agents now make that direction concrete. Generative user interfaces, usually shortened to GenUI, bring it to the screen: software can assemble an interface for one request at the moment it is needed instead of sending everyone through the same set of pages.

In November 2025, [Google introduced Gemini 3 experiments that generated a custom interface from a user’s prompt](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/). In April 2026, Google released version 0.9 of [A2UI, an open proposal for agent-driven interfaces](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-v0-9-generative-ui/). It allows an agent to request suitable interface elements while the receiving application keeps control of how those elements look and behave. Design still matters, but the screen becomes more adaptable and less permanent.

The closest science-fiction metaphor is the holodeck in *Star Trek: First Contact*, the 1996 film in which a computer builds an environment around the immediate mission. GenUI suggests a restrained business version: one customer receives a comparison, another receives a form, and both are assembled around what they are trying to do. When the scenery can be generated on demand, the quality of the underlying world becomes decisive.

That is the part of the digital singularity now coming into view. The testable consequence is simple: as more journeys are mediated by AI, companies that cannot trace and correct their own claims will lose visibility in answers and control over commercial errors. The first serious failure may not be a broken page. It may be a confident, polished answer built from a promise the business stopped making months ago.

GenUI is the spectacular part because we can watch the interface appear. I think the more important decision sits underneath it. The website is becoming one expression of the business among many; the knowledge catalog is the asset that matters now.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>GenUI makes interfaces adaptive and temporary, in line with the direction described in <em>The Digital Singularity Shift</em>. Generated screens are the spectacle, but verified company knowledge is their foundation. The knowledge catalog is what matters now.</p></div>
