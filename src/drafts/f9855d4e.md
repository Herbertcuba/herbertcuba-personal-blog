I tried to remember the last time a company website actually changed a decision I was about to make. I could not. I asked a chatbot, read the AI summary Google served me, compared two options inside a conversation, and never opened a single page.

Phil Risher, who runs marketing for home-services companies in the US, recently put numbers and a name to this shift in a video that has been viewed over 250,000 times. His claim: the website as the hub of your digital presence is dying. What replaces it is something most leadership teams have never heard of, a knowledge catalog.

I think he is right about the direction. The implication is bigger than he states it, especially for anyone who has spent the last decade investing in a modern content platform.

<div class="article-tldr">
<span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span>
<p>Search is turning into conversation, and conversation is turning into agents that act on our behalf. In that world your website stops being the destination and your verified, structured company data becomes the asset agents consume. That data layer is called a knowledge catalog. If your organization already runs a headless CMS, you are closer to having one than you think, and the same consultancies that built your content platform are the right people to build it.</p>
</div>

## What actually changed

The model the commercial web ran on for twenty-five years, search, click, visit, convert, is breaking at both ends.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Most Google searches already end without a click, and AI summaries cut clicking further. Google is moving from keywords to conversations to agents that act for the user. The new scoreboard is share of voice and mention rate.</p></div>

The first shift is zero-click search. In a 2024 clickstream study by SparkToro and Datos, 58.5% of US Google searches ended without any click at all, 59.7% in the EU. And when Google adds an AI summary, it gets starker: Pew Research tracked real browsing in 2025 and found people clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits with an AI summary, against 15% without. Links cited inside the summary were clicked 1% of the time.

The second shift is Google moving from keywords to conversations. People describe a situation and get a synthesized answer instead of typing three words and scanning ten blue links. In Pew's data, around 60% of question-form searches already got an AI summary.

The third shift is the big one. Agents. An agent is software that pursues a goal on your behalf: it reasons, calls tools, checks the result, and keeps going until the job is done. In the video, Risher quotes Google CEO Sundar Pichai calling 2027 the year of agents. What I can verify is close enough: in an April 2026 interview Pichai described Search turning into an "agent manager," and in April 2025, at Cloud Next, Google launched A2A, an open protocol that lets one company's agent talk to another's, with more than 50 launch partners. Field-service platforms are already racing to add AI booking; ServiceTitan has shipped AI assistants that handle it.

Take a regional plumbing company with forty technicians. Today a homeowner searches, clicks, reads, and calls. In the agentic version, the homeowner's assistant asks the company's agent: do you cover this zip code, what does a water heater replacement cost, when is the earliest slot. The answers come from the company's data. The homepage is never rendered.

Measurement follows. When there are no clicks, rankings stop being the scoreboard. The replacements are share of voice and mention rate: how often agents name you when they answer.

## What a knowledge catalog is

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog is a verified, structured dataset about your company, deployed so AI systems can query it. Your company's official answers, machine-readable and kept current, served to agents instead of rendered for browsers.</p></div>

Risher defines it as a verified dataset of information about your company, deployed as an agent. Services and service areas, pricing, credentials, policies, real customer questions. All of it structured and kept current, so when an agent asks, the answer it gets back is one you would sign off on. The term is his, and I think it is a good one, so I will use it rather than pretend Google named a product category.

For the engineers reading: the consumer here is an agent running a ReAct loop, a cycle of reason, act, observe. Frameworks like LangGraph exist to build exactly this: a StateGraph defines the steps, a TypedDict (a Python dictionary where every field has a declared type) defines the data the agent carries between them. The plumbing company's catalog is what that loop queries when it needs ground truth about pricing or coverage.

That reframes what your content is. Risher phrases it sharply: your content is training data for the future internet. Every page you publish is read by machines long before a human skims it.

## Why structured content is the key

The catalog only works if the data inside it can be trusted by a machine.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents cannot responsibly act on free-flowing marketing prose. They need typed, validated fields with owners and timestamps. Structured content, content broken into governed pieces instead of pages, is what makes a knowledge catalog possible at all.</p></div>

A long marketing page about water heater services is fine for a human with patience. For an agent it is a liability. Somewhere in paragraph nine there is a price, but is it current, does it include installation, does it apply to this zip code? The agent has to guess, and agents that guess get things wrong in your name.

Structured content solves this by breaking information into typed fields with clear ownership. Price is a number with a currency and a valid-from date. Coverage area is a list of zip codes maintained by operations. When the homeowner's assistant asks what a replacement costs, the answer comes from a field someone owns, pulled from prose nobody has to parse.

Google says the same in its own guidance to site owners: publish unique, non-commodity content, and use structured data to share it "in a machine-readable way." In my experience, this is the part organizations underestimate. They hear "AI needs our data" and picture a chatbot bolted onto the website. The actual work is editorial: deciding what your official answers are, and giving each one an owner who keeps it true.

## Headless CMS is a head start

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A modern headless CMS already stores content as structured, API-served data instead of pages. That is most of what a knowledge catalog needs. Organizations that made that investment are years ahead of those still publishing page-shaped content.</p></div>

If your organization runs a headless CMS like Contentful, Sanity, or Optimizely's SaaS platform, your content already lives as structured entries behind an API. The website is one consumer of that content. A knowledge catalog is a second consumer, exposed in a form agents can query and act on. The vendors see it coming: all three have shipped agent-facing products in the last year.

I have written before about [the quiet truth about headless CMS](/posts/the-quiet-truth-about-headless-cms/). The frontend flexibility got all the attention at the time. The asset that compounds is the content model underneath, and that model is exactly what this next era rewards. Companies still trapped in page-centric platforms will have to retrofit structure onto prose. Companies with a clean content model mostly have to expose and govern what they already hold.

## This was predictable, and it was predicted

Claiming foresight costs nothing, so the specifics have to carry the weight.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Digital Singularity Shift described a world where interfaces are generated on demand and value moves to the underlying knowledge. Agent-consumed knowledge catalogs are that thesis arriving in a very practical form.</p></div>

In [The Digital Singularity Shift](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/) I argued that we are moving toward generative UI, interfaces assembled in the moment for each user and each intent. Google has since shipped generative interfaces in Gemini 3 and published A2UI, an open standard for agent-driven interfaces. If the interface is generated on demand, the durable asset is what sits beneath it: verified knowledge the generation draws from. A website is a frozen interface. A knowledge catalog is the layer that survives when interfaces stop being frozen.

Fair warning, the death of the website has been predicted before. Mobile apps were supposed to kill it, then voice search. Those were new channels. An agent is a new intermediary that sits between the customer and every channel at once, and the platform owner is rebuilding its core product around it.

<p>Late in the film, Theodore asks Samantha how many others she is talking to while she talks to him. Thousands, she admits. The conversation he experiences is a sliver of her actual traffic. That is the web your company is heading into. The human browsing your site is one conversation among thousands, and most of the others are agents querying your data at machine speed. Building for only the human in that scene means building for the sliver.</p>

I don't know whether 2027 is exactly the year this tips. Timing predictions in this space have a poor track record, mine included. But I think the direction is settled, and direction is what investment decisions should price in.

## Who should build it

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Go to the consultancy that built your content platform, the firm that already knows your content model. Firms invested in selling you a specific platform will answer every question with their own product.</p></div>

My take: start with the partner who built your content platform. The Valtechs of the world, and the established Contentful, Sanity, and Optimizely partners, already understand your content model and how your organization actually changes. A knowledge catalog is an extension of that work: same model, new consumer, stricter verification.

Be wary of platform-invested vendors here. If someone's revenue depends on you buying their specific tool, their answer to "what is a knowledge catalog" will suspiciously resemble whatever they sell. A consultancy starts from your content and your agents' needs. A platform vendor starts from its license.

## Where to start

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Pick one high-value domain, structure it and verify it, then expose it to agents and measure whether they mention you more. A small, governable start beats a grand strategy deck.</p></div>

For the plumbing company, the starting point is obvious: pricing and coverage for its top five services. For your organization, pick the domain agents will ask about first. Structure it in your CMS, assign an owner to every field, and expose it in a form agents can consume. Then track the new scoreboard: are you being named, and for what.

I think the companies that move early here get a durable edge, the same kind early movers on structured content got when search rewarded it. The catalog compounds. Every verified answer makes the next one easier to trust.

The website had a good twenty-five year run as the center of digital strategy. The next center is your knowledge, verified and machine-readable, doing its thousands of conversations whether anyone ever visits your homepage or not.
