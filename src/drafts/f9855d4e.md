Your website is becoming irrelevant to a growing share of customer decisions. It will still explain products, serve customers and close business, but the answer that shapes a decision may now appear in search, an AI assistant or an agent before the customer sees a page. The website is not disappearing. It is losing its position as the automatic center of the journey.

That creates an **answer-control gap**: the distance between the approved company record you can govern and the public answer you can only observe and influence. The boundary matters. You can control source records and the path into interfaces you own, but you cannot force an external AI service to update on your schedule, use your data or respect your permissions.

I think the strategic response is a **knowledge catalog**, a governed body of company knowledge designed to work beyond any single page. Its purpose is concrete: move approved answers through owned channels faster, preserve the context that makes a fact safe to use and prevent software from making commitments nobody authorized.

## From search results to generated journeys

The shift is already visible in search. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), a nonpartisan American research organization, published a July 2025 analysis of browsing activity from 900 US adults during March of that year. People clicked a traditional Google Search result in 8% of visits that included an AI summary, compared with 15% when no summary appeared. They clicked a source cited inside the summary in only 1% of visits.

Pew also found that 60% of queries beginning with “who,” “what,” “when” or “why” produced an AI summary. That is a specific subset, not every question asked in search, but it shows why conversational behavior matters: the more a query resembles a request for an answer, the more likely the interface is to provide that answer directly.

The movement away from clicks began before widespread AI answers. [SparkToro, an audience-research company, and Datos, a browsing-data provider](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/), estimated in July 2024 that 58.5% of American Google searches and 59.7% of searches in the European Union ended without a click. Those figures include both ended sessions and revised searches, so they do not prove that AI caused the missing clicks. They do show that website traffic was already an incomplete measure of whether a company entered the customer's consideration.

The interface is changing at the same time. A **generative user interface**, often shortened to GenUI, creates a screen for the request at hand instead of selecting a finished page from a fixed library. Google demonstrated request-specific interfaces when it released its Gemini 3 AI model in [November 2025](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/). In [April 2026](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-v0-9-generative-ui/), it published A2UI v0.9, a machine-readable format for task-specific interface instructions: an AI system describes what the interface needs to do, while the receiving application renders components it has already approved. These releases show a product direction rather than universal adoption, but the screen can now be assembled after the question is asked.

The next step is action. An **AI agent** is software that works through several steps toward a goal, such as comparing options, collecting information and requesting a booking. On [April 9, 2025](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/), Google announced Agent2Agent, or A2A, with support and contributions from more than 50 technology partners. A protocol is an agreed method of communication; A2A lets separately built agents exchange information securely and coordinate work across business applications.

A2A is transport, not authority. It does not make an outside model read your catalog, obey your permissions or gain the right to sign a contract. I don't know when customers will trust agents with high-stakes purchases, because fraud, liability and habit remain serious constraints. I think A2A still matters because answers and actions can move away from the company page, making the quality of the knowledge underneath more important than the route used to reach it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Search often supplies an answer without a visit, GenUI can assemble a screen around a request, and A2A lets agents coordinate. Adoption will vary, but the customer journey is already separating from the company page.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog gives facts boundaries

[Phil Risher of Phlash Consulting](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo), a digital-marketing firm for local-service businesses, offered a useful definition in June 2026: a knowledge catalog is “a verified data set of information about your business.” I think that is the right starting point, although an enterprise needs more than a collection of correct facts. Every important claim also needs its source, owner, scope, effective date, approval state and permitted use.

There is a naming wrinkle worth understanding. [Google Cloud, Google's cloud-computing division, introduced Google Cloud Knowledge Catalog in April 2026](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/data-analytics/introducing-the-google-cloud-knowledge-catalog) to provide governed context about enterprise data and AI assets. That named product may become one component of a solution. The broader knowledge catalog described here is a vendor-neutral operating model that connects business decisions, records and delivery paths, regardless of which software holds each part.

Imagine a manufacturer that has verified that one current product model no longer contains a restricted chemical. The claim record points to the laboratory report, identifies the model and serial-number range, names the markets where the statement applies, carries an effective date and assigns an accountable owner. Its approval can let a customer assistant answer a buyer's question while rejecting a request to issue a regulatory certificate or extend the claim to an older unit. A fact can be correct and still be unsafe when software loses its boundaries.

The delivery path turns that record into an answer the company can govern. When an owned assistant receives a question, it requests the current record from the catalog. The catalog checks who or what is asking, the relevant market and the requested action before returning the approved fields. The interface shows the answer while the system logs the record version, the access decision and the output.

If the approval is withdrawn, the owner can restore the previous version or require the assistant to refuse safely. The audit trail shows what changed, who approved it and which answers used that version. This is where control is real. Once a public AI service creates an answer outside this path, the company can publish better evidence, watch the result and seek corrections, but it cannot guarantee the output.

A knowledge catalog should connect existing systems rather than pretend to replace them. Product systems may hold specifications, while a content-management system holds the explanation customers can understand. The new agreement is organizational: which source wins, who decides, what context travels with an answer and which actions remain forbidden.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog connects an approved claim to its evidence, boundaries and allowed uses, then carries it through a checked and auditable path into interfaces the company owns.</p></div>

## Structured content creates the head start

**Structured content** stores an idea as named, reusable fields instead of burying it inside a finished block of page copy. In the manufacturer example, the claim, product model, market, evidence, effective date and approval status remain separate. A website, service assistant or internal agent can then use the same approved record without guessing which sentence contains the rule.

This is different from **web structured data**, also called schema markup, which adds standard labels to visible page content so search systems can classify it. In [May 2025 guidance for its AI search experiences](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search), Google Search Central, the company's documentation for website owners, said its systems consider information shared in machine-readable structured data and asked publishers to create unique, non-commodity content. Both parts matter. Better labels cannot rescue generic or stale knowledge, and valuable knowledge is difficult to govern when it remains trapped inside finished pages.

Risher describes content as “training data for the future internet.” I would use that phrase carefully because AI systems do not all learn in the same way: some are trained on large collections, while others retrieve current records when they answer. The strategic point survives the technical distinction. Company knowledge is becoming source material for answers and actions, so its structure, origin and permission to use matter more.

A **headless content management system**, or headless CMS, stores managed content separately from the website design and makes it available to other channels through software interfaces. I think organizations with mature content models, named owners and reliable integrations have a substantial head start because much of their knowledge is already separated from presentation. That is an architectural judgment, not a measured market advantage. A modern CMS filled with duplicates, outdated claims and unclear ownership offers very little advantage.

This change also fits the argument in [*The Digital Singularity Shift*](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/), my 2024 book about what happens when AI makes functional differences easier to copy. Its published thesis is that competition then moves toward meaning, story and belief. I am not claiming the book named A2A or today's product stack. The match is structural: when software can generate a useful interface for each request, presentation becomes easier to reproduce, while distinctive company knowledge and the trust attached to it remain harder to copy. Structured content is how that value travels without losing its origin.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structured content preserves the meaning and limits of a claim beyond one page. A mature headless CMS supplies useful raw material, but the real advantage comes from distinctive knowledge, clear ownership and reliable delivery paths.</p></div>

## Start with one answer, not another platform

The evidence here contains no measured Swedish or Norwegian enterprise case with a published baseline and outcome. That gap matters because American click behavior is not a forecast for every Nordic company, and vendor releases are not proof of adoption. The responsible response is a local, reversible pilot that creates evidence inside your own business.

Choose one product family, one market and one customer question whose wrong answer creates a visible cost, such as a correction, a compliance escalation or an unauthorized offer. Build one claim record with its source, scope, dates, owner, approval and allowed actions, then connect it to one interface you own. Give the test six weeks and cap the effort before work begins.

| Role | Planning envelope | Decision right |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Executive sponsor | 4 hours total | Owns the budget and the scale-or-stop decision |
| Business owner | 4 hours per week | Approves the claim and resolves source conflicts |
| Content lead | 8 hours per week | Owns the record, wording and review workflow |
| Integration lead | 8 hours per week | Owns retrieval, access checks, logging and rollback |
| Legal and security | 12 hours shared | Can veto an unsafe disclosure or action |

Using six weeks and eight-hour working days, that envelope is roughly 136 internal hours. I think an executive should also set an external-fee ceiling of SEK 500,000 and reject any requirement for a new platform license during the pilot. These are proposed decision limits, not industry benchmarks. If one governed answer cannot produce useful evidence within them, a wider program has not earned approval.

Make the evaluation repeatable. Select twenty real customer questions from search or service logs and prepare two ordinary phrasings of each, so the team can see whether wording rather than the catalog caused a change. Run every phrasing three times before and after one approved fact update. Keep the prompt set fixed, and record the service, displayed model version, date, market and every output. The business owner grades accuracy and scope against a written answer key; the risk reviewer grades whether each response stayed within its permitted use.

Keep the claim versions, approvals, answer key, outputs, grader decisions and rollback log for twelve months after the scale-or-stop decision, then apply the company's normal records policy. That retention period is a proposed pilot rule rather than a legal standard. Its purpose is to preserve enough evidence to explain the decision without creating an open-ended archive.

My take is to pass only if four conditions are met. The changed fact must reach every owned test interface in no more than half the current update time, create no unauthorized commitment in the full test set, reduce correction or review effort and survive a tested rollback. Public AI services belong in the observation set, not the pass gate, because their models and update cycles sit outside company control.

Keep search rankings, but add two bounded observations for those public services. **Mention rate** is the share of a fixed set of prompts whose answers name the company. **AI share of voice** is the company's share of all brand mentions across a fixed competitor set. Neither is a universal standard or proof of revenue, so pair both with a business result such as fewer corrections, fewer escalations or more qualified inquiries.

The consultancy that built the current content platform is a sensible place to start because it already knows the content model and integrations. [Valtech](https://www.valtech.com/), which describes itself as a global business transformation agency, is one possible example; the solution-partner directories maintained by content-platform companies [Contentful](https://www.contentful.com/partners/solutions/) and [Sanity](https://www.sanity.io/agency-partners), and by digital-experience company [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/partners/), offer other candidates. These are starting points, not endorsements, and a partner badge does not prove independence.

Require candidates to disclose license-resale income, platform investments and sales incentives. Separate service fees from software fees, secure full export rights, test the handover and make sure your company retains the knowledge records and audit evidence. Deep platform skill is valuable, but the design must serve your knowledge rather than a supplier's commercial agenda.

Begin with the answer whose loss of context would cost the business most. The website can remain an important interface, but the durable asset is the approved company knowledge that can travel, be checked and be withdrawn when the interface is no longer yours.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Start building the knowledge catalog now with a six-week, one-answer pilot, explicit decision rights, fixed resource limits and a business outcome beyond visibility. Control the records and serving paths you own; monitor and influence everything beyond them. Build the knowledge before the interface stops being yours.</p></div>
