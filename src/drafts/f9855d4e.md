A website can remain useful long after it stops being the center of the customer journey. Companies still fund it as the place where discovery, explanation and conversion come together, while customers increasingly ask a search engine or AI assistant to do that work for them.

The shift is already measurable. In March 2025, [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), a nonpartisan US research organization, tracked the browsing of 900 adults. People clicked a traditional result on 8% of visits when Google displayed an AI summary, compared with 15% when it did not. Sources inside the summary received a click in just 1% of visits.

This changes the investment question. The website still has a job, but the stronger long-term asset is the verified business knowledge that search systems, AI agents and future interfaces can use without first decoding a page.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The website is becoming one possible interface to the business, while the reusable knowledge beneath it is becoming the strategic asset.</p>
</div>

## Discovery is becoming delegation

The decline of the click began before the current wave of AI. A [2024 study by audience-research company SparkToro and clickstream provider Datos](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/) found that 58.5% of US Google searches and 59.7% of EU searches ended without a click. Those figures include abandoned and reformulated searches, so AI did not cause all of them. Pew supplies the AI-specific signal: roughly 60% of question-shaped searches in its March 2025 sample produced an AI summary.

We have heard predictions about the death of the website before. Mobile apps and social networks added destinations, yet they still expected people to visit an interface. An AI agent changes the mechanism because it can interpret a request and carry out several steps with limited human input.

Google made that direction concrete at Google Cloud Next, its annual enterprise technology conference, in April 2025. [Agent2Agent, or A2A](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/), is an open protocol that lets AI agents securely exchange information and coordinate work across different business systems. Trust, liability, payments and customer habits will slow fully autonomous commerce, so no precise adoption date is credible. Even so, the market is moving from helping people find a business toward helping software act on their behalf.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI answers remove the visit, and AI agents can remove the handoff from discovery to action; that makes machine-usable business knowledge commercially important.</p>
</div>

## A knowledge catalog is the new center

In a June 2026 video, [Phil Risher of digital marketing firm Phlash Consulting](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo) uses the term **knowledge catalog** for the verified information a company makes available to AI systems. It is a useful name, although it is not a verified Google product category. I mean a maintained body of facts about the business: products, services, pricing rules, policies, locations, credentials and answers to real customer questions.

For machines to use that knowledge reliably, it needs **structured content**. This means storing information in defined fields with clear relationships instead of burying it inside the layout of a page. A service area becomes data with a name, boundary, owner and review date, for example, rather than a sentence an AI system must infer from marketing copy. In May 2025, [Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search), Google's guidance service for website owners, likewise advised companies to provide unique content and structured data that its systems can read.

Verification matters as much as structure. A polished catalog full of stale prices or conflicting policies will produce fast, confident errors. Each important claim therefore needs an accountable owner, a source and a review cycle. Visibility also broadens beyond rankings toward **mention rate**, how often an AI answer names the company, and **share of voice**, its presence compared with competitors across relevant questions.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A knowledge catalog turns distinctive business facts into structured, owned and current information that AI systems can retrieve without guessing from web pages.</p>
</div>

## The investment has already moved upstream

A **headless content management system**, or headless CMS, stores content separately from website design and supplies it through application programming interfaces, which are standard connections used by other software. Companies with this architecture already have content models and reusable fields. That is a real head start, though a headless CMS filled with generic or poorly governed content is not yet a knowledge catalog.

The near-term investment should move toward the quality, ownership and portability of this knowledge layer, while repeated visual redesigns receive more scrutiny. An independent consultancy that already understands the content platform may be well placed to help. The selection should remain vendor-neutral and test for headless-CMS implementation capability, knowledge governance, export rights, where data is stored, fees that do not reward one platform choice, access controls and a handover proven in practice.

Ownership cannot sit vaguely with “digital.” Business functions must own the accuracy and permission of their claims, while technology and security own dependable access to them; one executive needs to resolve the trade-offs. This is the market movement I describe as [the Digital Singularity](/posts/mckinsey-digital-singularity/): stable boundaries between content, software and channels weaken as AI assembles an interaction around the user's intent. In that environment, knowledge designed for reuse compounds in value.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Headless CMS architecture provides useful raw material, but the defensible investment is governed and portable knowledge with explicit business ownership.</p>
</div>

## Generative UI makes the catalog the main event

**Generative UI** is an interface assembled by an AI model at the moment of a request instead of being fully designed in advance. In November 2025, [Google introduced generative interfaces in Gemini 3](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/), its AI model and assistant. A month later it introduced [A2UI](https://developers.googleblog.com/introducing-a2ui-an-open-project-for-agent-driven-interfaces/), an open specification that lets agents send interface descriptions which different apps can render safely.

Design still matters because people need clarity, trust and control. Its strategic weight changes when the screen can be recomposed for each person and task. The fixed frontend gets a shorter useful life, while accurate product facts, policies, permissions and relationships remain useful across websites, assistants and interfaces that have not been invented yet.

That is where I think near-term digital investment belongs. Keep the website good, but stop treating the page as the final form of the business. As generative UI makes interfaces temporary, the knowledge catalog becomes the durable center.
