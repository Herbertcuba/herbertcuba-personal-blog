Your website can be excellent and still vanish from the customer journey. A search engine can answer a question on its own page, an AI assistant can compare suppliers, and an authorized agent can complete a task without the customer visiting a company homepage.

The website still matters as a channel, but it is losing its privileged role as the digital hub. I think leaders should now treat pages as outputs of a more durable asset: a **knowledge catalog**, meaning a governed body of verified company information that people, search systems and AI agents can use.

This changes the investment question. The priority is no longer limited to improving the next website. It is to make the knowledge beneath every interface accurate, structured and usable within clear commercial limits.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The website is becoming one destination among many. The durable asset is governed company knowledge that can supply websites, AI answers and authorized agent actions.</p></div>

## Search no longer guarantees a visit

The traffic model was weakening before agents entered the discussion. A [2024 study by SparkToro and clickstream provider Datos](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/) found that 58.5% of US Google searches and 59.7% of EU searches produced no click. Those figures include sessions that ended and searches that were reformulated, so they do not show that AI caused the behavior.

AI-specific evidence points in the same direction. The nonpartisan [Pew Research Center tracked the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/). People clicked a traditional result on 8% of visits with a Google AI summary, compared with 15% of visits without one, while links cited inside the summary received clicks on only 1% of visits with a summary. This was an observational comparison rather than proof that the summary caused every difference, but the business implication is hard to ignore: being present in an answer can matter even when a visit never follows.

The next shift is from answers to action. An **AI agent** is software that can pursue a goal and take permitted steps, rather than only produce text. At Google Cloud Next ’25, the company’s annual cloud conference in April 2025, Google announced [Agent2Agent, or A2A](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/), an open protocol supported at launch by more than 50 technology partners. A protocol is a shared set of rules; A2A lets participating agents communicate, securely exchange information and coordinate actions across different systems.

That protocol supplies coordination, not truth. It does not certify a supplier’s facts or give an agent permission to commit money on a customer’s behalf. I don’t know whether agent-led buying will become normal in every industry in two years or five, but organizations need time to prepare the knowledge and controls those journeys require.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Search already resolves many journeys without a click, and A2A gives participating agents a way to coordinate. Accurate business knowledge and authority to act remain separate responsibilities for the company.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog holds the business answer

In his June 2026 video [*Your Website Won’t Matter in 3 Years (Here’s What to Build Instead)*](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo), consultant Phil Risher of Phlash Consulting used “knowledge catalog” for a verified set of information about a business that AI systems can consume. It is a useful working term, but it is not a verified Google product or official standard.

My working definition is a governed collection of company facts and explanations that can be reused outside the page where they were first published. It might cover products, services, market availability, pricing rules, policies, credentials, approved evidence and the questions customers repeatedly ask. I think each important record should identify its source, owner, applicable market, effective date and review status. In this context, “verified” means that the company can show who approved the information and when it was checked, not that a machine declared it true.

Consider a customer asking whether a product bought under one contract can be serviced in another country. A brochure may contain fragments of the answer, while the current exception sits in a policy document and the effective date lives in an internal system. A knowledge catalog brings those parts into one controlled answer that an employee, a website or an agent can retrieve without guessing.

Commercial authority remains a different layer. An agent may be allowed to explain the service policy but barred from approving a refund, changing a contract or exposing confidential terms. The catalog governs what the organization knows; identity, access and transaction rules govern what an agent may do with that knowledge.

Risher calls every piece of content “training data for the future internet.” I think the useful business reading is metaphorical: content becomes evidence that AI systems may retrieve, compare and cite. It does not mean that publishing a page automatically retrains every model.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog is a working model for approved, current and reusable company knowledge. It should preserve both the answer and its governance, while transaction permissions remain a separate control.</p></div>

## Structure makes knowledge reusable

Many web pages combine business information with the layout used to present it. **Structured content** separates that information into named fields and reusable records. A service policy, for example, can store the eligible product, market, contract type, exception and effective date as distinct pieces of meaning instead of burying them in one paragraph.

**Structured data** is different. It is standardized markup on a web page that describes and classifies the page’s visible content in a machine-readable format. [Google Search Central, Google’s guidance service for site owners, said in May 2025](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search) that structured data helps its systems consider page information, while the markup must match what visitors can see. Structured content keeps knowledge reusable across channels; structured data helps a search system interpret a specific published page.

A **headless content management system**, or headless CMS, stores and manages content separately from its visual presentation, then supplies it to websites, apps and other channels through controlled software connections. If an organization already models products, policies and support information this way, that architecture can provide a practical starting point for a knowledge catalog.

The advantage is conditional. A headless CMS full of duplicated, generic or ownerless content does not become trustworthy merely because other software can access it. The useful head start comes from reusable content models, clear ownership and review routines already being in place. My take is that the platform matters less than the discipline around the information inside it, especially when the next interface may be generated somewhere else.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structured content preserves meaning independently of a page, while structured data labels visible page content for machines. A headless CMS can supply the starting architecture only when the information is owned, current and reusable.</p></div>

## The interface becomes a variable

A **generative user interface**, often shortened to GenUI, is a screen assembled by AI for the user’s current request rather than designed once in advance for everyone. Google introduced prompt-generated interface experiments with [Gemini 3, a version of its Gemini AI assistant, in November 2025](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/). In April 2026, it published [A2UI v0.9](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-v0-9-generative-ui/), an open set of rules through which an agent can describe an interface and the receiving application can render it with its own components.

These developments do not establish that conventional websites will disappear. They show that presentation can increasingly be assembled when a request arrives, while the underlying facts still need a reliable source. I think this makes the knowledge layer more valuable relative to any single front end.

In [*The Digital Singularity Shift*](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/), my book about how AI makes functional advantages easier to reproduce, I argue that durable value moves toward meaning, story and belief. The current rise of generated interfaces and agent-readable knowledge is consistent with that thesis. A polished screen may become cheaper to assemble, while distinctive knowledge, explicit permissions and a trusted reason to choose the company remain organizational work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>GenUI makes the interface adaptable to each request, but it still depends on reliable underlying knowledge. That moves strategic weight from the fixed page toward governed facts, permissions and meaning.</p></div>

## Start with one costly question

Make the first project a controlled business question rather than a software purchase. Choose one customer or employee question whose answer is expensive when it is wrong, then trace every source needed to answer it. Decide which answer is authoritative, name its owner, record where and when it applies, and expose only the fields an external system should receive. Then test whether a person, the website and an authorized agent reach the same approved answer.

The consultancy that built your content platform may be a strong candidate for this work because it already understands the content model and integrations. Valtech, a global business-transformation agency, and specialists listed in the partner networks of content-platform providers Contentful, Sanity and Optimizely give buyers a practical field to evaluate. A partner badge confirms a relationship with a platform; it does not prove independence, quality or fit.

Platform expertise has real value, so the choice is not simply “vendor bad, consultant good.” I think leaders should ask every prospective partner to disclose license incentives and platform commitments, then compare portability, access controls, data location, ownership and handover terms. The goal is advice shaped by the company’s knowledge architecture rather than by the number of seats a supplier can sell.

Measurement must change as well. Keep rankings, but add company-defined measures for **mention rate**, the share of a fixed set of relevant AI prompts in which the company appears, and **AI share of voice**, its share of mentions among a chosen group of competitors. Fix the questions, AI systems, languages, markets and time period before comparing results. These are operating definitions, not universal standards, although the category is already being productized: Optimizely lists the [Conductor Competitive AI Share of Voice Agent](https://www.optimizely.com/agents/competitive-ai-share-of-voice-agent).

Start now, but start with governance rather than spectacle. The companies that control what machines can know about them, and what agents may do with that knowledge, will retain influence when the customer decision no longer happens on their page.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Choose one costly question, build a governed answer, test it across human and machine channels, and evaluate partners for both expertise and incentives. When agents become the front door, control of the knowledge beneath them becomes control of how the company is understood.</p></div>
