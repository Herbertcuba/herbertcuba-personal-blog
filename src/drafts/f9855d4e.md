I tried to map the next customer journey as a familiar funnel: search, click, website, conversion. The data removed one step. The website.

Phil Risher, who runs Phlash Consulting, put a useful name to the replacement in a YouTube video viewed more than 250,000 times. He calls it a knowledge catalog, a verified dataset about your company that AI systems can consume directly.

I think he has the direction right. The bigger change is who speaks for your company when the customer delegates the journey to an agent.

<div class="article-tldr">
<span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span>
<p>Search is becoming conversation, and conversation is becoming action. Companies need a verified, structured knowledge catalog that agents can query, test, and act on. A modern headless CMS gives you a head start, while answer ownership decides whether the system can be trusted.</p>
</div>

## Search moved from clicks to conversations

The old search funnel is breaking at both ends.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Google increasingly answers questions before a website visit happens. Agents add the next step by taking action for the user, which makes your company data more important than your homepage.</p></div>

A 2024 SparkToro and Datos study found that 58.5% of US Google searches ended without a click. The figure was 59.7% in the EU. Pew Research then tracked browsing in 2025 and found that people clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits when Google showed an AI summary, compared with 15% without one. Links cited inside the summary received clicks in 1% of visits.

The studies cover US and EU behavior, so the exact Nordic speed remains uncertain.

Agents extend the shift. An AI agent pursues a goal through tools and checks each result. Risher's video attributes the line "2027 is the year of the agents" to Sundar Pichai. I could not verify that wording. In April 2026, Pichai did describe Search becoming an "agent manager."

Google launched the Agent2Agent protocol, A2A, at Cloud Next in April 2025 with more than 50 partners. A2A gives agents a shared way to exchange information and coordinate work across companies. Field-service platform ServiceTitan already ships AI assistants that can handle booking.

Imagine a Nordic heat-pump company. Today, a customer searches for installation, opens three sites, compares prices, and calls. Tomorrow, the customer's agent asks which company serves the address, what installation costs, and when a certified technician is available. The homepage may never load.

Rankings will still tell part of the story. Share of voice and mention rate now tell you whether AI answers include your company at all.

## A knowledge catalog is your official answer set

Risher defines a knowledge catalog as verified company information that can be deployed through an agent.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog stores the answers your company is willing to stand behind. Each answer has a clear type, owner, scope, and validity period, so agents can use it without extracting facts from marketing prose.</p></div>

For our heat-pump company, the catalog contains service areas, prices, certifications, warranty terms, and real customer questions.

I think the term is useful because it adds accountability to familiar technology. Schema.org describes web content. Model Context Protocol, MCP, connects AI applications to tools and data. A2A coordinates agents. The catalog exposes governed company knowledge through these mechanisms.

Risher calls content "training data for the future internet." A catalog usually feeds retrieval at runtime rather than model training, but his direction holds: agents learn what your company can promise from the material you publish.

A single entry could look like this:

```yaml
service: heat_pump_installation
price_sek_from: 89000
includes_standard_installation: true
service_area: ["SE-AB"]
valid_until: 2026-12-31
answer_owner: pricing@company.example
```

The field names remove ambiguity. The date limits the answer. The owner says who approved it.

## Structured content gives agents something they can trust

An agent needs fields it can test before it can act safely.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structured content turns a claim into typed data with validation rules. Versioned entries, test questions, and expiry checks let the company reject stale answers and roll back a bad release.</p></div>

A page that says installation is "usually affordable across the Stockholm area" forces the agent to interpret prose. The catalog entry gives it a number, a region code, and a deadline.

Google's own Search Central guidance asks site owners to publish unique content and share structured data in a machine-readable way. That is the bridge from a page to an agent answer.

The technical path is simple enough to explain. The customer's agent uses a ReAct loop. ReAct means the model reasons about its next step, acts through a tool, observes the result, and repeats. It might call an MCP tool backed by the CMS API, read the price entry, then ask the company's booking agent for a slot through A2A.

Junior engineers often meet this pattern through LangGraph, a framework for agent workflows built as graphs. A StateGraph defines the steps and transitions. A TypedDict describes the Python dictionary passed between steps for static checking. Pydantic or JSON Schema can handle runtime validation.

For our heat-pump flow, the state holds the address, service type, approved price, and booking status. Once `valid_until` passes, the price transition fails closed. The agent returns "price unavailable" and routes the case to a human.

Before publishing a catalog version, run a golden set of common customer questions against it. Store the expected answers. A failed test blocks release. A bad release rolls back by restoring the previous catalog version. I think this verification loop is where structured content becomes operational infrastructure.

## Headless CMS gives you a head start

A modern content platform already stores much of the catalog's raw material.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Headless CMS platforms separate content from pages and expose it through APIs. Teams with clean models can add agent access and governance, while page-bound teams must first rebuild their content structure.</p></div>

Contentful, Sanity, and Optimizely already store content as reusable entries with schemas and APIs. Their recent agent-facing products show where the market is going. The website becomes one consumer of the content model. An MCP server can become another.

In my experience, the difficult part is the model. Someone must decide whether `price` means a fixed amount, a starting amount, or a market estimate. Pricing, operations, and legal settle that together.

Disciplined headless CMS programs have a structural advantage: content types, ownership workflows, and APIs already exist. I wrote about that foundation in [The Quiet Truth About Headless CMS](/posts/the-quiet-truth-about-headless-cms/). I think many companies bought frontend freedom and accidentally prepared for an agent economy.

## The Digital Singularity predicted the disposable interface

Generative UI makes the fixed frontend less central with every release.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>When an AI system can generate the interface for each request, the reusable asset moves underneath the screen. The knowledge catalog survives each generated interface because it supplies the verified facts.</p></div>

In [The Digital Singularity Shift](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/), I argued that interfaces would be assembled for each person and intent. Gemini 3 now ships generative interfaces, and Google published A2UI as an open standard for agent-driven interfaces.

A fixed website is one prepared view. GenUI can produce another for each request. Every view still needs the same verified source.

Websites survived earlier death predictions about apps and voice search. Those added channels. An agent changes the intermediary by sitting between the customer and every channel.

## Answer ownership turns data into delegated authority

The catalog speaks in your company's name, which gives every field operational weight.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An answer owner attests that a field is accurate, approved for a defined use, and valid for a stated period. The organization also needs a clear way to revoke that authority when the answer changes.</p></div>

For the heat-pump company, pricing owns the amount and operations owns the service area. Legal approves warranty language. A content lead keeps the answers coherent across channels.

People may resist signing a field because it sounds like unlimited personal liability. Make the attestation narrow: one answer, one use, one date, with a visible review process. The job becomes bounded.

This fits control work European companies already face. The AI Act adds transparency duties around AI interactions. GDPR already requires governance of personal data. A catalog gives both programs a place for approvals and revocation. Delegated authority needs a revocation path.

Klarna is the useful Nordic warning. Its AI assistant handled two-thirds of customer-service conversations in its first month in 2024. In 2025, the CEO said cost had received too much weight and the company was hiring humans back for quality. Public Nordic examples of governed catalogs remain scarce.

The strongest counterargument says platform indemnity and common verification standards will absorb the liability. They may reduce financial exposure. Approval of your current price and policy still belongs inside the company. You still need an answer owner.

## Choose a partner that can remain independent

The team that built your content platform may already know where the useful knowledge lives.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Start with an independent consultancy that understands your content model and can work across platforms. Keep the verified dataset portable and make agent access reversible.</p></div>

My take: begin with the consultancy that implemented your CMS. Valtech and independent CMS partners can extend the existing content model into an agent-facing catalog.

An independent consultancy can begin with your official answers and choose the smallest architecture that works. A vendor tied to one platform has a reason to turn the catalog into another license.

Ask two procurement questions early. Where does the verified dataset live? Who can export it and revoke agent access? If those answers are vague, your corporate memory may be moving into a closed system.

## Build the first catalog slice now

Start with the twenty questions customers ask before they buy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Choose one high-value domain, give every field an owner, expose it through a controlled interface, and test the answers monthly. Measure AI mentions alongside rankings and expand only after the first slice stays correct.</p></div>

For the heat-pump company, the first slice is pricing and service coverage. A content lead structures the fields. An engineer exposes a read-only MCP tool over the CMS API. Pricing and operations approve the golden answers before release.

Run the twenty-question test once a month. Ask the major assistants your most common customer questions. Record whether they mention your company, which facts they use, and where answers fail. AI visibility tools can automate the tracking, while a spreadsheet is enough to begin.

Give the pilot a production owner before it succeeds. I would budget six to ten weeks for one domain with a clean content model. The business case is access and control: more agent journeys reach a usable answer, while stale prices have a defined stop.

A website can keep serving people. Your knowledge catalog will serve the conversations they delegate. Build it before the next customer asks an agent about you, because the agent will answer from whatever it can find.
