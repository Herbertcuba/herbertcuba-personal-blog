Your website is becoming irrelevant to a growing share of customer decisions. It will still explain products, serve customers and close business, but the answer that shapes a decision may now appear in search, an AI assistant or an agent before the customer sees a page.

That creates what I call the **answer-control gap**: the distance between the approved company record you can govern and the public answer you can only observe and influence. The distinction matters. You can control the source record and the path into interfaces you own. You cannot force an external AI service to update on your schedule, use your data or respect your permissions.

I think the strategic response is a **knowledge catalog**, a governed body of company knowledge designed to survive beyond one page. It is not a promise to control the whole internet. Its value is more practical and testable: approved answers should move faster through governed channels, keep their business context and stop software from making commitments nobody authorized.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The website is becoming one interface among many. A knowledge catalog can close the answer-control gap inside channels the company governs, while public AI answers remain an influence and monitoring problem.</p></div>

## The answer has left the page

The shift is visible in how people use search. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), a nonpartisan American research organization, published a July 2025 analysis of browsing activity from 900 US adults during March of that year. People clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits that included an AI summary in Google Search, the search engine operated by the technology company Google, compared with 15% when no summary appeared. They clicked a source cited inside the summary in only 1% of visits, and roughly 60% of searches phrased as questions produced an AI summary.

A second study shows that the behavior predates the current wave of AI answers. [SparkToro, an audience-research company, and Datos, a browsing-data provider](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/), estimated in July 2024 that 58.5% of American Google searches and 59.7% of searches in the European Union ended without a click. Those totals include both ended sessions and revised queries, so they do not show that AI caused the missing clicks. Neither study tells a Swedish or Norwegian company how quickly its own customers will change. Together, they show why website traffic can no longer be the only evidence that the company was considered.

The interface is changing at the same time. A **generative user interface**, often shortened to GenUI, creates a screen for the request at hand instead of selecting a finished page from a fixed library. Google demonstrated request-specific interfaces when it released its Gemini 3 AI model in [November 2025](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/). In [April 2026](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-v0-9-generative-ui/), Google published A2UI v0.9, a format that lets an AI system describe the interface it needs while the receiving application renders approved components. These releases show product direction, not universal adoption, but they make the mechanism concrete: the screen can be assembled after the question is asked.

The next step is action. An **AI agent** is software that works through several steps toward a goal, such as comparing options, collecting information and requesting a booking. At Google Cloud Next, Google's annual cloud-computing conference, the company launched [Agent2Agent, or A2A, in April 2025](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/) with support and contributions from more than 50 technology partners. A protocol is simply an agreed method of communication. A2A lets separately built agents exchange information securely and coordinate work across business applications.

A2A does not make an outside model read your catalog, obey your permissions or gain authority to sign a contract. I don't know when customers will trust agents with high-stakes purchases, because fraud, liability and habit remain real constraints. I think the launch still matters: once interaction and action can happen outside the page, the quality of the underlying company knowledge matters more than the route used to reach it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Search often supplies an answer without a visit, GenUI can assemble the screen around a request, and A2A lets agents coordinate. The adoption timeline is uncertain, but the customer journey is already separating from the company page.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog gives every claim a boundary

[Phil Risher of Phlash Consulting](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo), a digital-marketing firm for local service businesses, offered a useful definition in June 2026: a knowledge catalog is “a verified data set of information about your business.” That is a good starting point, but an enterprise needs more than verified facts. Each fact also needs its source, scope, effective date, owner, approval state and allowed use.

The name now has a narrower product meaning too. [Google Cloud, Google's cloud-computing division, introduced Google Cloud Knowledge Catalog in April 2026](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/data-analytics/introducing-the-google-cloud-knowledge-catalog) to give AI agents governed context about enterprise data and AI assets. I think leaders should treat that product as one possible component, not as the complete business operating model. The company still has to decide which claims are approved and what software may do with them.

Consider a manufacturer that has verified that a certain model no longer contains a restricted chemical. A usable claim record would point to the laboratory report, identify the model and serial-number range, name the markets where the statement applies, carry an effective date and assign an accountable owner. Its approval might allow an assistant to answer an eligible customer's question. The same record should reject a request to issue a regulatory certificate or extend the claim to an older unit.

That record then needs a governed path. The source system supplies the approved version. The catalog checks the identity of the user or application, the market and the allowed action before sending the record to an owned website or assistant. The system logs which version produced the answer.

If the approval is withdrawn, the owner can restore the previous version or require a safe refusal, and the audit record shows what changed. This is where control is real. Once a public AI service creates its own answer outside that path, the company can measure and influence the result but cannot guarantee it.

The catalog should connect existing systems rather than replace them. Product and master-data systems hold shared facts; content-management systems hold publishable explanations; data catalogs show where data lives and who owns it; knowledge graphs describe relationships between facts. The new work is the agreement across them: which source wins, who decides, what context travels with the answer and which actions are forbidden.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog extends beyond a database or one Google Cloud product: it is the governed connection between an approved claim, its evidence and boundaries, and the owned interfaces allowed to use it.</p></div>

## Structured content is the head start

**Structured content** stores a statement as named, reusable fields instead of burying it in one block of page copy. In the manufacturer example, the claim, product model, market, evidence, effective date and approval status remain separate. That structure lets a website, service assistant or internal agent use the same approved record without guessing which sentence contains the rule.

This is different from **web structured data**, also called schema markup, which adds standardized labels to visible page content so search systems can classify it. In [May 2025 guidance for its AI search experiences](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search), Google said its systems consider information shared in machine-readable structured data and asked publishers to create unique, non-commodity content. Both parts matter. Better labels cannot rescue generic or stale knowledge, and valuable knowledge is hard to govern when it remains trapped inside finished pages.

A **headless content management system**, or headless CMS, keeps managed content separate from the website design and makes it available to other channels through software interfaces. I think organizations with mature content models, named owners and reliable integrations have a substantial head start because much of the knowledge is already separated from presentation. That is an architectural judgment, not a measured market advantage. A modern CMS full of duplicates, unclear ownership and outdated claims provides very little advantage.

[*The Digital Singularity Shift*](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/), my 2024 book about the consequences of AI abundance, argues that functional differences become easier to copy and durable value moves toward meaning, story and belief. The book does not name A2A or prescribe a knowledge catalog. Its thesis does explain why this moment matters: when a polished interface can be generated for each request, presentation becomes easier to reproduce, while distinctive knowledge and the trust attached to it remain harder to copy. Structured content is how that value travels without losing its origin.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structured content preserves the meaning and limits of a claim beyond one page. A well-governed headless CMS supplies useful raw material, but the real advantage comes from distinctive knowledge, clear ownership and clean delivery paths.</p></div>

## Prove one answer before buying another platform

The evidence above is not a Nordic enterprise case, and I would not use American click behavior or vendor launches as a forecast for every Nordic business. The responsible response to that gap is a local, reversible test. Choose one product family, one market and one customer question whose wrong answer creates a visible cost, such as a correction, a compliance escalation or an unauthorized offer.

Use a six-week planning envelope. Plan four decision hours for one executive sponsor. An accountable business owner contributes half a day each week; a content lead and an integration lead contribute one day each week; legal and security share roughly twelve review hours. That comes to about 140 internal hours.

My proposed external-fee cap is SEK 500,000 with no new platform license. These figures are decision limits, not industry benchmarks: if one governed answer cannot produce evidence within them, a wider program has not earned approval.

Decision rights should be equally clear. The sponsor owns the budget and the scale-or-stop decision. The business owner approves the claim and resolves conflicts between source systems.

Content and integration leads are responsible for the record and delivery path. Legal or security can veto an unsafe disclosure or action. The implementation partner can build and advise, but it cannot approve the business claim it is paid to move.

Make the evaluation reproducible. Select twenty real customer questions from search or service logs and write two ordinary variations of each. Run every version three times before and after one approved fact change. Keep the prompt set fixed, and record the service, displayed model version, date, market and every output. The model version matters because a provider change can otherwise look like an improvement in your catalog.

Have the business owner grade accuracy and scope against a written answer key, while the risk reviewer grades whether the response stayed inside its allowed use. Preserve the frozen source record, approvals, outputs, grader decisions and rollback evidence under the company's normal audit schedule.

The pass gate should connect technical behavior to a business consequence. My take is to require the changed fact to reach every owned test interface in no more than half the baseline time, produce no unauthorized commitment in the complete test set, reduce correction or review effort, and survive a tested rollback. Public AI services belong in the observation set, not the pass gate, because their models and update cycles sit outside company control.

Keep search rankings, but add two bounded visibility measures for those public services. **Mention rate** is the share of fixed prompts whose answers name the company. **AI share of voice** is the company's share of all brand mentions across a preselected competitor set. Neither measure is a universal standard or proof of revenue. Pair them with a business result such as fewer corrections, fewer escalations or more qualified inquiries, or they will become new vanity metrics.

The consultancy that built the current content platform is a sensible place to start because it already knows the content model and integrations. A global consultancy such as [Valtech](https://www.valtech.com/), or specialists in the partner networks of enterprise content-platform companies [Contentful](https://www.contentful.com/partners/), [Sanity](https://www.sanity.io/agency-partners) and [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/partners/), can supply candidates for a shortlist. These are examples, not endorsements, and a partner badge proves a commercial relationship rather than independence or delivery quality.

I think the deciding question is whether the consultancy can protect your architecture from its own incentives. Require evidence of headless-CMS implementation skill and knowledge governance, full export rights, a declared data location, fees separated from license resale, access controls that limit each user or system to its role, and a tested handover. Ask every bidder to disclose platform investments and sales incentives. Deep platform knowledge is useful, but the records, evaluation set, audit history and right to change suppliers must remain yours.

Start with the answer whose loss of context would cost the business most. Scale when the governed path moves it faster, blocks forbidden uses and lowers the cost of correction. The durable digital asset is approved company knowledge that can travel, be checked and be withdrawn when the interface is no longer yours.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Run a six-week, one-answer pilot with explicit decision rights, a fixed resource cap, repeatable tests and a business outcome beyond visibility. Use an implementation partner for delivery, but keep the knowledge records, audit evidence, export rights and supplier choice under company control.</p></div>
