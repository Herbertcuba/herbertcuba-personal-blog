I tried to remember the last time a company website actually changed a decision I was about to make. I could not. I asked a chatbot, read the AI summary Google served me, and never opened a single page.

Phil Risher, who runs marketing for US home-services companies, put numbers and a name to this shift in a video viewed over 250,000 times. His claim: the website as the hub of your digital presence is dying, replaced by a knowledge catalog, a verified dataset about your company that AI systems consume directly.

I think he is right about the direction. His video stops at the adaptation playbook, though. The interesting question sits one step out: when an agent answers in your company's name, who owns that answer?

<div class="article-tldr">
<span class="article-tldr__label">The takeaway</span>
<p>Search is turning into conversation, and conversation into agents that act for us. Your verified, structured company data becomes the asset agents consume; that data layer is a knowledge catalog, and a headless CMS puts you closer to one than you think. The coming fault line is governance: the agent speaks with your authority, so someone has to sign its answers.</p>
</div>

## What actually changed

The model the commercial web ran on for twenty-five years, search, click, visit, convert, is breaking at both ends.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Most Google searches already end without a click, and AI summaries cut clicking further. Google is moving from keywords to conversations to agents. The new scoreboard is share of voice and mention rate.</p></div>

The first shift is zero-click search. In a 2024 SparkToro and Datos clickstream study, 58.5% of US Google searches ended without any click at all, 59.7% in the EU. Pew Research tracked real browsing in 2025: with an AI summary, people clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits against 15% without, and links cited inside the summary were clicked 1% of the time.

The second shift, agents, dwarfs the first. An agent pursues a goal on your behalf: it reasons, calls tools, checks results, keeps going until the job is done. Risher quotes Google CEO Sundar Pichai calling 2027 the year of agents. What I can verify is close enough: in April 2026 Pichai described Search becoming an "agent manager," and at Cloud Next in April 2025 Google launched A2A, an open protocol letting one company's agent talk to another's, backed by more than 50 partners. ServiceTitan already ships AI assistants that handle booking.

Take a regional plumbing company with forty technicians. Today a homeowner searches, clicks, reads, and calls. In the agentic version, the homeowner's assistant asks the company's agent: do you cover this zip code, what does a water heater replacement cost, when is the earliest slot. The answers come from the company's data. The homepage is never rendered.

## What a knowledge catalog is

Risher defines it as a verified dataset of information about your company, deployed as an agent.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Your company's official answers, structured and verified, served to agents in machine-readable form instead of rendered for browsers. The plumbing has prior art; what is new is the ownership layered on top.</p></div>

Services and service areas, pricing, credentials, policies, real customer questions, all structured and kept current, so the answer an agent gets back is one you would sign off on. The term is Risher's, and I think it is a good one, so I use it rather than pretend Google named a product category.

The plumbing is old: schema.org markup since 2011, llms.txt, and Anthropic's MCP, an open standard connecting AI models to data and tools, are the current exposure mechanisms. A2A connects agents to each other, MCP connects an agent to your data. What is new is the governance: a governed schema.org entry is still just markup, while a knowledge catalog is markup someone is accountable for.

A catalog entry:

```
service: water_heater_replacement
price_usd: 2400
includes_installation: true
valid_from: 2026-07-01
coverage_zip: [98101, 98102, 98103]
owner: j.alvarez@company.com
```

Every field is typed and dated, with a named owner. For the engineers: the consumer is an agent running a ReAct loop, a cycle of reason, act, observe. Frameworks like LangGraph build exactly this; a StateGraph defines the steps, a TypedDict (a Python dictionary where every field has a declared type) defines the data carried between them. The catalog is that loop's ground truth. Risher phrases the reframe sharply: your content is training data for the future internet.

## Why structured content is the key

The catalog only works if the data inside it can be trusted by a machine.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents cannot responsibly act on marketing prose. They need typed, validated fields with owners and timestamps. Structured content is what makes a knowledge catalog possible.</p></div>

A long marketing page about water heater services is fine for a human with patience. For an agent it is a liability. Somewhere in paragraph nine there is a price, but is it current, does it include installation, does it apply to this zip code? The agent has to guess, and agents that guess get things wrong in your name.

Structured content breaks information into typed fields with clear ownership. Price is a number with a currency and a valid-from date. Coverage is a zip-code list maintained by operations. The answer to "what does a replacement cost" comes from a field someone owns.

Google's guidance to site owners says the same: publish unique, non-commodity content and share it "in a machine-readable way." In my experience, organizations underestimate this part. The actual work is editorial: deciding what your official answers are, and giving each one an owner who keeps it true.

## Headless CMS is a head start

If you invested in a modern content platform, you already own most of the raw material.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A headless CMS already stores content as structured, API-served data. That is most of what a knowledge catalog needs. Organizations that made that investment are years ahead.</p></div>

With a headless CMS like Contentful, Sanity, or Optimizely's SaaS platform, your content already lives as structured entries behind an API. The website is one consumer; a knowledge catalog is a second, exposed for agents to query and act on.

I have written before about [the quiet truth about headless CMS](/posts/the-quiet-truth-about-headless-cms/). The frontend flexibility got the attention; the asset that grows in value is the content model underneath, and that model is what this era rewards. Page-centric platforms must retrofit structure onto prose. A clean content model mostly needs exposing and governing.

## This was predictable, and it was predicted

Claiming foresight costs nothing, so the specifics have to carry the weight.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Digital Singularity Shift described interfaces generated on demand, with value moving to the underlying knowledge. Knowledge catalogs are that thesis arriving in practical form.</p></div>

In [The Digital Singularity Shift](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/) I argued we are moving toward generative UI, interfaces assembled in the moment for each user and intent. Google has since shipped generative interfaces in Gemini 3 and published A2UI, an open standard for agent-driven interfaces. If interfaces are generated on demand, the durable asset sits beneath: verified knowledge they draw from. A website is a frozen interface. A knowledge catalog is the layer that survives when interfaces stop being frozen.

Fair warning: the website's death has been predicted before, by mobile apps, then voice search. Those were new channels; an agent sits between the customer and every channel at once, and the platform owner is rebuilding its core product around that fact.

## Who owns the answer

Here is where I part ways with the video.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A deployed catalog answers in your company's name, so answer ownership, knowing who signs every answer, becomes a board-level discipline. My prediction: within two years, liability for agent-answered corporate data becomes the procurement blocker for this category.</p></div>

Everything above describes building the catalog; almost nothing covers running one. An agent that quotes your prices, confirms your coverage, and books your technicians speaks with your authority. When it is wrong, the customer blames you. The model does not take the angry call.

That is why I think the next discipline deserves its own name: answer ownership. Every field has an owner who keeps it true; every answer needs a named human accountable for what the company just said, with verification before deployment and rollback when an answer goes stale. Mänsklig tillsyn, human oversight, as my Swedish colleagues put it. These are board questions about delegated authority; editorial workflows alone will not answer them.

Europe is already forcing the issue. Most EU AI Act obligations apply from August 2026, including transparency duties when people interact with AI. Deploy company data as an agent, and regulators have a framework for asking who was responsible for its answers.

Klarna reported its AI assistant handled two-thirds of customer service conversations in its first month in 2024; in 2025 its CEO said publicly the company had weighted cost too heavily and was hiring humans back for quality. Even the Nordics' most aggressive adopter recalibrated once the answers started carrying the brand.

So the falsifiable version, to be precise: within two years the procurement blocker will be liability, ahead of the technology itself. I could be wrong; if adoption outruns regulation, I will be. Better wrong on record than vaguely right.

## Who should build it

Go to the people who already know your content model.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Start with the consultancy that built your content platform. Firms invested in selling you a specific platform will answer every question with their own product.</p></div>

My take: start with the partner who built your content platform. The Valtechs and the established Contentful, Sanity, and Optimizely partners already know your content model and how your organization changes. A knowledge catalog extends that work: same model, new consumer, stricter verification.

Be wary of platform-invested vendors. If someone's revenue depends on you buying their tool, their answer to "what is a knowledge catalog" will suspiciously resemble whatever they sell. A consultancy starts from your content. A platform vendor starts from its license.

## Where to start

Start small and name the owner on day one.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Pick one high-value domain, structure and verify it, expose it to agents, and measure whether they name you. A first domain is weeks of work. Decide now who owns it when the pilot works.</p></div>

For the plumbing company the start is obvious: pricing and coverage for its top five services. For your organization, pick the domain agents will ask about first. In my experience a first domain is six to ten weeks: one content lead structuring fields, one engineer part-time on exposure, one owner accountable per field. Directional numbers, but defensible in a budget meeting.

Measurement is runnable today. Tools like Otterly.ai and Semrush's AI Visibility Toolkit track how often AI answers mention your brand. A spreadsheet works too: monthly, ask the major assistants your twenty most common customer questions and log whether you are named, and for what.

Decide the production owner before the pilot succeeds. A catalog pilot dies easily as pilot number sixteen with no owner and no budget line. When it works, it lands with whoever owns customer-facing data quality, with a budget attached.

I think early movers here get a durable edge, the same kind early adopters of structured content got when search rewarded it. Every verified answer makes the next one easier to trust.

The website had a good twenty-five year run as the center of digital strategy. The next center is your knowledge, verified and machine-readable, doing its thousands of conversations whether anyone ever visits your homepage or not. The only question is who signs what it says.
