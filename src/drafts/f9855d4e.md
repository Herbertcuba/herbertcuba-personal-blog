Your website will remain useful. It is already losing its privileged role as the place where customers must come for an answer, because search can answer without sending a visit, software can create an interface for one request, and an AI agent can prepare the next step.

I think traffic loss is only the visible effect. The strategic problem is the **answer-control gap**: the distance between an answer your company has approved and the answer a customer receives from a website, search service or AI assistant.

That gap has two parts. Your company can control answers on interfaces it operates, while it can only monitor and influence outside AI services. There is another boundary leaders must protect: approved knowledge tells an agent what may be said, but it never gives the agent permission to spend money, change a contract or make another consequential decision.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The website is becoming one interface among many. Leaders need to govern the approved knowledge beneath those interfaces while separating the answers they control, the external answers they can only influence and the actions that still require authorization.</p></div>

## The answer is leaving the page

The video's widely repeated 58% figure needs a date and a boundary. [SparkToro](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/), an audience-research company, analyzed a large panel of searches on Google, the search and technology company. It estimated that 58.5% of US searches and 59.7% of European Union searches ended without a click in 2024. A **zero-click search** means the person opened no result; the session may have ended with an answer, abandonment or a different query. The figure shows that visits were already optional, not that AI caused every missing click.

The AI-specific evidence is narrower and more useful. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), a nonpartisan US research organization, examined March 2025 browsing activity from 900 American adults. A traditional result received a click in 8% of Google visits with an AI summary and 15% without one, while a source inside the summary received a click in only 1% of visits where a summary appeared. This is an observed association, not proof that the summary alone caused the difference, but it shows how an answer layer can interrupt the old search-to-website journey.

The interface itself is becoming less fixed. A **generative user interface**, or GenUI, is a screen assembled for a particular request instead of a page designed in advance for everyone. Google, the search and technology company, demonstrated this with its Gemini 3 AI model in [November 2025](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/). When software can make the screen at the moment of need, the page becomes one temporary presentation of the knowledge beneath it.

The next step is action. An **AI agent** is software that carries out several steps toward a goal. During Google Cloud Next, its enterprise technology conference, in [April 2025](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/), Google announced the open **Agent2Agent protocol**, or A2A, with support and contributions from more than 50 technology partners. The protocol gives agents built by different organizations a common way to exchange information and coordinate work. It establishes a technical route from answer to action, although it does not prove broad commercial adoption or give an agent business authority.

I don't know how quickly customers will trust agents with expensive, sensitive or regulated decisions. I think the defensible conclusion is narrower: discovery can move from page to answer, and software can begin to move from answer to action. That is enough to weaken the website's claim to be the single digital hub.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Zero-click behavior predates the current agent wave, while independent research shows that AI summaries are associated with fewer result clicks. Generated interfaces and A2A add two real capabilities, but neither broad adoption nor authority to act should be assumed.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog governs the answer

Phil Risher, owner of Phlash Consulting, a marketing firm for local-service businesses, described a **knowledge catalog** in a [June 2026 video](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo) as “a verified data set of information about your business.” Google Cloud, Google's enterprise computing business, introduced [a narrower product with the same name](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/data-analytics/introducing-the-google-cloud-knowledge-catalog) in April 2026 for governed enterprise data and context. No verified Google source defines Risher's broader business model in the same way, so I use the term here for a practical operating capability, not a settled product category.

For a company, that capability is a governed set of approved claims: service facts, pricing rules, policies, credentials and answers. Each claim carries enough context for people and software to know where it came from, where it applies, who owns it and when it must be reviewed. A hypothetical returns record makes the difference concrete.

| Field | Example value |
| --- | --- |
| Identifier and version | `returns-se-consumer`, version 4 |
| Approved claim | Consumer returns are accepted within 30 days |
| Source and recorded origin | Current returns policy, section 3 |
| Owner | Commerce operations |
| Valid scope | Sweden, consumer purchases, public support |
| Effective date, expiry and review date | Recorded with the policy version |
| Validation result | Approved for answers within the stated scope |

A request about Norway or a business contract must be rejected because the claim is outside its valid market, audience and use. The catalog can support an answer about the Swedish policy, but it cannot authorize a refund. An agent may propose that action; an identified user with the right permission, a fixed business rule and a named business owner must authorize the consequential step, while the risk owner retains the power to block it.

Most organizations already hold parts of this knowledge. A content management system, or CMS, manages editorial material; a product information system manages product attributes; a data catalog helps people find datasets; a knowledge graph records relationships; and retrieval software selects relevant material for an AI response. The knowledge catalog adds a shared approval contract across them, so source, scope, ownership, version and review status travel with a claim wherever it is used.

**Structured content** supplies the raw material by storing meaning in named, reusable fields instead of burying it inside finished page copy. Published in a **machine-readable** format, meaning a structure software can reliably parse, those fields can feed a website, service desk, company agent or future interface without rewriting the underlying claim. They can also give outside AI services better evidence, although the company cannot make those services use it or repeat it correctly.

This is why a **headless CMS** matters. It stores content separately from website design and supplies it to different interfaces through software connections called APIs. In [May 2025 guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search), Google Search Central, the company's documentation for website owners, advised publishers to use structured data and create original, useful material rather than information competitors can easily copy. I think an organization with mature content models, named owners and dependable update processes has a substantial head start, but that is an architectural judgment rather than a measured market result. Poorly governed content remains poor input, however modern the CMS may be.

Risher calls content “training data for the future internet.” The phrase is useful if we do not take it too literally, because an AI service may retrieve or cite published material rather than retrain its model on every record. The executive implication survives: content is no longer only copy placed on a page. It is evidence that machines must be able to find, interpret and keep within its approved limits.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog is an operating capability that keeps approved claims connected to source, scope, ownership and review status. Structured content makes those claims reusable, and a mature headless CMS can provide a head start without guaranteeing quality or granting an agent authority to act.</p></div>

## The Digital Singularity shifts the scarce asset

[*The Digital Singularity Shift*](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/), my 2024 book about AI making functional differences easier to copy, argues that meaning, story and belief become the remaining battleground. The current evidence does not prove that every website will disappear. It shows the mechanism extending into the interface through which customers meet a company.

GenUI makes presentation easier to reproduce because software can assemble a useful screen for each request. AI-mediated search chooses which knowledge appears in that screen, while agents may prepare an action from the resulting answer. I think authority is therefore the next fault line: which source deserves trust, where its claim applies and who is accountable when it changes.

A carefully designed website still matters when a person chooses to visit. Its strategic value changes when the same approved claim must survive in search, an assistant, a sales tool and an interface that did not exist when the claim was written. Trusted meaning that can travel without losing its limits becomes scarcer than one perfect frontend.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>As AI makes interfaces easier to generate, verified meaning and accountable authority become harder to reproduce. That is the strategic connection between The Digital Singularity and the knowledge catalog.</p></div>

## Prove the need before buying the platform

The research assembled for this article contains no measured Swedish or Norwegian enterprise case with a published baseline, cost and outcome. US behavior studies and vendor announcements cannot fill that gap. My take is to make the first Nordic pilot produce local evidence rather than turn proposed limits into borrowed benchmarks.

The knowledge-catalog capability should be earned by a failed answer-control test. Start with a one-page charter and use the current content systems as the baseline.

| Charter item | Predeclared decision |
| --- | --- |
| Scope | Six weeks, one policy family, one market and one customer-service interface the company controls |
| Answer test | Run 100 fixed questions, including paraphrases, expired wording, wrong-market requests and requests beyond the user's authority |
| Critical limits | Every answer has an approved source; zero wrong-market, expired or unauthorized answers; approved changes appear within one business day |
| Quality and business measure | Define noncritical defects before the baseline, then track one owned outcome such as correction effort, repeat contacts or policy-related escalations |
| External test | Use 50 fixed questions in two outside AI services selected for documented relevance to the target market and customer journey |
| Economic boundary | No more than 60 internal hours, €25,000 in external fees and no new software license for the first scope |
| Decision and rollback | Scale only if the current setup first misses the agreed limits and the pilot then meets them; otherwise disconnect the pilot path, restore the previous source and retain the evidence |
| Ownership | Name a business owner, delivery owner, scoring owner and risk owner, with the risk owner able to block unsafe use |
| Handover and review | Fund handover before scaling; review claims after source changes and at least quarterly, with the business owner chairing a monthly service review |

Those numbers are proposed decision limits, not market prices or evidence of likely return. The business measure matters because a technically correct answer has limited value if customers still call again, staff spend just as long correcting it or policy-risk exposure remains unchanged. Define the baseline and the person who scores it before the pilot starts, then give disagreements a two-person review rather than letting the delivery team grade its own work.

The external test needs the same discipline because outside AI services change without your permission. Select the two services before the baseline using customer research or market data, not convenience. Run each fixed question twice at every weekly check for 12 weeks, and for every run keep the date, service name, displayed model or version when available, exact question, raw answer, citations and scorer's result. If a service changes materially during the test, mark a break in the series and establish a new baseline instead of silently comparing unlike systems.

Track answer accuracy first. Then add **mention rate**, the percentage of tested answers that name your company, and **AI share of voice**, your share of mentions across a fixed competitor group. These measures can sit beside search rankings, but they do not prove revenue and they do not mean you control the outside service. Predeclare what improvement would justify continued work and publish the question set, scoring rule, failures, internal hours, external cost and final result. That is how a first Nordic pilot becomes evidence another leadership team can inspect.

Begin with the consultancy that already understands your content platform. [Valtech](https://www.valtech.com/), a global business-transformation agency, is one example. The partner directories of headless-CMS providers [Contentful](https://www.contentful.com/partners/) and [Sanity](https://www.sanity.io/agency-partners/), and the solution-partner network around [Optimizely](https://www.optimizely.com/partners/), a digital-experience platform, are other places to look. A partner badge does not establish independence, so require disclosure of license-resale income, full export rights and a written reason for every proposed new platform. Deep product access can be valuable, but it should not quietly turn an evidence test into a license sale.

I think leaders should begin now with an audit of approved knowledge, its owners and its valid uses. Then test whether the systems already in place can preserve those limits when the interface changes. If they can, keep them. If they cannot, build only the knowledge-catalog capability the evidence says you need.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Use a bounded charter to test answer control, business impact and external visibility separately, with preselected services, replayable records, named owners, cost limits and rollback. The future belongs to companies that can prove the authority of an answer before they automate its reach or its action.</p></div>
