Your website is losing its job as the digital hub.

It will still matter for complex offers, transactions and direct relationships. But customers no longer have to enter through the front door you designed. Search engines can answer their questions, and AI agents can begin to act on those answers before a company page appears.

I think many leadership teams still treat the finished website as the asset and the information inside it as material for filling pages. That order is starting to reverse. When interfaces can be generated for each request, the page becomes temporary while the company’s verified knowledge has to survive every interface.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The website is becoming one entrance to the business rather than the required entrance. This makes verified company knowledge more durable than any single design or channel.</p></div>

## Search is moving from a click to an answer and an action

The movement away from website visits began before the current AI wave. In July 2024, [SparkToro, an audience-research company, and Datos, a browsing-data provider](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/), estimated that 58.5% of US Google searches and 59.7% of EU searches ended without a click. Those figures include sessions that ended and queries that were reformulated, so they do not prove that AI caused the behavior or that every searcher found an answer. They show that the old search model already failed to deliver a website visit most of the time.

AI-generated summaries make the next stage easier to measure. [Pew Research Center, a nonpartisan US research organization, studied the Google browsing activity of 900 American adults in March 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/). People clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits when Google displayed an AI summary, compared with 15% when no summary appeared. They clicked a source cited inside the summary in only 1% of visits. Pew also found that about 60% of searches beginning with question words produced a summary, which is consistent with search becoming more like a conversation than a list of keywords.

The next move is from answers to actions. An AI agent is software that receives a goal and permission to carry out several steps for a person or organization. At Google Cloud Next ’25, the company’s annual enterprise technology conference, Google launched [Agent2Agent, or A2A](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/), with support from more than 50 technology partners. A2A is an open set of rules that allows independently built agents to exchange information and coordinate work across business systems. A buying agent could ask a supplier’s agent about availability and terms before preparing an order for approval.

That does not make the agent an all-powerful decision-maker. The protocol helps software communicate, while each company still has to determine what an agent may access and what actions require human approval. I don’t know how quickly people will delegate expensive or sensitive purchases because trust, liability and habit remain real barriers. An agent only needs to compare options, exclude a supplier or arrange the next step, however, to change the journey before a website gets involved.

This also changes what visibility means. Rankings remain useful, but I think leaders should add two measures: mention rate, which tracks how often selected AI answers name the company, and AI share of voice, which compares those mentions with competitors across a fixed set of important questions. These measures are young, and the vendors selling them have an interest in promoting them. A defensible baseline matters more than a flattering count. The business question is whether your company appears accurately when an AI system answers in your category.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Search already produces many journeys without a click, AI summaries reduce clicks further, and A2A lets agents coordinate work across systems. Companies therefore need to be present and accurate inside answers and actions, not only high in a list of links.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog gives company facts an owner

In a June 2026 video, [Phil Risher of the marketing firm Phlash Consulting](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo) uses “knowledge catalog” to describe a verified set of business information that can be deployed through an agent. Available Google documentation does not establish the phrase as an official Google product or standard. I think it is still a useful name, provided we define it plainly rather than borrow authority the term does not yet have.

A knowledge catalog is the company-owned body of approved information arranged so people and software can find a fact without guessing what it means, where it applies or who authorized it. It includes product and service facts, pricing logic, policies, credentials and the questions customers actually ask. Each important claim carries context: an approved source, a business owner, the market or product to which it applies, a review date and a status showing whether it may still be used.

Consider the promise “delivery within 24 hours.” On a conventional website, it may appear in campaign copy, a help article and several country pages with slightly different conditions. In a catalog, the promise is connected to the eligible products and markets, the policy that approves it, the responsible owner and the next review date. If the customer’s market is unknown, the safe response is to ask for it rather than quietly apply a US promise in Sweden.

This difference becomes expensive when the wrong fact travels. A stale sentence on one page can be corrected on that page. The same sentence reused by search summaries, service assistants and outside agents can shape several parts of the customer journey, while nobody knows which version produced the answer. My take is that traceability becomes scarce as interfaces multiply. A company cannot correct misinformation at speed if it cannot identify the source and owner of its own claims.

Risher calls content “training data for the future internet.” The phrase captures the strategic importance of company knowledge, but it can mislead a business reader about the mechanism. Many AI systems will use retrieval, meaning they look up approved information while forming an answer rather than permanently teaching that information to the underlying AI model. Either way, the requirement is similar: the fact must retain its meaning, scope and authority after it leaves the page where it was written.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog is a governed source of company facts rather than another collection of pages. It connects every important claim to its conditions, approved source, owner and current status so an answer can be used, challenged and corrected.</p></div>

## Structured content creates the practical head start

Structured content stores an idea in named parts instead of locking it inside the layout of a finished page. The delivery promise becomes connected information about time, market, eligible product, source policy and review date. Because each part keeps its meaning, one approved record can feed a website, a service tool or an agent without being copied into several conflicting versions.

Google’s own guidance points in this direction. In May 2025, [Google Search Central, the company’s official guidance service for website owners](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search/), said structured data helps organizations share information in a machine-readable form its systems can consider. Machine-readable means software can identify what each part represents instead of inferring meaning from the visual arrangement of a page. The same guidance emphasized original, useful content, because structure cannot make a generic claim distinctive or a stale claim true.

This is why a well-run headless content management system provides a practical head start. A headless system separates reusable content from the design of one website, then supplies it to sites, apps and internal tools through software connections. If a company has already modeled its products, policies and approval flows this way, it owns much of the raw material for a knowledge catalog. If the platform merely stores page-shaped copy, the logo on the software matters far less.

The content-platform market is responding. [Sanity](https://www.sanity.io/blog/agent-actions-ai-building-blocks-for-structured-content) and [Contentful](https://www.contentful.com/blog/remote-mcp-now-generally-available), two vendors of structured-content platforms, have announced tools that connect authorized AI assistants and agents to content workflows. [Optimizely, a digital-experience platform provider](https://www.optimizely.com/agents/competitive-ai-share-of-voice-agent), now offers a tool for measuring visibility in AI answers. These companies sell into the trend, so their product announcements are not neutral evidence of its size. They do show that content platforms are preparing for consumers other than human website visitors.

I think the advantage belongs to organizations that already have reusable content, clear ownership and working review processes, not simply to those that bought a headless system. Structured content is necessary because machines need explicit meaning. Governance makes that meaning trustworthy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structured content lets one approved fact travel without losing its meaning. A mature headless content management system supplies useful raw material, but the real advantage comes from combining that structure with ownership, review and withdrawal.</p></div>

## Generative interfaces confirm the deeper shift

My 2024 book, *[The Digital Singularity Shift](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/)*, argues that AI makes functional digital features easier to reproduce and moves value toward the meaning underneath them. The developments since then make that direction more concrete. AI answers weaken the compulsory click, agents begin to coordinate tasks, and generative user interfaces make the visible layer adaptable.

A generative user interface, often shortened to GenUI, is a screen assembled by AI for the request at hand rather than selected from a fixed path designed in advance. In November 2025, [Google’s Gemini 3 AI model introduced experiments that generated a custom interface from a user’s prompt](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/). In April 2026, Google released version 0.9 of [A2UI, an open proposal for agent-driven interfaces](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-v0-9-generative-ui/). It lets an agent request suitable interface elements while the receiving application controls how those elements look and behave.

Think of the holodeck in *Star Trek: The Next Generation*, the science-fiction television series launched in 1987. The holodeck is a fictional room that renders a responsive simulated environment around its users. GenUI is a modest business version: one customer may receive a comparison, another a form, with each interface assembled around the immediate task. When the scenery can change on demand, the quality of the underlying world becomes decisive.

Design does not stop mattering. Trust, accessibility and brand expression still shape whether people use an interface. I think the strategic hierarchy changes, though: the front end becomes one temporary expression of the business, while the verified knowledge underneath it has to work across many expressions.

The consequence is testable. As AI-mediated journeys grow, companies with unowned or untraceable claims should expect two problems to appear together: weaker control over how they are represented and slower correction when an AI repeats obsolete commercial information. The first serious failure may not be a broken page. It may be a polished answer built from a promise the business stopped making months ago.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>GenUI turns the interface into an adaptable layer, matching the direction described in <em>The Digital Singularity Shift</em>. The screen may change for every request, but the company facts beneath it still need stable meaning, ownership and authority.</p></div>

## Start with ten claims, not another redesign

The same consultancy that modeled and connected your current content may be well placed to extend it into a knowledge catalog. A global digital consultancy such as Valtech, or an implementation partner in the Contentful, Sanity or Optimizely ecosystem, can bring relevant content-modeling and integration skills. The category label is not enough. Choose a consultancy whose advice is not tied to selling more licenses on one platform, and make that independence visible in the commercial agreement.

The selection questions are straightforward. Who owns the content model and the resulting data? Can the company export everything in a usable form, and where will the data reside? How are fees separated from platform incentives? Which people and agents may see or change each class of information? Who maintains the governance process after launch, and can the supplier prove that handover and exit have been tested? A2A coordination should never be treated as permission to expose the whole catalog. Agent access needs to be limited to the minimum required for the task and easy to withdraw.

Do not begin with a company-wide transformation. Run a 30-day discovery exercise around one high-volume, high-risk set of facts, such as delivery promises, return policies or product eligibility. Select ten claims and record the approved wording, applicable market, source policy, business owner, review date, current status and withdrawal path. Then find where those claims have been copied, and identify at least one stale, conflicting or unowned version. If the team cannot complete that exercise, the first investment belongs in knowledge governance rather than another layer of AI.

The initial scorecard should be equally small. Track how many of the ten claims have an approved source and owner, how many are current, whether test questions return the right answer for the right market, and whether a withdrawn fact disappears from every connected channel. Add rankings, AI mention rate and share of voice as visibility signals, but do not let them become vanity numbers detached from answer quality.

That gives the board a reversible decision rather than a speculative technology program. The company learns whether its content platform provides a genuine head start, where authority is missing and what a larger catalog would require before committing to a platform or supplier. I think this is the investment choice leadership teams should make now: establish control over the facts every interface will reuse before paying to redesign one more interface.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Begin with a 30-day audit of ten valuable, risky claims and make their sources, owners, scope and withdrawal paths explicit. Choose an implementation partner around independence, export rights, controlled access and tested handover, then expand only when the pilot proves that the company can keep its answers accurate.</p></div>
