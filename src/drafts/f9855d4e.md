Your website is losing its job as the center of your digital business.

It will remain a valuable place to explain complex offers, express the brand and build a direct relationship with customers. What is fading is the compulsory journey through search result, click and company page. More decisions now begin inside an AI answer, and the next wave of systems will be able to carry parts of those decisions into action.

I think many companies are still investing as if the page were the permanent asset. The page is becoming a temporary presentation layer. The more durable asset is the knowledge underneath it: the facts, rules and evidence that any trustworthy interface needs.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The website will survive, but its position as the required doorway into a business is weakening. Company knowledge can remain useful even when discovery, comparison and action happen somewhere else.</p></div>

## Discovery is moving before the click

The shift did not begin with AI. A [2024 study by marketing analytics company SparkToro and browsing-data provider Datos](https://sparktoro.com/blog/2024-zero-click-search-study/) estimated that 58.5% of US Google searches and 59.7% of EU searches ended without a click. Those totals include people who stopped searching or tried another query, so they do not show that AI caused the behavior. They do show that a website visit was already far from certain.

AI-generated summaries make the new pattern easier to measure. In March 2025, [Pew Research Center, a nonpartisan US research organization, studied the Google browsing activity of 900 American adults](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/). People clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits when Google displayed an AI summary, compared with 15% when no summary appeared. They clicked a source cited inside the summary in only 1% of visits. Pew also found that roughly 60% of searches beginning with words such as who, what, when and why produced an AI summary, which means conversational questions are especially likely to be answered before the click.

The next change is from answers to delegated tasks. An AI agent is software given a goal and permission to complete several steps on a person's or company's behalf. At Google Cloud Next '25, Google's annual enterprise technology conference, the company introduced [Agent2Agent, or A2A](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/a2a-a-new-era-of-agent-interoperability/), an open protocol that lets agents exchange information and coordinate work across different business systems. A2A creates a common language for coordination; each company still decides what an agent may access, say or do.

I don't know how quickly people will hand agents major purchases. Trust, liability, payments and habit remain real barriers. Yet an agent does not need to complete the whole transaction to change the market. Once it can compare suppliers, reject an ineligible option or arrange the next step, the company website is no longer the only place where commercial progress happens.

Measurement has to follow that change. [Semrush, a search analytics company, analyzed 200,000 keywords collected in September 2024](https://www.semrush.com/blog/ai-overviews-study/) and found that a top-ten traditional ranking did not guarantee inclusion in Google's AI Overview. Rankings still matter, but I think leaders should also track mention rate, meaning how often AI answers name the company, and AI share of voice, meaning its presence relative to competitors. The practical question is no longer only “Where do we rank?” It is also “Are we present in the answer the customer receives?”

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Search already produces many journeys without a website visit, AI summaries reduce clicks further, and agents can move a task forward across business systems. Visibility must therefore be understood inside the answer and the action, not only in a list of links.</p></div>

## A knowledge catalog makes company facts usable

In a June 2026 video, [Phil Risher, a marketing consultant at Phlash Consulting](https://youtu.be/xAIeA3ewRbo), describes a knowledge catalog as a verified set of information about a business that can be deployed through an agent. The phrase is useful, although it is not the name of an official Google product or technical standard. I use it for a company-owned body of knowledge that software can read without guessing what each fact means.

That catalog could contain services, product limits, pricing rules, locations, credentials, policies and the questions customers repeatedly ask. “Verified” means the business can trace an important statement to an approved source, identify who owns it and know when it needs review. “Machine-readable” means the statement has consistent labels and fields instead of depending on the visual arrangement of one web page.

Consider a warranty. A page might bury its duration, eligible products and regional exceptions in several paragraphs. In a knowledge catalog, those parts remain connected but distinct, along with the approved policy and review date. The website can turn the record into polished prose, while a service tool or agent can answer a narrow question without silently dropping a condition.

This matters because the first serious failure, I think, will be a public AI answer repeating an outdated price or policy faster than the business can trace and correct it. That distance between an answer and an accountable source is the verification gap. Risher calls content “training data for the future internet,” which captures the strategic importance even though the immediate mechanism will often be retrieval: the AI looks up relevant company information while forming an answer rather than learning it permanently through model training.

A catalog does not make every claim true by magic. It gives the organization a way to prove, update and withdraw the claims it is prepared to stand behind. That is what turns content from page copy into business infrastructure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A knowledge catalog is verified, company-owned information arranged so people and software can use it consistently. Its business value lies in closing the gap between what an AI system says and what the organization can prove, update or withdraw.</p></div>

## Structured content creates the head start

Structured content stores an idea in named parts rather than treating a finished page as one block. A service record might separate its name, eligible region, price rule and review date. Because those parts retain their meaning, the same approved record can support a website, an employee tool and an agent without being copied into three conflicting versions.

This direction appears in Google's own guidance. In May 2025, [Google Search Central, the company's official service for website owners](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/05/succeeding-in-ai-search/), said structured data helps companies share information in a machine-readable form its systems can consider. The same guidance emphasized original, useful material. Structure makes knowledge easier for machines to handle, but it cannot make a generic statement distinctive or an old statement accurate.

Organizations with a well-run headless content management system have a practical head start. A headless CMS separates reusable content from the design of a single website and supplies it to different channels through software connections. If a company already models product facts, manages approval rights and publishes one record in several places, much of the raw material exists. A modern platform filled with unstructured page copy offers no such advantage.

The consultancy that built the content platform may be well placed to extend this work because it already understands the content model and publishing process. My take is to choose for capability and incentives rather than platform loyalty. The company should own the knowledge model, be able to move its information elsewhere and receive advice that would remain sound if the software changed. Product expertise is valuable, but the knowledge catalog cannot become an excuse to make one license permanent.

I think this is a stronger near-term investment than another broad redesign. A redesign improves one interface. Verified structured knowledge can improve that interface while also serving search, customer service, employee tools and agents.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structured content allows one approved fact to travel without losing its meaning. A mature headless CMS and a capable consultancy can accelerate the shift, provided the business retains ownership, portability and freedom from one platform's incentives.</p></div>

## Generative UI reveals what endures

*[The Digital Singularity Shift](/books/the-digital-singularity-shift/)*, a 2024 business book about AI making digital creation abundant, argues that functional features become easier to reproduce and value moves toward the meaning beneath them. I think AI answers and agents now make that argument concrete. Generative user interface, often shortened to GenUI, adds the final piece: the screen itself can be assembled for a particular request instead of designed in advance for every visitor.

In November 2025, [Google introduced Gemini 3 experiments that generated an interface when a user submitted a prompt](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/gemini-3-gemini-app/). In April 2026, the company published [A2UI](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-v0-9-generative-ui/), a proposed open standard through which an agent can request suitable interface elements while an application uses its own approved design components. These releases do not make design irrelevant. They show that the form can increasingly adapt to the moment while the underlying facts and permissions remain stable.

The useful science-fiction comparison is the holodeck in *Star Trek: First Contact*, the 1996 film in which a computer creates a setting around the immediate mission. GenUI points toward a modest business version of that idea: one customer receives a comparison, another a form and a third a dashboard, each assembled around the task at hand. When the scenery can change on demand, investing only in the scenery makes less sense.

The website will remain one important expression of the company, but it will no longer carry the whole digital strategy. So make the knowledge catalog an investment priority now, before generated interfaces turn today's carefully designed homepage into one temporary view among thousands.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>GenUI makes interfaces more adaptive and temporary, matching the direction described in <em>The Digital Singularity Shift</em>. The durable investment is the verified knowledge beneath those screens, and business leaders should make that catalog a priority now.</p></div>
