<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Lance Martin's two-hour workshop on agent graphs spends 15 minutes drawing the graph and 105 minutes on evaluation. That ratio is the whole lesson. A graph without an eval set is a diagram with opinions.</p></div>

I tried to count how many agent architecture diagrams I have seen this year. I lost track somewhere around forty. Boxes, arrows, a loop back to the LLM, maybe a little database cylinder in the corner. They all look the same, and they all get the same reaction: screenshot, repost, "this is the future."

Last week one of those diagrams went properly viral. Lance Martin, who works at Anthropic and used to be at LangChain, gave a free two-hour workshop on agent graphs at the AI Engineer World's Fair. A hype account clipped the spicy part: most Anthropic engineers are supposedly running self-improving loops, and in a few months we will all be building graphs instead of writing prompts. Half a million views. Thousands of reposts. Everyone screenshot the graph.

Then I read a second post about the same workshop, from an engineer named Jey who apparently watched the whole thing. His observation was different. The graph diagram is done in the first 15 minutes. The remaining 105 minutes keep returning to one topic: evaluation. The workshop ends with building eval datasets for the graph.

Fifteen minutes on the drawing. One hundred and five on the proof.

I think that ratio is the most honest thing anyone has said about agent engineering this year.

## What the graph actually is

The graph is the easy part, and it is worth understanding anyway.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An agent graph is a flowchart your code can execute. Nodes are steps, edges are decisions, state is the shared notebook every step reads and writes. Drawing one takes an afternoon.</p></div>

Quick grounding, because the jargon gets thrown around loose. A basic LLM call is one shot: prompt in, text out. A ReAct loop (reasoning plus acting) lets the model think, call a tool, look at the result, and think again, in a loop, until it decides it is done. That works for simple tasks. It falls apart when the task has real structure, because the model is juggling the plan, the progress, and the next step all inside one context window.

A graph pulls that structure out of the model's head and puts it in your code. In LangGraph (the library Lance helped build, and the one the workshop uses) you define three things. Nodes: functions that do one step, like "search the docs" or "draft the answer." Edges: the rules for which node runs next, sometimes a fixed line, sometimes a decision the LLM makes. State: a shared object, usually defined as a TypedDict (a Python dict where you declare the exact keys and their types up front), that every node reads from and writes to. You wire it together in a StateGraph, which is the container that holds your nodes, your edges, and your state definition.

Say you are building a support bot that answers questions about your product docs. The graph version looks like this: a classify node reads the question and decides if it needs the docs at all. A retrieve node searches the docs. An answer node writes the reply. A check node decides if the reply is good enough or loops back to retrieve more. That is the whole architecture. Four boxes, a few arrows. You could draw it on a napkin, and I think that is exactly why it spreads so fast on X.

An afternoon of work, honestly. Maybe two if it is your first time.

## Where the 105 minutes go

The graph runs. Then it meets real users, and the afternoon is over.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Real inputs are nothing like your test inputs. Evaluation is how you find out which node breaks first, and building the eval dataset is most of the actual job.</p></div>

Your support bot works beautifully on the ten questions you tried while building it. Then a real customer writes "it broke again lol" with no product name, no error, no context. Which node handles that? A customer pastes in 4,000 words of logs. A customer asks in Spanish when your docs are in English. A customer asks two questions in one message and one of them is a complaint about billing that should go to a human.

Every one of those cases is a decision your graph makes, and every decision can be wrong. The classify node misroutes. The retrieve node pulls the wrong doc. The check node waves a bad answer through. The painful part is that you cannot see any of this from the diagram. The diagram looks identical whether it works or not.

This is what evaluation means in practice. You build a dataset of real-ish inputs, including the ugly ones, and you write down what good output looks like for each. Then you run the graph against it and score every node. Not once. Every time you change anything. When the workshop spends 105 minutes here, that is the time going into: which inputs do I test, what does "correct" mean for this node, how do I score an answer that is half right, how do I catch the retrieval node quietly getting worse when I tweak the answer prompt.

Jey's post had the sharpest version of this I have seen: which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself?

Sit with your own architecture for a second and answer that honestly. For most of us, including me, the answer is uncomfortable.

## The diagram with opinions

A graph you have not evaluated is a bet you have not checked.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Drawing the graph is a day. Proving it holds on inputs you did not choose is the job. The eval set is what turns your architecture from a guess into an instrument.</p></div>

There is a scene in The Matrix where Neo finally sees the code behind the world instead of the world. Everyone wants to be Neo. The boring truth of agent engineering is closer to the other side of that movie: the machines run the simulation, and the humans who survive are the ones who built instruments to measure what the machines are actually doing. The graph is the world you drew. The eval set is the instrument panel. Without it you are flying on vibes.

I think this is also the right way to read the viral claim. "No more prompting" is the new "no more code." Take it seriously and deflate it at the same time. Yes, the center of gravity is moving from crafting one perfect prompt to orchestrating a system. But the work that replaces prompting is not drawing boxes. It is the unglamorous craft of deciding what correct looks like, for inputs you did not pick, and proving your system gets there. The prompt did not disappear. It got demoted to one node among many, and the eval set became the boss of all of them.

This connects to something I have written about before with typed state. The graph is the control plane: it decides what runs and when. The types and the evals are the trust plane: they decide whether you can believe what ran. A control plane without a trust plane is just motion.

I don't know how fast the self-improving loops actually spread outside the frontier labs. The 70% number is a paraphrase of a paraphrase, and even if Lance said something close to it, what Anthropic's engineers do with Anthropic's tooling tells you what the tools will assume in a year. It says very little about what your team should do this quarter.

## What to do on Monday

The actionable version is small and unsexy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Before you redraw your architecture, write twenty inputs you did not choose, define what a good answer is for each, and make your graph survive them. That dataset is the asset.</p></div>

If you have an agent in production, or on the way, my take is simple. Open a spreadsheet. Write down twenty real inputs, from logs, from support tickets, from that one user who types like a detective novel. For each one, write what a good outcome looks like. Run your graph against all twenty. Score it by hand if you have to.

You will find a node that fails in a way that surprises you. That surprise is the most valuable artifact in your whole system, because it is the first piece of an eval set that tells the truth.

The graph you drew is a hypothesis. The eval set is the experiment. Everyone has a hypothesis. Almost nobody runs the experiment.

Drawing a graph is an afternoon. Proving it holds on inputs you did not choose is the job.
