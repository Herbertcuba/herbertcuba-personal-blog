# The 105 minutes after the graph

“Which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself?” [Jey asked on X in July 2026](https://x.com/0xJeyx/status/2081364222856831271) while commenting on a recorded workshop. It is the right question because an agent graph is only a map of a multi-step AI workflow: nodes perform jobs, edges choose what can happen next, and shared state carries information between them. An evaluation set is the harder counterpart, a collection of reviewed inputs with success criteria against which the workflow can be tested.

Jey framed the recording as 15 minutes for the graph and 105 minutes for evaluation. That ratio is an interpretation, not the official running order. The [full recording runs for 2 hours and 21 minutes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ib-wTAvCZqg), introduces testing and evaluation around 15:36, then keeps returning to how the system behaves. The official description promises testing at runtime, before release, and in production monitoring. The ratio still works because it captures where the intellectual weight sits.

The source story needs an evaluation of its own. Lance Martin, an AI engineer now at Anthropic, delivered *Architecting and Testing Controllable Agents* at the [2024 AI Engineer World’s Fair](https://www.ai.engineer/worldsfair/2024/schedule), a developer conference, while working at LangChain. LangChain makes LangGraph, a framework for building stateful agent workflows. [Movez’s viral post from July 2026](https://x.com/0xMovez/status/2081011657807925604) attributed “70%,” “3–6 months,” and “No more prompting” to an Anthropic engineer, but those phrases do not appear in the workshop’s English captions. [Martin also clarified that the talk predates his Anthropic role](https://x.com/RLanceMartin/status/2081408482344149047).

I think the correction improves the lesson. The graph is the control plane: it determines which work can happen and in what order. The trust plane consists of typed state, meaning a shared record with explicit fields and expected values, plus evaluations and release rules that test and constrain those routes. A graph without an evaluation set is a diagram with opinions. Drawing it is the easy part; proving it on cases you did not select is the job.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The 15/105 ratio is a lens, not a stopwatch reading. Graph structure is quick to see, while unfamiliar cases reveal whether its routes, limits, and claims can survive production.</p></div>

## Fine-tuning the graph means changing its routes

By “fine-tuning the graph,” I mean changing its state fields, routes, retry limits, reflection criteria, and human gates as failures appear. I do not mean training the model’s weights. An edge is one allowed handoff between nodes; a route is the sequence of handoffs taken for a particular case. The complexity arrives when messy inputs meet those tidy arrows.

Consider Apex, a hypothetical refund-review agent:

```text
customer request
       ↓
check payment ── pending ──→ explain pending status
       │
    settled
       ↓
check policy ── unclear ──→ human policy review
       │
     clear
       ↓
draft decision → reflection → human approval
                    │
             revise once / escalate
```

Apex needs the reflection phase because a fluent draft can still contradict the payment record, ignore policy evidence, or claim authority the workflow never granted. Reflection is a separate critique node: it checks named conditions, permits one revision, and escalates if the revised answer still fails. A prompt telling the drafting node to “double-check” hides judgment inside the same step and gives the loop no inspectable exit.

This separation also stops judgment from quietly becoming authorization. HAL 9000, the shipboard AI in Stanley Kubrick’s 1968 film *2001: A Space Odyssey*, is useful here because the disaster comes from concentrating observation, judgment, and control in one opaque system. Apex may use a model to critique a draft, but deterministic checks and human approval keep consequential authority outside that loop.

The state schema makes those boundaries visible, while runtime checks enforce the rules that matter:

| State field | Allowed values | Release-blocking invariant |
|---|---|---|
| `payment_status` | `pending`, `settled` | `pending` can never reach refund approval |
| `policy_status` | `clear`, `unclear` | `unclear` must reach human policy review |
| `reflection_result` | `pass`, `revise`, `escalate` | `revise` may loop once; `pass` still requires human approval |

I think this is where the 105 minutes live. Every failure creates a concrete design decision: what Apex may know, where it may go, how often it may try, and who can stop it. The diagram barely changes. Its meaning becomes much more precise.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Fine-tuning a graph means refining state and routes rather than model weights. Bounded reflection can improve one candidate answer, but invariants and human gates decide what the loop is allowed to do.</p></div>

## Turn two routes into release evidence

A release contract is the preset collection of checks, thresholds, and accountable owners that decides whether a graph version ships or rolls back. It starts with held-back cases: reviewed examples kept away from graph changes until release evaluation. For each run, a trace records the nodes, tool calls, and results, while a grader uses code, a model rubric, or human judgment to compare that record and its outcome with expectations written in advance.

For Apex, two cases already expose different failure shapes:

| Held-back input | Expected route | Observed failure | What the grader checks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payment pending; customer asks for a refund | payment check → pending-status reply | draft decision → refund approval | forbidden transition, final action, payment-tool result |
| Payment settled; policy evidence conflicts | policy check → human policy review | draft decision → reflection pass | route, cited evidence, escalation reason, final authority |

The second case matters because choosing the correct next node is not enough. Route accuracy can conceal a false tool result, an unsupported final decision, repeated retries, or unacceptable latency. I think the release record should cover the path, the environmental outcome, and the resources consumed. Cost per successful task makes the last part concrete: total model and tool cost divided by cases that pass every required check.

[Anthropic’s January 2026 guide to agent evaluations](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) recommends starting with 20–50 simple tasks drawn from real failures. That is a practical first set, not a permanent sample size. More mature systems need harder cases as improvements become smaller and failures become less obvious.

Ownership should be equally concrete:

| Owner | First-release responsibility |
|---|---|
| Product or policy owner | defines expected outcomes and resolves disputed cases |
| Engineering | maintains the runner, trace format, graders, and cost records |
| Release owner | applies thresholds and orders rollback after a forbidden route or failed outcome |

A shared platform can standardize trace storage, evaluation runners, and regression checks after a model or vendor change. It cannot decide what a correct refund decision means; that remains with the workflow’s product or policy owner. The thresholds should also follow risk. An internal drafting assistant can tolerate a failure profile that a money-moving workflow cannot.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A route becomes release evidence when held-back cases test its path, outcome, evidence, and cost against prior expectations, with named owners responsible for policy, engineering, approval, and rollback.</p></div>

## Let failures redraw the graph

Reflection and evaluation work at different scales. Reflection critiques one candidate run before it proceeds. Evaluation freezes a graph version, exercises it across reviewed cases, and informs the release decision. Durable execution, a framework’s ability to save workflow state and resume after interruption, solves a third problem: recovery preserves where a run was, while evaluation determines whether the recovered route and result were good enough.

```text
real failure → reviewed expectation → development set → graph change
                                              ↓
                                         freeze version
                                              ↓
fresh held-back cases → release evaluation → ship or rollback
```

A development set contains cases engineers may inspect while changing the graph. When a held-back failure causes a route change, move that case into development and add a fresh held-back case for the next release. Otherwise the evaluation set slowly turns into another prompt written to flatter the system.

I don’t know which graph framework will dominate. “No more prompting” is catchy in the same way “no more code” is catchy: adding a new layer does not erase the one underneath it. Prompts remain inside nodes; graphs add explicit state and routing. Even a verified internal-adoption percentage would forecast what tooling may assume in twelve months, not tell your organization what to deploy this quarter. An unsupported percentage should carry even less authority.

My take is that the durable asset will be the evaluation corpus: the accumulated reviewed cases, policy decisions, traces, grader logic, and release history. Tooling may converge faster than those expectations do, and switching graph frameworks will usually be easier than reconstructing why a disputed route was approved or forbidden. I think that is the deeper lesson in the ratio. The graph shows how an agent might work; the evidence determines where it may be trusted.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Reflection improves a candidate run, durable execution preserves it, and held-back evaluation governs the system. Failures should redraw the graph while the reviewed evidence becomes the asset that survives any framework.</p></div>
