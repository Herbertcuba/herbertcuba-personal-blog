# The 105 minutes after the graph

“Which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself?” [Jey asks](https://x.com/0xJeyx/status/2081364222856831271). His post frames Lance Martin’s workshop as a 15/105 split: fifteen minutes to draw the graph, then 105 minutes returning to evaluation. The ratio is a diagnostic metaphor. It captures the deeper allocation between a quick diagram and prolonged evidence.

The [official recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ib-wTAvCZqg) runs about 2 hours 22 minutes and comes from the 2024 AI Engineer World’s Fair, when the [schedule](https://www.ai.engineer/worldsfair/2024/schedule) listed Martin with LangChain. Testing enters the auto-generated captions around 15:36; evaluation datasets return in the Q&A around 1:42:00. Those timestamps support the arc, although the workshop used no literal 15/105 agenda.

A [separate viral post from Movez](https://x.com/0xMovez/status/2081011657807925604) attributes “70%,” “3-6 months,” and “No more prompting” to an Anthropic engineer. YouTube’s current English auto-generated caption track contains no matches for that figure or those phrases, although automatic captions can miss speech. [Martin clarified](https://x.com/RLanceMartin/status/2081408482344149047) that the workshop predates his Anthropic role. This recording therefore cannot support the viral attribution.

I think that correction strengthens the useful argument. In a graph, a node performs one step, an edge permits a handoff, and a route is the edge selected for a particular case. The graph is the control plane because it chooses the next step. The trust plane is the evidence and release process that decides whether those choices may reach customers. Jey compresses the gap well: “A graph without an eval set is a diagram with opinions.”

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The 15/105 split is a diagnostic: drawing routes is quick, while unfamiliar cases supply the evidence that gives those routes production authority.</p></div>

## Test the transition that carries risk

Consider a hypothetical refund graph. Its typed state, a shared record with fixed fields and allowed values, says whether payment is complete and review is required. A pending payment leads to `payment_review`; a direct handoff to `refund_approval` is rejected before money moves. This transition is the consequential unit of proof.

A held-back evaluation case stays outside graph design and tuning until the candidate is fixed. The policy owner specifies its expected next node before the run. Route accuracy is the share of cases sent to that pre-specified destination. I think typed state earns its cost here: a policy sentence becomes a checkable route, tested against an unfamiliar input.

A separate review node adds latency and model calls, so held-back cases must show a measurable routing gain within the run’s cost budget. Letting one model propose a refund and certify the decision resembles HAL 9000 piloting the ship while signing its own safety report. I think separation helps only when independent evidence proves the benefit.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Typed state makes a risky handoff enforceable, while held-back cases show whether an added review step improves routing enough to justify its cost.</p></div>

## Give the eval set authority

[Anthropic recommends](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) starting with 20 to 50 simple tasks drawn from real failures. A team can begin with one consequential customer journey instead of applying the same process to every internal assistant. Support supplies reviewed failures, then the policy owner settles disputed expected outcomes before testing. Engineering maintains the test harness, while the release owner sets a threshold based on the cost of a wrong route.

The dataset maintainer freezes the held-back set for each release cycle. Once a case influences graph design, it moves into the development set because it can no longer provide clean evidence about unfamiliar inputs. A reviewed replacement enters the next held-back version. This is [ordinary test-set hygiene](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/common_pitfalls.html#data-leakage) applied to an agent workflow, with controlled mutation rights instead of a shared folder anyone can improve during the run.

A grader is the check that scores a result. In the refund example, code can verify the selected route, while a written rubric can guide human review when policy language leaves room for judgment. The release record identifies the candidate model and prompt, links them to the applicable policy and frozen dataset, and preserves the threshold result. I think a consequential graph becomes production-ready only when a named approver can sign that record or block the release. Lower-risk workflows may combine roles; decisions involving money or rights deserve separate policy and approval owners so an audit can reconstruct and reverse the choice.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A held-back set becomes release evidence when its expected outcomes are pre-specified, its contents stay frozen during testing, and an accountable approver can act on the result.</p></div>

## Tooling will converge before evidence does

Graph frameworks already provide routing and [persistent state that can resume interrupted runs](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/durable-execution). I think those mechanics will become easier to copy over the next year. A framework cannot manufacture a representative history of your workflow’s failures, since that evidence grows from live cases and policy changes inside the organization.

The prediction is testable. After a model or policy change, teams with versioned, failure-derived held-back sets should show a lower route-regression rate than teams relying on developer-written examples. Route-regression rate means the share of previously correct audit cases now sent to the wrong node. If the advantage fails to appear on an independent audit set, the evaluation process needs revision rather than ceremonial protection.

“No more prompting” deserves the same serious deflation as “no more code.” Prompts remain inside nodes; graphs add state and route decisions around them. I don’t know which framework will dominate, and the unsupported 70% figure cannot decide what an organization should ship this quarter. My take is that tooling assumptions will change faster than the burden of proof.

Drawing a graph is an afternoon. Proving it holds on inputs you did not choose is the job.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Frameworks can standardize graph mechanics, but stable releases will belong to teams whose failure-derived evidence can stop a weak route from shipping.</p></div>
