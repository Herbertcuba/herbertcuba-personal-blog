A security-incident assistant receives an alert, two weeks of logs, the company handbook and the whole chat history. Every model call then receives that bundle again, although nobody has decided which step needs which evidence.

That is the subtle art of context engineering. A token is a piece of text processed by a language model, and a [context window](https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/context-windows) is the finite space available for a call's input and output. The target is the minimum sufficient context for each decision, plus evidence that the omissions do not change the result.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Token efficiency starts by assigning evidence to decisions instead of sending one comprehensive bundle to every model call.</p></div>

## Three layers, three different jobs

Prompt engineering improves instructions inside one call. Context engineering selects the instructions, evidence, tool results and prior state that call can see. Graph engineering decides how several calls and tools divide the work.

| Layer | Design question | Incident example |
|---|---|---|
| Prompt | How should this job be done? | Classify the alert against a severity rubric. |
| Context | What does this job need to see? | The alert, relevant identity events and that rubric. |
| Graph | What happens before and after? | Retrieve evidence, investigate, challenge, then route. |

In an [August 2026 video](https://youtu.be/JWhICz1QR8M), technology entrepreneur Greg Isenberg presents graph engineering as designing work around AI rather than stretching one conversation across an entire process. He separates a **knowledge graph**, which records entities and relationships, from an **agent graph**, which moves work between jobs. One connects an account to devices and privileges; the other decides what investigates those facts next.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Prompts shape a job, context supplies its evidence, and an agent graph controls how that job connects to the workflow.</p></div>

## Make the workflow visible

Consider a hypothetical assistant investigating a suspicious administrator login:

```text
intake → classify → retrieve evidence
                       ↙       ↘
              identity check   log check
                       ↘       ↙
                         merge → skeptic → gate
```

A **node** performs one job. An **edge** is an allowed connection, while a **transition** is one actual move along it. **Shared state** is the structured incident record passed between nodes. **Conditional routing** reads that state and chooses the next edge, perhaps closing a harmless alert or escalating a credible one.

Intake, classification and retrieval run sequentially because each depends on the previous result. The checks can fan out in parallel and merge, forming the split-and-rejoin **diamond topology**. Each branch receives its own evidence slice. A checker verifies required fields; a skeptic tries to disprove the proposed conclusion. A human gate belongs before disabling an account because review should rise with the cost of a wrong action.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The graph saves context by separating dependent steps, parallel checks and risky actions, while shared state carries what later decisions require.</p></div>

## Earn the graph gradually

Isenberg's practical ladder starts with people running the workflow manually, then exposes handoffs as file artifacts, and only then orchestrates stable parts. This reveals what each step consumes and produces before a framework hides the handoff. The smallest useful graph may be one investigator, one skeptic and one human gate.

Token control then becomes concrete:

- retrieve the relevant rule or log range on demand;
- pass structured state instead of replaying the transcript;
- keep source artifacts by reference rather than copying them into every branch;
- use [prompt caching](https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-caching) for unchanged instruction prefixes where supported.

**Persistent state** preserves the current incident across pauses and retries. **Memory** carries selected facts across incidents, so it needs a stricter admission rule. This is why [APEX, Herbert's Agentic Production Execution framework](/posts/2026-04-05-apex-framework/), has a reflection phase: compare the merged conclusion with the goal and evidence before granting the graph more authority.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Manual runs and explicit artifacts reveal the context boundaries; orchestration, persistence and reflection should follow once those boundaries are understood.</p></div>

## Test what you removed

Isenberg makes the practitioner case for workflow design, but his video contains no eval discussion. That bridge comes from separate evidence. In a 2024 AI Engineer World's Fair workshop lasting about two hours and 22 minutes, Lance Martin, then at LangChain and later at Anthropic, introduced agent graphs early and returned repeatedly to evaluation. A transcript audit found 101 uses of “eval”; Jey's [July 2026 post](https://x.com/0xJeyx/status/2081364222856831271) framed the imbalance as roughly 15 minutes of graph and 105 minutes of eval work.

A checker reviews one run. An **eval set** repeats that separation across representative and difficult inputs, especially cases the builders did not choose for a demo. [Anthropic's guidance](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) suggests starting with 20 to 50 tasks from real failures. They reveal whether shorter context dropped decisive evidence, routing chose the wrong branch, or the human gate arrived too late.

Here, graph tuning means adjusting route thresholds, context slices, merge rules, retries and stop conditions. A viral July 2026 paraphrase by Movez claimed that 70% of Anthropic engineers used self-improving loops and predicted “no more prompting” within three to six months. Those figures do not appear in the audited workshop transcripts or Martin's reviewed posts, so they should not drive this quarter's architecture.

Before adding another node, hold back real incidents and ask which existing node has never met an input its builders did not choose. A lean graph earns production authority only when its token savings survive that test.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A small graph becomes trustworthy when held-back failures show that its leaner context, routes and human gates still produce the intended outcome.</p></div>
