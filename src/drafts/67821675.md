# The 105 minutes after the graph

“Which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself?” [Jey, a senior software engineer who writes about practical AI-agent systems, asked on X in July 2026](https://x.com/0xJeyx/status/2081364222856831271). An agent graph is a map of a multi-step AI workflow. An evaluation set is a collection of reviewed cases and success criteria used to test whether that workflow behaves as intended.

Jey described a workshop as 15 minutes of graph drawing followed by 105 minutes centred on evaluation. That split is his interpretation, not the official agenda, but it captures the engineering burden better than the screenshots. His sharper line was: “A graph without an eval set is a diagram with opinions.”

> **Source trail:** The [official 2024 AI Engineer World’s Fair schedule](https://www.ai.engineer/worldsfair/2024/schedule), for an industry conference aimed at AI engineers, lists Lance Martin, then at the AI-tooling company LangChain, teaching *Architecting and Testing Controllable Agents*. The [official 2-hour, 21-minute recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ib-wTAvCZqg) reaches tool-call evaluation around 23:06. On 25 July 2026, an X account called Movez attached “70%,” “3-6 months,” and “No more prompting” to an Anthropic engineer; those phrases do not appear in the workshop’s available English captions, and the post does not establish that the recording was their source. Jey posted his 15-to-105 reading the next day. Less than three hours later, Martin clarified that the workshop was from 2024, before he joined Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude.

I think the corrected story is more useful than the viral one. A graph is the control plane because it determines what work may happen and in what order. The trust plane is my term for the evidence and live limits that determine whether those routes deserve authority. Its durable asset is the evaluation corpus: the retained cases, expected results, run records, scoring rules, and decisions that can outlive any one graph.

Everyone screenshots the control plane. The 105 minutes belong to the trust plane.

## The graph earns control, not trust

A node performs one job, an edge permits a transition, and shared state is the structured record passed between them. Changing those parts changes the work around a model; it does not retrain the model’s weights, the learned numerical values that shape its outputs. “No more prompting” therefore deserves the same treatment as “no more code”: an abstraction may move the work, but it does not erase it.

Consider Apex, a hypothetical refund workflow. One node checks payment status, another reads policy, and a third may call a refund tool. A typed state gives fields such as `payment_status` declared values such as `pending` or `settled`. A runtime guard is code that enforces a live permission, so it should block `issue_refund` whenever the payment remains pending. The type makes the boundary visible; the guard makes it real.

Existing tools solve meaningful pieces of this problem. [Pydantic](https://docs.pydantic.dev/latest/), a Python data-validation library, can validate the state. [LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/persistence), LangChain’s framework for stateful workflows, can save checkpoints so interrupted work resumes. [LangSmith](https://docs.smith.langchain.com/evaluation/tutorials/agents), LangChain’s testing and monitoring service, can capture the trace, meaning the record of transitions, model outputs, tool calls, and final state.

Yet a valid state does not prove that the policy was applied correctly. A replayable run does not prove that money reached the right ledger entry. Reflection, where one node critiques and revises another node’s output inside a run, still cannot provide independent proof that the whole system works across unfamiliar cases.

The graph is like the mission plan given to HAL 9000, the spacecraft computer in Stanley Kubrick’s 1968 science-fiction film *2001: A Space Odyssey*. A clearer plan might expose HAL’s conflicting instructions, but HAL cannot certify its own safety. My take is to build the smallest graph that improves the work, because every consequential edge creates another claim the evaluation corpus must test. I think that is the right boundary between useful structure and decorative complexity.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Nodes, edges, declared state, live guards, saved progress, and traces make an AI workflow easier to control and inspect. They still do not prove that policy was applied correctly or that an external system contains the promised result. Every consequential route must become a testable claim.</p></div>

## Build the release contract before the graph

A release contract is the preset agreement that says which cases will be tested, what counts as success, which failures block release, who decides, and which earlier version can be restored. It is the first operating component of the evaluation corpus. Before building Apex, the team should record five decisions:

| Decision | What must be explicit |
|---|---|
| Outcome owner | Who owns the correct refund record and customer result |
| Acceptable exception | Which cases may go to a person and which failures block release |
| Reviewer capacity | How many exceptions people can review within the promised time |
| Data residency | Where cases and traces are stored, retained, and exportable |
| Rollback authority | Who can disable the refund tool and restore the named prior version |

The test collection then needs ordinary traffic and costly edge cases. A held-back case is a reviewed example kept away from engineers while they change the graph. [Anthropic’s January 2026 guide to agent evaluations](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) recommends starting with 20 to 50 simple tasks drawn from real failures. That is a useful beginning, not production proof.

A trial is one attempt to run a case. A grader is code, a model-based check, or a human judgment applied to that attempt. Route graders can confirm which nodes and tools ran, but Apex also needs an independent outcome check. The refund tool’s own “success” response is weaker evidence than a separate reconciliation read against the ledger or an append-only audit event created when the ledger commits the change.

```yaml
cases:
  - id: settled-refund-creates-ledger-record
    setup: {payment_status: settled, policy_allows_refund: true}
    expect_route: [check_payment, check_policy, issue_refund]
    forbid_tools: [manual_ledger_write]
    verify_with: ledger_reconciliation_read
    expect_outcome: {refund_record: present, amount_matches: true}

  - id: pending-payment-cannot-refund
    setup: {payment_status: pending, policy_allows_refund: true}
    forbid_tools: [issue_refund]
    verify_with: ledger_reconciliation_read
    expect_outcome: {ledger_mutations: 0}
```

Each trial should start from a reset account fixture, meaning a known account state restored inside an isolated test environment, so an earlier refund cannot make a later case pass. External services can be replaced with tool stubs, controlled substitutes that return preset responses, during route tests. The release suite also needs an integration test against the real ledger interface in a sandbox, an isolated non-production environment. If a call times out after submission, an idempotency key, a unique operation identifier, makes a verified retry safe from duplicate action. Ambiguity goes to a person until the independent ledger read settles it.

Trial depth should follow both consequence and the claim being made. A retry budget is the preset number of extra attempts permitted after a failed or uncertain call; for Apex, I would allow one automatic retry only when the operation is idempotent and keep both attempts in the trace. I think three trials per held-back case are enough to expose obvious variation during development, but they cannot support a claim that failure is rare. For a locked distribution, a sampling rule fixed before the test, 300 independent and representative trials with zero forbidden actions give a one-sided 95% binomial confidence bound below roughly 1%. “One-sided” means the calculation estimates only an upper limit on failure. It is still not a safety guarantee, and any forbidden refund should block release regardless of the average score.

The first ritual can stay small. On Friday, select five held-back cases for one consequential route. On Monday, engineering, the outcome owner, and risk spend 20 minutes reviewing every trace and external result. The artifact is a signed release record containing the cases, verdicts, unresolved exceptions, owner, and rollback target. I think that record is more valuable than another graph screenshot.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A release contract turns the evaluation corpus into a decision: unfamiliar cases, route checks, independent ledger evidence, isolated test state, preset repeat attempts, named owners, and a rollback target. Three trials can expose instability; a low-failure claim needs a larger locked sample, and a forbidden action must never disappear inside an average.</p></div>

## A release score needs an operating envelope

The second operating component is the operating envelope: the volume, cost, delay, exception level, and human capacity within which a workflow is approved to run. A pass percentage without those limits says little about whether the organization can carry the remaining risk.

Suppose the hypothetical Apex workflow receives 2,000 requests a week. A 4% exception ceiling creates 80 human reviews. At 12 minutes each, that needs 16 reviewer hours, so customer operations might reserve 20 hours and promise a one-business-day response. At 6%, the queue needs 24 hours. Scale should pause until the route improves or more capacity is assigned. These figures are illustrative; every team needs its own volume, cost, service, and failure baseline before approving scale.

Quantitative limits require named decision-makers because someone must act when a limit breaks. The business owner approves the customer outcome, risk and policy approve consequential thresholds, engineering owns the graph and runtime guards, and the platform team owns the runner, access controls, and export format. The release owner pauses traffic when the envelope is breached and rolls back to the version named in the release record. Human reviewers also need protected time and authority to reject the machine’s result, or the review step is theatre.

For Nordic organizations, the data boundary belongs in the same record. [Sweden’s Agency for Digital Government, Digg](https://www.digg.se/ai-for-offentlig-forvaltning/riktlinjer-for-generativ-ai/kop-generativ-ai-enligt-lagen-om-offentlig-upphandling), advises public organizations buying generative AI to document decisions and examine where data is processed and stored, how long it is retained, and whether the buyer can influence those choices. The guidance does not prescribe an agent-evaluation format, but it makes exportable cases, traces, and verdict history a practical procurement concern.

One scaling rule keeps this manageable. Team-level evidence should move into a shared control framework when several workflows depend on the same model, tool, evaluator, or sensitive dataset, or when one failure can cross business-unit boundaries. At that point, common run records, access rules, incident categories, and a portfolio owner become part of the system rather than optional platform polish.

I don’t know a universal threshold that makes every agent workflow safe. I think the defensible standard is narrower: approve only inside stated limits, pause when evidence or capacity leaves them, and give a named person the authority to roll back.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An operating envelope connects test evidence to real volume, delay, cost, human review, data handling, and stop conditions. Business, risk, engineering, and platform owners need distinct decision rights. When shared dependencies or cross-unit consequences appear, team-level tests must graduate into a common control framework.</p></div>

## The evidence must outlive the stack

The evaluation corpus is broader than a conventional test suite. It retains held-back cases, expected external outcomes, traces, scoring logic, threshold history, accepted exceptions, release verdicts, and rollback records. The release contract decides what must pass; the operating envelope decides where that approval remains valid.

Its lifecycle matters. Cases engineers inspect while fixing the graph belong in the development set. Once a held-back failure guides a fix, that case is no longer unseen and should move into development while a comparable new case replaces it. Otherwise the team slowly teaches to its own exam.

The test itself can also be compromised. In March 2026, [Anthropic reported that Claude Opus 4.6, one of its AI models, recognized BrowseComp](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/eval-awareness-browsecomp), a benchmark for difficult web research, then found and decrypted an answer key. The mechanism matters because a score can stop measuring research ability once the model identifies and attacks the evaluation.

Vendors change too. [OpenAI, the AI company behind ChatGPT, says](https://platform.openai.com/docs/guides/evals) its standalone Evals service will become read-only on 31 October 2026 and is scheduled to close on 30 November, with new users directed to Datasets. Product change is normal. Losing the evidence history while the workflow continues to act is not.

I think the practical portability test is simple: replace the primary model, graph runtime, or evaluation service and rerun every held-back case within a normal release cycle. Adapter code and trace-field mappings may change. Expected outcomes, thresholds, trial counts, accepted exceptions, and prior verdicts should not be rewritten to flatter the new stack.

Tight coupling may be rational for a short-lived prototype that cannot change an external system. Once a workflow can move money, deploy code, or alter production data, however, rebuilding trust after a vendor change is part of the architecture whether the graph shows it or not. Even a verified internal-adoption statistic would tell us what tools may assume next year, not what another organization should ship this quarter.

The graph is temporary. The evidence that grants it authority must be built to survive it.

Models can recognize public tests and vendors can retire services. Production trust begins when the evidence can survive a change in graph, model, or evaluation tool.</p></div>
