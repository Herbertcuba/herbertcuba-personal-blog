Last week a clip from a two-year-old workshop went viral as breaking news. A hype account clipped Lance Martin's free two-hour session on agent graphs from the AI Engineer World's Fair 2024 (a talk he gave before he joined Anthropic), framed it as fresh intel from inside the lab, and pulled in half a million views with a claim: 70% of Anthropic engineers already run self-improving loops, and in a few months we'll all build graphs instead of writing prompts.

I tried to find that number in the actual workshop material before writing this. I couldn't. As far as I can tell it's a paraphrase of a paraphrase, and Lance himself replied to the thread laughing that the talk is from 2024. So I'll leave the number aside. The workshop itself has a better story anyway.

Another attendee, Jey, clocked what the viral post skipped. The graph diagram is done in the first 15 minutes. The remaining 105 minutes keep returning to one topic: evaluation. The session ends with everyone building eval datasets for the graph they drew in the first quarter of an hour.

That ratio is the whole argument. Fifteen minutes of architecture, 105 minutes of proof. Everyone screenshots the graph. Almost nobody ships the eval set.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A hype account turned a 2024 workshop into "no more prompting." The workshop itself spent 15 minutes on the graph and 105 on evaluation. That ratio is where agent engineering actually lives.</p></div>

## The diagram is the easy part

Drawing an agent graph is an afternoon of work, and I think that is exactly why it goes viral.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Graphs spread because they fit in a screenshot. Eval sets don't, because they are folders of awkward inputs and scoring functions. The thing that spreads is the thing that took 15 minutes.</p></div>

Say you're building an agent that triages a shared support inbox. In LangGraph, the Python library Lance helped build at LangChain, you start with a StateGraph: a container that holds your nodes and the edges between them. Each node is one step of the job. One node classifies the incoming email. One retrieves the right help docs. One drafts a reply. One decides whether to escalate to a human. You define your nodes, wire the edges, decide where the loop closes.

The state moving between those nodes is a TypedDict: a plain Python dictionary with a declared shape, so every key has a name and a type. Yours might carry the email subject, the body, the assigned category, the draft reply. Every node reads from it and writes back to it, which means you always know what the agent knows.

A lot of these graphs are ReAct loops under the hood. ReAct is short for reasoning plus acting: the model thinks, calls a tool, looks at the result, and thinks again, in a loop, until it has an answer. Wire that loop into a graph and you still make dinner.

A diagram is legible in a way a test harness never will be. You can't screenshot judgment. Half a million people will look at your graph. A few thousand will ever look at your golden dataset.

## A graph without an eval set is a diagram with opinions

Jey's phrase, and the best line to come out of the whole thread.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A graph states what you hope the system does. An eval set proves what it does on inputs you didn't pick. Most agent systems in production have only ever seen inputs their own authors wrote.</p></div>

The graph says what you hope the system does. It proves nothing about what the system does on inputs you didn't hand-pick.

Proving it holds is the job. For the support agent, that means an eval set of real emails: the angry customer who pastes a spreadsheet into the message body, the one-line "it's broken" with no product named, the polite email in Spanish when your docs are English-only, the spam that reads like a sales lead. Your classifier either survives those or it doesn't, and the only way to know is to run them.

Jey's post ends with a question I've been turning over since: which node in your graph has never met an input you didn't write yourself? For most agent systems I see in the wild, the honest answer is all of them. The demo works because the demo's inputs were chosen to make it work.

I think most teams skip this step for a dull reason: nobody gets promoted for a golden dataset. I don't know how to make evals cheap. They're expensive to build and boring to maintain. I just know the cost doesn't go away by ignoring it.

## The trust plane extends the typed-state argument

This connects to something I wrote earlier this year about typed state.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Graphs are the control plane, types and evals are the trust plane. Demos live in the first, customer renewals live in the second. You need both, and the second is what most teams skip.</p></div>

My argument then was that types are how you make an agent's working memory legible: if the state moving between nodes has a defined shape, you can reason about the system instead of guessing at it. The TypedDict in your support agent is doing exactly that.

I think of the full picture as two planes. Graphs are the control plane. They decide what runs, in what order, with what retries. Types and evals are the trust plane. They decide whether you can believe what ran. A team that invests only in the control plane ends up with a very elegant machine they can't trust. A team that invests in both has something they can hand to a customer.

In my experience, the control plane is where the demos live and the trust plane is where the renewals live. Procurement doesn't ask to see your graph. They ask what happens when it fails, and the only honest answer comes from evals.

## The new "no more code"

The "no more prompting" claim deserves two responses at once: take it seriously, then deflate it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>"No more prompting" rhymes with 2023's "no more code." Prompts move inside nodes, and the scarce skill becomes knowing whether they work. That skill is evaluation.</p></div>

Seriously: prompting as the primary interface to a model is shrinking. When you move from a single call to a graph with tools and state, the unit of work shifts from writing the perfect prompt to designing the system the prompts live inside. The 15-minute diagram reflects that shift.

Deflating: we've heard this shape before. "No more code" was the claim in 2023, and what happened is code got cheaper and judgment about code got more expensive. I think prompting follows the same curve. Prompts move inside nodes. The skill becomes knowing which node to put them in and how to tell whether they're working. Which is, again, evals.

There's a scene in The Matrix where Neo finally sees the code behind the world instead of the world itself. Everyone in the agent space right now wants to be Neo staring at the graph, seeing the structure. I think the more useful job is Cypher's before he betrayed everyone: running the monitors, watching what the system does instead of what the architecture diagram says it should do. The person reading the traces is the person who knows what's true.

## What the 70% signal actually means

Even setting aside whether the number is real, adoption inside Anthropic is a signal.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Frontier-lab adoption predicts what tooling will assume next year. Your org's quarter is a separate question. Steal the workshop's ratio: 15 minutes on the graph, 105 on the evals.</p></div>

It's a different signal than most people are taking from it. What frontier labs adopt tells you what the tooling will assume in 12 months. When the people building the models build their own work as graphs with self-improving loops, the frameworks, the SDKs, and the defaults will follow them. Watch that when you make platform bets.

Your roadmap this quarter is a separate question. Anthropic's engineers have eval infrastructure most companies don't, problems shaped like their tooling, and a tolerance for frontier failure modes that a bank or a hospital can't afford. Copy their architecture without their trust plane and you ship a drawing.

My take: steal the ratio. For every fifteen minutes your team spends drawing the graph, budget the 105 for the evals. The workshop already told you where the work is. The viral post just cropped that part out.
