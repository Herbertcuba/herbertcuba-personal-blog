Last week a clip from Lance Martin's agent-graphs workshop went viral. Half a million views, thousands of reposts, and a claim spreading through my timeline: 70% of Anthropic engineers are already using self-improving loops, and within a few months we'll all be building graphs instead of writing prompts.

I tried to find that number in the actual workshop material before writing this. I couldn't. As far as I can tell, it's a paraphrase from a hype account, not a quote from Lance. So I'll leave it aside. What I did find in the workshop itself is a better story.

Another attendee, Jey, clocked something the viral post skipped entirely. The graph diagram is done in the first 15 minutes of a two-hour session. The remaining 105 minutes keep circling back to one topic: evaluation. The workshop ends with everyone building eval datasets for the graph they drew in the first quarter of an hour.

That ratio is the whole argument. Fifteen minutes of architecture, 105 minutes of proof. Everyone screenshots the graph. Almost nobody ships the eval set.

<strong>TL;DR:</strong> A viral clip turned a 2024 workshop into "no more prompting." The workshop spent 15 minutes on the graph and 105 on evaluation. That ratio is where agent engineering lives.
</div>

## The diagram is the easy part

Drawing an agent graph is an afternoon of work. You define your nodes, wire the edges, decide where the loop closes. Any engineer who has read the LangGraph docs or sat through one workshop can produce a plausible-looking diagram by dinner.

I think this is exactly why the graph is what goes viral. It's visible. It fits in a screenshot. It has nodes and arrows and it looks like engineering. A diagram is legible in a way that a test harness never will be, because you can't screenshot judgment.

The eval set has none of these properties. It's a folder of inputs you didn't choose yourself, expected outputs you had to argue about, and scoring functions someone has to maintain. Nobody reposts that. Half a million people will look at a graph. A few thousand will ever look at your golden dataset.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<strong>TL;DR:</strong> Graphs go viral because they're visible and fit in a screenshot. Eval sets don't, because they're folders of awkward inputs and scoring functions. The thing that spreads is the thing that took 15 minutes.
</div>

## A graph without an eval set is a diagram with opinions

Jey's phrase, not mine: a graph without an eval set is a diagram with opinions. It says what you hope the system does. It proves nothing about what the system does on inputs you didn't hand-pick.

Proving it holds is the job. That means adversarial inputs, malformed data, the user who pastes a spreadsheet into a field meant for a sentence, the edge case that only shows up on a Friday afternoon when the upstream API is degraded. Your graph either survives those or it doesn't, and the only way to know is to test against cases you would never have written yourself.

There's a useful question buried in Jey's post that I've been turning over since: which node in your graph has never met an input you didn't write yourself? For most of the agent systems I see in the wild, the honest answer is all of them. The demo works because the demo's inputs were chosen to make it work.

I don't know how to make this cheap. Eval sets are expensive to build and boring to maintain. I just know the cost doesn't go away by ignoring it.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<strong>TL;DR:</strong> A graph states what you hope the system does. An eval set proves what it does on inputs you didn't pick. Most agent systems in production have only ever seen inputs their own authors wrote.
</div>

## The trust plane extends the typed-state argument

This connects directly to something I wrote earlier this year about typed state. My argument then was that types are how you make an agent's working memory legible: if the state moving between nodes has a defined shape, you can reason about the system instead of guessing at it.

Graphs are the control plane. They decide what runs, in what order, with what retries. Types and evals are the trust plane. They decide whether you can believe what ran. A team that invests only in the control plane ends up with a very elegant machine they can't trust. A team that invests in both has something they can hand to a customer.

In my experience, the control plane is where the demos live and the trust plane is where the renewals live. Procurement doesn't ask to see your graph. They ask what happens when it fails, and the only honest answer comes from evals.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<strong>TL;DR:</strong> Graphs are the control plane, types and evals are the trust plane. Demos live in the first, customer renewals live in the second. You need both, and the second is what most teams skip.
</div>

## The new "no more code"

The "no more prompting" claim deserves two responses at once, because it's worth taking seriously and deflating at the same time.

Seriously: prompting as the primary interface to a model is shrinking. When you move from a single call to a graph with loops, tools, and state, the unit of work shifts from writing the perfect prompt to designing the system the prompts live inside. The 15-minute diagram reflects that shift.

Deflating: we've heard this shape before. "No more code" was the claim in 2023, and what happened is that code got cheaper and judgment about code got more expensive. I think prompting follows the same curve. Prompts move inside nodes. The skill becomes knowing which node to put them in and how to tell whether they're working. Which is, again, evals.

There's a scene in The Matrix where Neo finally sees the code behind the world instead of the world itself. Everyone in the agent space right now wants to be Neo staring at the graph, seeing the structure. I think the more useful job is Cypher's before he betrayed everyone: running the monitors, watching what the system does instead of what the architecture diagram says it should do. The person reading the traces is the person who knows what's true.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<strong>TL;DR:</strong> "No more prompting" rhymes with 2023's "no more code." Prompts move inside nodes, and the scarce skill becomes knowing whether they work. That skill is evaluation.
</div>

## What the 70% signal actually means

Even setting aside whether the number is real, adoption inside Anthropic is a signal worth reading carefully. It's a different signal than most people are taking from it.

Frontier-lab adoption tells you what the tooling will assume in 12 months. When the people building the models build their own work as graphs with self-improving loops, the frameworks, the SDKs, and the defaults will follow them. That's worth watching when you make platform bets.

It does not tell you what your org should do this quarter. Anthropic's engineers have eval infrastructure most companies don't, problems shaped like their tooling, and a tolerance for frontier failure modes that a bank or a hospital can't afford. Copying their architecture without copying their trust plane gives you the diagram and none of the proof.

My take: steal the ratio, not the roadmap. For every fifteen minutes your team spends on the graph, budget the 105 for the evals. The workshop already told you where the work is. The viral post just cropped that part out.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<strong>TL;DR:</strong> Frontier-lab adoption predicts what tooling will assume next year, not what your org should ship this quarter. Steal the workshop's ratio, 15 minutes on the graph and 105 on the evals, instead of copying the architecture without the proof.
</div>
