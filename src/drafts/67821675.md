Consider an AI system reviewing a 90-page supplier contract. The waste begins when every model call receives the agreement, policy library, negotiation history, tool output, and conversation so far. Each specialist rereads material it cannot act on.

A **context window** is the finite input-and-output space available to one model call. **Tokens** are the pieces of text processed inside it. Context engineering decides what deserves that scarce space; graph engineering creates boundaries where those decisions can be enforced.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Token optimization starts by limiting each decision to relevant evidence, then testing that limit rather than assuming less context is safe.</p>
</div>

## Prompt, context, and graph engineering solve different problems

These three disciplines work at different levels:

| Discipline | Question | Contract example |
|---|---|---|
| Prompt engineering | How should one task be instructed? | “Extract every termination right and cite its clause.” |
| Context engineering | What should this call see? | The relevant clauses and approved policy, not the entire negotiation history. |
| Graph engineering | How should work move between calls? | Route legal, security, and commercial checks, then merge their findings. |

[Greg Isenberg](https://youtu.be/JWhICz1QR8M), a technology entrepreneur explaining graph engineering in an August 2026 video, presents the last layer as designing work around AI. He distinguishes an **agent graph**, which routes jobs and handoffs, from a **knowledge graph**, which records entities and their relationships.

His practical ladder is useful: perform the workflow manually, expose its handoffs as file artifacts such as `clauses.json` and `security-review.md`, then orchestrate the stable parts. This keeps teams from automating a process they do not yet understand.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Prompts shape one instruction, context supplies one decision, and graphs organize the handoffs across many decisions.</p>
</div>

## Build the smallest graph that exposes the decisions

A **node** performs one job. An **edge** is an allowed connection, while a **transition** is the edge chosen during a run. **Shared state** holds current artifacts, and **conditional routing** selects the next transition from that state.

```text
intake → clause map ─┬→ legal check ─────┐
                     ├→ security check ──┼→ merge → skeptic → route
                     └→ commercial check ┘
```

Intake followed by clause mapping is sequential because the second step needs the first result. The three checks then **fan out** in parallel and **merge** into one review. That split-and-rejoin shape is a **diamond topology**.

The skeptic is a checker node with a deliberately separate job: find unsupported conclusions, missing citations, and conflicts between branches. This is the logic behind the reflection phase in [APEX](/posts/2026-04-05-apex-framework/), the Agentic Production Execution operating model: production and review remain separate before action. The route can send ordinary wording to a draft, unusual liability to counsel, and sensitive data terms to security leadership.

The diagram is quick. Tuning its state fields, route conditions, and review thresholds is the engineering, which is why human gates should follow risk rather than appear after every node.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The useful graph is the smallest one that separates real decisions, parallelizes independent work, and makes risky routes visible.</p>
</div>

## Spend tokens at the handoffs

The graph becomes a context budget when each boundary changes what travels forward:

- **Retrieve locally.** Give the legal node relevant clauses and legal policy; let the security node retrieve its own controls.
- **Pass artifacts, not transcripts.** A defined-schema handoff record has required fields such as clause ID, finding, evidence, confidence, and unresolved question.
- **Cache stable material.** Where supported, [prompt caching](https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-caching) can reuse an unchanged prefix instead of processing it repeatedly.
- **Separate state from memory.** Persistent state saves this contract’s artifacts across pauses and retries. Memory carries selected facts across contracts and should be retrieved only when the next decision needs them.

Start with the smallest useful graph and add a branch only when it creates a distinct context boundary, independent check, or risk decision. A low-risk standard clause may proceed automatically; a novel indemnity clause should stop for a named human reviewer. Fewer tokens are valuable only inside that operating constraint.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Token savings come from local retrieval, compact handoffs, reusable stable context, selective memory, and routes matched to consequence.</p>
</div>

## A checker reviews one run; an eval set tests the route

There is an important attribution boundary here. Isenberg’s video makes the practitioner case for designing work around AI, but its transcript contains no discussion of evals. The trust argument comes from separate sources.

Lance Martin, now at Anthropic and formerly at LangChain, taught a workshop called *Architecting and Testing Controllable Agents* at the 2024 AI Engineer World’s Fair. [Jey](https://x.com/0xJeyx/status/2081364222856831271), an X account covering AI engineering, later framed its structure as 15 minutes drawing the graph and 105 minutes returning to evaluation. The ratio matters more than the stopwatch.

A checker inspects one contract run. An **eval set** repeats that separation across representative and difficult cases with expected outcomes or grading rules, including inputs the builders did not write. Anthropic’s guidance suggests starting with [20 to 50 tasks drawn from real failures](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents).

For this graph, hold back contracts with missing exhibits, conflicting policies, and unfamiliar clause combinations. Record quality and token use by route. A cheaper path that overlooks a restriction is simply a cheaper failure.

Graphs are the control plane; defined handoffs and evals are the trust plane. So the useful final question is the one Jey’s framing leaves us with: which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself?

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A checker challenges one output; an eval set proves whether the graph’s cheaper context boundaries survive unfamiliar cases.</p>
</div>
