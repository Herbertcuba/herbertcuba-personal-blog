Last week a clip of Lance Martin went viral. Half a million views, thousands of reposts. The framing: an Anthropic engineer says 70% of their engineers run self-improving loops, and in 3 to 6 months we will all build graphs instead of writing prompts. No more prompting.

The clip is real. The framing is not. Martin replied to the thread himself, laughing: the talk is from the AI Engineer World's Fair 2024, before he joined Anthropic. A hype account clipped a two-year-old workshop, dressed it as news from inside Anthropic, and the timeline ate it.

I think that correction is the most interesting part of the whole episode. Because when you sit down and watch what the workshop actually spends its two hours on, the viral takeaway collapses.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A 2024 workshop got clipped as breaking Anthropic news. The clip sells graphs. The workshop sells evals. The ratio between the two is the entire argument.</p></div>

## What the workshop actually teaches

A developer named Jey did something almost nobody does with viral clips: he watched the full recording and took notes with timestamps. His post is the source for everything in this section.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The diagram is finished in the first 15 minutes. The remaining 105 minutes keep returning to evaluation, and the workshop closes by building eval datasets for the graph.</p></div>

The graph diagram is done in the first fifteen minutes. Evaluation shows up at 23:06 and keeps coming back, and the closing section is literally building eval datasets for the graph.

Read that ratio again. Fifteen minutes of drawing. One hundred and five minutes of checking whether the drawing survives contact with reality. Jey's summary line is better than anything I would write: "A graph without an eval set is a diagram with opinions."

So the viral version got the direction exactly backwards. The graph is the easy part, the part you can screenshot. The eval set is the job.

## The invoice agent, drawn and checked

One example, all the way through. An agent that reads incoming invoices and pulls out vendor, date, and total.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A LangGraph is boxes, arrows, and a shared state object. None of that tells you whether the agent is right. Only an eval set does.</p></div>

In LangGraph, the library Martin worked on at LangChain, a graph has three parts. Nodes are functions that do work. Edges are arrows that decide which node runs next. State is the shared object every node can read and update, usually defined as a TypedDict, a Python dict where you declare the keys and their types up front. You wire it all into a StateGraph, the container that runs the flow.

```python
class InvoiceState(TypedDict):
    raw_text: str
    vendor: str
    total: float
    needs_review: bool
```

Four nodes. Parse the invoice, extract the fields, flag anything odd, return the result. An afternoon of work. The diagram looks great in a deck.

But the graph says nothing about correctness. It says the flow. A flow can be perfectly executed and perfectly wrong. The model reads an invoice with two totals on it, net and gross, picks one, and the graph happily carries that wrong number to the end. Every node did its job. The answer is still wrong.

This is where the eval set comes in. An eval set is a collection of inputs you did not write for the demo, paired with the outputs you expect. Ugly invoices. Scans at an angle. Two totals, missing dates, a vendor name that is also a common noun. You run the graph against the set and count how often it holds.

Jey's post ends with the sharpest version of this I have seen: which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself?

## Why the hype version wins anyway

The 70% number is a paraphrase of a paraphrase. One account says 70, another quoting the same clip says 80. Whether Martin ever stated any such number is unverifiable, and he is not the source being cited either way. The number is doing emotional work, not informational work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>"No more prompting" is the new "no more code." The claim spreads because it sells relief from the hard part, and the hard part is the eval set.</p></div>

"No more prompting" is the new "no more code." Same shape, same appeal. Both promise that the tedious layer disappears and you get to keep the fun layer. And both turn out to mean something narrower: the layer changes form, and the work moves somewhere less visible.

There is a scene in The Matrix where Neo gets kung fu uploaded in seconds and says "I know kung fu." The fantasy of the viral clip is that graphs work like that: draw the right diagram and the skill is installed. The workshop, the actual 2024 recording, is Morpheus's reply. Show me. The upload is the graph. The sparring program, the place where the claim gets tested against resistance you did not choreograph, is the eval set.

I don't know how fast self-improving loops will spread inside the big labs, and I would not trust anyone who claims to. What adoption inside Anthropic would tell you, if the number were real, is what tooling will assume in 12 months. It tells you nothing about what your team should do this quarter.

## What to do this quarter

My take: steal the ratio, ignore the timeline.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Spend one afternoon on the graph and the rest of the sprint on the eval set. Inputs you did not choose, expected outputs, run on every change.</p></div>

If you are a junior engineer building your first agent, the 15-to-105 split is the most useful thing you can take from that workshop. Draw the graph in an afternoon. Nodes, edges, a typed state. Then spend the rest of the week on the eval set, because that is where the engineering lives.

Start small. Twenty real inputs, not two hundred synthetic ones. Take invoices from the last month, strip what you must, and write down by hand what the right answer is for each. Include the two that made someone groan. That groan is information.

Then wire the set into your workflow so the graph has to pass it before any change ships. A graph with an eval set is a system. A graph without one is a diagram with opinions, and Jey is right that most of what gets screenshot and shared is the second kind.

The clip that went viral promised you could skip the unglamorous part. The workshop it was cut from spends 88% of its runtime on the unglamorous part. That gap is the whole story. Drawing the graph is the afternoon. Proving it holds on inputs you did not choose is the job.
