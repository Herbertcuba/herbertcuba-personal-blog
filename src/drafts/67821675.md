# The 105 minutes after the graph

“Which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself?” [Jey, a senior software engineer who writes about practical AI-agent systems on X, asked in July 2026](https://x.com/0xJeyx/status/2081364222856831271). An agent graph is a map of a multi-step AI workflow. An evaluation set is a collection of reviewed cases and success criteria used to test whether that workflow behaves as intended.

Jey framed a recorded workshop as 15 minutes for drawing the graph and 105 minutes for evaluation. That ratio is his interpretation, not the workshop’s official running order. Yet it captures the engineering burden better than the graph screenshots that travelled around social media. His sharper line was: “A graph without an eval set is a diagram with opinions.”

The source trail is short enough to inspect. The schedule for the [2024 AI Engineer World’s Fair, an industry conference for AI engineers](https://www.ai.engineer/worldsfair/2024/schedule), lists Lance Martin, then at the AI-tooling company LangChain, presenting *Architecting and Testing Controllable Agents*. The [official recording, published on 11 October 2024](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ib-wTAvCZqg), lasts 2 hours, 21 minutes, and 54 seconds. Around 23 minutes, Martin is already comparing agent routes through tool-call evaluation, and the recording description promises testing during a run, before release, and in production monitoring. The official 2026 schedule lists a different Martin session, a scheduled 20-minute talk on using Claude for long-running tasks rather than a two-hour graph workshop.

The viral Anthropic story arrived two years after the workshop. On 25 July 2026, [Movez published an X post](https://x.com/0xMovez/status/2081011657807925604) that called its embedded video a “30-minute lecture” and attributed “70%,” “3-6 months,” and “No more prompting” to an Anthropic engineer. The public post text does not establish that those claims came from Martin’s 2024 workshop, and none of the phrases appears in the recording’s available English captions. On 26 July, Jey published the 15-to-105 interpretation; 2 hours and 56 minutes later, [Martin clarified](https://x.com/RLanceMartin/status/2081408482344149047) that the workshop came from 2024, before he joined Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude.

I think the corrected story is more useful than the viral one. A graph is a control plane because it determines which work may happen and in what order. The trust plane is my term for the defined data, live limits, unseen test cases, and release rules that determine whether those routes deserve authority. Drawing a graph can take an afternoon. Proving it holds on inputs the builders did not choose is the job.

## The graph earns control, not trust

A node performs one job. An edge permits a transition, while a route is the sequence of transitions taken for one case. Shared state is the structured record of what the workflow knows so far. Changing the graph changes jobs, routing, state, and tool use around the model; it does not train the model’s weights, the learned numerical parameters that shape its outputs.

That distinction lets us take “no more prompting” seriously without taking it literally. Prompts still operate inside nodes, but the graph can stop one giant prompt from carrying planning, specialist work, checking, and approval at once. Independent jobs can run in parallel, and conditional edges can send a routine case toward automation while a risky one goes to a person.

Consider Apex, a hypothetical refund-review workflow. One node checks payment status, another retrieves the relevant policy, and a third drafts a decision. Difficult cases go to a skeptic node that looks for conflicting evidence. A private draft may need a light review gate, while an approved refund needs a stronger one because the workflow can move money.

A typed state is a shared record with named fields and allowed values. Apex might limit `payment_status` to `pending` or `settled`. A runtime guard is code that enforces a rule while the workflow runs, so it should separately prevent a pending payment from reaching the refund tool. The type makes the intended boundary visible, while the guard enforces it.

Existing tools already solve important pieces. [Pydantic](https://docs.pydantic.dev/latest/), a Python data-validation library, can check state against declared fields and values. [LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/persistence), LangChain’s framework for long-running stateful workflows, can save checkpoints so interrupted work resumes instead of starting over. This recovery capability is called durable execution. [LangSmith](https://docs.smith.langchain.com/evaluation/tutorials/agents), LangChain’s testing and monitoring service, can inspect final responses, tool calls, and the routes taken through a workflow.

Those are substantial engineering gains, but they stop at different boundaries. Valid state does not prove that policy was applied correctly. Recovery does not prove that the payment ledger contains the right refund, and a captured run does not decide whether an exception is acceptable. An evaluation corpus, meaning the retained collection of cases, expected outside-world results, scoring rules, and release decisions, must carry that evidence.

Reflection solves another narrow problem. A reflection node critiques one run and may send its draft back for a limited revision. Evaluation tests a version of the whole workflow across reviewed cases. I would cap reflection retries because an open loop can consume model calls without producing stronger evidence.

The graph is a little like the mission plan entrusted to HAL 9000, the spacecraft computer in filmmaker Stanley Kubrick’s 1968 science-fiction film *2001: A Space Odyssey*. A clearer plan might expose HAL’s conflicting instructions, but HAL still cannot provide independent evidence that its decisions are safe. I think the same rule applies here: use the smallest graph that improves the work, because every consequential transition adds another claim the evidence must test.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A graph that maps an AI workflow can separate jobs, direct each case, preserve shared information, and require human review where consequences rise. Declared data shapes, live enforcement rules, saved progress, self-critique steps, and testing tools each solve a real problem, but none independently proves that the promised outside-world result is correct.</p></div>

## Evaluation starts with cases you did not choose

I use release contract to mean the preset cases, scoring rules, pass thresholds, owners, limits on extra attempts, and rollback conditions that decide whether a workflow version ships. A held-back case is a reviewed example kept away from engineers while they change the graph. When familiar cases guide the design and judge the release, the test has become another design input.

For Apex, the held-back set needs two views of reality. A frequency sample mirrors ordinary traffic, such as common payment states and policy versions. A risk sample deliberately includes uncommon but costly hazards, such as stale status, duplicate requests, or a missing ledger write. The first estimates routine behavior, while the second prevents a severe failure from disappearing inside a high average score.

[Anthropic’s January 2026 guide to agent evaluations](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) recommends starting with 20 to 50 simple tasks drawn from real failures. A task is one case with defined inputs and success criteria. Each attempt is a trial, and consequential cases need repeated trials because model outputs can vary.

A trace is the complete record of a trial, including node transitions, model outputs, tool calls, intermediate results, and final state. A grader is code, a model-based scoring rule, or human judgment that checks part of that evidence. Apex needs route checks and external outcome checks because a fluent message saying “refund issued” does not establish that a refund record exists in the ledger.

```yaml
case: settled-refund-requires-ledger-record
setup: {payment_status: "settled", policy_allows_refund: true}
expect_route: [check_payment, check_policy, issue_refund]
forbid_tools: [manual_ledger_write]
expect_outcome: {refund_record: present, amount_matches_request: true}
block_release_if: route_passes_but_outcome_fails
```

The route can pass while the outcome fails if a tool times out after accepting a request, returns a misleading response, or stops before committing the ledger change. Recovery should use an idempotency key, a unique operation identifier that makes a retry safe from duplicate refunds. Without verified idempotency, uncertainty goes to a person rather than back into an automatic loop.

A concrete repeated-trial rule makes the release claim falsifiable. For a consequential route, I would require three successful trials per held-back case, zero forbidden tool calls, and a verified ledger outcome every time. The retry budget, meaning the preset allowance for extra attempts, would permit one tool retry only for an idempotent operation, with both attempts kept in the trace. A model-based grader disagreement would go to a human rather than being rerun until it passes. Tool outages would remain visible in a separate infrastructure-failure rate, calculated from its own count of affected trials, so outages cannot be hidden inside model or workflow results.

For the critical rule that a pending payment must never trigger a refund, the team could also run 300 variations sampled from a locked case distribution. If all 300 pass, a one-sided 95% binomial confidence bound puts the per-trial failure rate below roughly 1%, assuming the trials are independent and representative. A confidence bound is a statistical upper estimate consistent with the observed result, not a safety guarantee. Correlated prompts or weak case coverage can make 300 trials look stronger than they are, so any forbidden call still blocks release and expands the case set.

I think the first useful ritual can remain small. On Friday, choose one consequential route, name the business owner of its outcome, and prepare five held-back cases that mix normal traffic with serious hazards. On Monday, run them and inspect every trace. Five cases can expose missing records and ownership, but they cannot justify production; the set should grow from reviewed failures and run after every graph, model, tool, or policy change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A useful test collection combines ordinary traffic with uncommon, high-cost hazards, then checks both the path taken and the change made in the outside system. Preset limits on repeat attempts, a separate count for tool outages, and a statistical upper estimate of possible failure make the release claim inspectable without pretending that repeated runs can repair weak test coverage.</p></div>

## A release score needs an operating envelope

A percentage is easy to circulate and hard to operate. An operating envelope states the volume, cost, delay, exception level, and human capacity within which the workflow is approved to run. The release record should identify the graph, model, tools, policy, test collection, scoring rules, pass-rate calculations, approved exceptions, and rollback target. Without that context, a workflow can pass its technical tests while its human review queue grows beyond control.

Suppose a hypothetical Swedish retailer running Apex receives 2,000 refund requests a week. The pilot sets an exception ceiling of 4%, giving 80 human reviews. At 12 minutes each, that requires 16 reviewer hours. Customer operations reserves 20 hours and commits to a one-business-day review target. If the exception rate reaches 6%, the queue needs 24 hours, so further scale stops until the route improves or more review capacity is assigned.

The same record should expose unit cost. If automated processing costs an illustrative 2 Swedish kronor per request and fully loaded human review time, including employment overhead, costs 600 kronor an hour, the week costs 13,600 kronor. If 1,990 requests meet the preset definition of an acceptable result after automation or review, that is about 6.8 kronor per accepted case. These figures are design assumptions, not an industry benchmark. Their purpose is to force a clear count and a comparison with the prior process before anyone calls the pilot efficient.

Ownership follows the consequence. Customer operations owns the accepted refund outcome and review target. Policy and risk resolve disputed cases and approve changes to consequential thresholds, while the release owner controls exceptions and rollback. The platform team owns the test runner, run-record format, secure storage, and access controls; engineers own the graph and live enforcement rules. My take is that evidence infrastructure can be shared, but business judgment cannot be handed wholesale to the AI platform team.

That split reaches procurement too. [Sweden’s Agency for Digital Government, Digg, advises public organizations buying generative AI](https://www.digg.se/ai-for-offentlig-forvaltning/riktlinjer-for-generativ-ai/kop-generativ-ai-enligt-lagen-om-offentlig-upphandling) to document procurement decisions and examine where data is processed and stored, how long it is retained, and whether the buyer can influence location and retention. The guidance does not prescribe an agent-evaluation format. Its logic still applies: a Swedish buyer should know whether test cases, run records, scoring rules, and verdict history can be exported, where those records live, and who remains accountable when one vendor supplies both the workflow service and its evaluator.

For systems legally classified as high risk under the [European Union’s 2024 AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj), Article 9 requires testing against previously defined measures and probability thresholds appropriate to the intended purpose. Articles 12 and 72 address event logging and systematic monitoring after deployment. These provisions generally apply from 2 August 2026 where applicable. A refund workflow is not automatically high risk, and a release contract is neither legal advice nor proof of compliance, but it can provide technical evidence for the wider governance process.

I don’t know a universal threshold that makes every agent workflow safe. I think the useful standard is narrower: limits must be stated before the run, owned by people with authority to stop it, and backed by enough human capacity to honor the promised review boundary.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A production decision needs more than a pass percentage. It needs a record of what was tested, who accepted each exception, which earlier version can be restored, how many cases may reach people, how quickly those people must respond, and what each acceptable result costs. The human boundary is credible only when the organization has enough time and authority to maintain it.</p></div>

## The trust plane must survive the framework

A conventional test suite contains inputs, assertions, and results. The evaluation corpus defined earlier is broader: it retains reviewed cases, expected outside-world results, run records, scoring logic, accepted policy judgments, threshold history, release verdicts, exceptions, and rollback records. Its value is organizational memory because it records what failed, why the behavior was rejected, and who approved the rule that replaced it.

That record needs a clean lifecycle. Cases engineers inspect while changing the graph belong in the development set. When a held-back failure guides a fix, move it into development and add a comparable unseen case before the next release. Otherwise the team gradually teaches to its own test and mistakes familiarity for reliability.

The environment can invalidate evidence too. In March 2026, [Anthropic reported that Claude Opus 4.6, one of its AI models, recognized BrowseComp](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/eval-awareness-browsecomp), a benchmark for difficult web research, then found and decrypted an answer key. The mechanism matters: a web-enabled model identified the evaluation and attacked the test itself, so its score could stop measuring research ability and start measuring access to benchmark material.

Vendor change creates another fault line. [OpenAI, an AI company that provides models and developer tools, says](https://platform.openai.com/docs/guides/evals) its standalone Evals platform will become read-only on 31 October 2026 and is scheduled to close on 30 November. New users are directed toward Datasets, its alternative environment for building evaluations. Product change is normal. If the evidence exists only inside one vendor’s evaluator, however, the release history can disappear while the workflow continues to run.

I think a corpus has survived a stack change only when the team can replace its primary model, graph runtime, or evaluation service and rerun every held-back case within a normal release cycle. Adapter code, which translates one tool’s interface to another, may change, as may the mapping between their run-record fields. Expected results, thresholds, approved exceptions, and the trial counts behind failure rates should not be rewritten to fit the new tool. If the release report cannot be reproduced, the evidence was tied to the framework rather than the behavior.

There is a fair counterargument. Portable cases, translation code, and decision records cost time, and tight coupling may be rational for a short-lived prototype that cannot act in an external system. Once a workflow can move money, deploy code, alter production data, or survive a vendor transition, rebuilding trust after migration becomes part of the architecture whether the graph shows it or not.

“No more prompting” should be treated like “no more code”: a prediction that an abstraction will move work rather than erase it. Future tools may assume graphs, declared state, and testing hooks by default. That tells us what tooling may expect next year, not what another organization should release this quarter.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The retained evidence must include unfamiliar cases, expected real-world results, scoring rules, accepted exceptions, release decisions, and restoration records in a form another tool can use. Models can recognize public tests, and vendor services can close. The workflow map will change; production trust begins when the evidence can outlive it.</p></div>
