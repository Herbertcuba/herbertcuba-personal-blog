A production alert can arrive with thousands of log lines, several dashboards, a deployment history, and a runbook, the documented response procedure. Giving all of it to one AI call feels thorough. It also forces the model to find a small signal inside a large pile of competing text.

A **[token](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/4936856-what-are-tokens-and-how-to-count-them)** is a small unit of text processed by a language model. A **[context window](https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/context-windows)** is the limited space for the call's instructions, retrieved evidence, tool results, history, and answer. **Context engineering** decides what occupies that space at each moment.

The subtle art is controlled omission. Give a decision enough evidence, keep a path back to the source, and create a test capable of showing that something important was left out.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Good context engineering reduces competition between useful evidence and everything a model could have been shown.</p></div>

## Begin with the work, not the graph

Greg Isenberg, CEO of Late Checkout, offers a useful separation in his August 2026 [video on graph engineering](https://youtu.be/JWhICz1QR8M). Prompt engineering concerns how the model is asked, context engineering concerns what it receives, and graph engineering concerns how work is arranged around it. The X account Movez's July 2026 slogan “[No more prompting](https://x.com/0xMovez/status/2081011657807925604)” therefore overstates the change: prompts remain inside the wider workflow.

| Layer | Incident-response decision |
|---|---|
| Prompt | How should this step classify the alert? |
| Context | Which log slice, change, and runbook section does it need? |
| Graph | What runs next, and who can halt an action? |

A **knowledge graph** records relationships among entities, such as services, owners, and dependencies. An **agent graph** maps a workflow made of bounded AI, software, and human steps. They can work together, but they describe different things.

Isenberg's maturity ladder starts before orchestration, meaning software that runs and routes the steps:

1. Perform the incident workflow manually until its real decisions appear.
2. Save file artifacts at stable handoffs, so another step can inspect the evidence and result.
3. Automate those handoffs only when their boundaries are clear.

An incident summary can carry timestamps, source links, and open questions forward. The next step retrieves the cited log range if needed instead of receiving the full transcript again.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Expose the manual handoffs first, then automate a workflow whose context boundaries already make sense.</p></div>

## Split only where context changes

A **node** performs one job. An **edge** connects jobs, a **transition** moves execution between them, and **conditional routing** chooses an edge from the current result. **Shared state** is the compact information later nodes can use, such as incident ID, severity, evidence references, and proposed action.

```text
                   ┌─ inspect logs ─────┐
alert ── fan-out ──┤                    ├─ merge ─ skeptic ─ contain / human gate
                   └─ inspect changes ──┘
```

Alert intake followed by classification is sequential. Sending the case to both inspection nodes is parallel **fan-out**; combining their outputs is a **merge**. That split-and-rejoin shape is a **diamond topology**. A **checker node** verifies that another node followed a rule, while a **skeptic node** looks for evidence against the proposed diagnosis. Each branch receives its own evidence rather than a copy of the whole incident record.

This is the smallest-useful-graph discipline: add a node when a separate decision, context boundary, or authority makes it necessary. Extra boxes are easy to draw, but every box creates another route to tune and observe.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Split work where evidence or authority changes, and keep shared state smaller than the material it points to.</p></div>

## Context also has a lifespan

**Persistent state** allows one incident run to resume after an interruption. Longer-lived **memory** carries selected information across different incidents. A confirmed failure signature may deserve that longer life; temporary reasoning and stale runbook text may not.

Human gates should rise with consequence. Gathering diagnostics can proceed automatically, isolating a production service may need an on-call engineer, and an irreversible action should require explicit authority. The human needs cited evidence and uncertainty, not a replay of every model call.

That is why [APEX, the Agentic Production Execution operating model](/posts/2026-04-05-apex-framework/), includes a Reflection phase. The hard part is adjusting context, routes, and stop conditions after outcomes reveal where the graph is weak.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Retention and human review are context decisions too: preserve what supports recovery, and escalate as consequences grow.</p></div>

## Test the omissions

Isenberg's video explains how to design work around AI, but it contains no discussion of evaluations. The next step needs a separate source. A checker node examines one incident run; an **evaluation set** repeats that check across cases, including **held-back cases** excluded from the graph's design.

Lance Martin, then at AI tooling company LangChain and now at model developer Anthropic, taught the 2024 *Architecting and Testing Controllable Agents* workshop at the AI Engineer World's Fair, a conference for AI builders. A transcript audit finds evaluation throughout the workshop. In July 2026, the X account Jey compressed that emphasis into a useful [15/105 reading](https://x.com/0xJeyx/status/2081364222856831271): about 15 minutes for the graph and 105 for testing. It is an interpretation, not the official agenda.

Anthropic's [guidance on agent evaluations](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) recommends beginning with 20 to 50 tasks grounded in real failures. For incident response, those cases might contain a stale runbook, misleading alert, missing telemetry, harmless deployment, or dangerous action proposed with high confidence.

A graph without an evaluation set is a diagram with opinions. Whenever context engineering removes tokens from a node, name the unfamiliar case that could prove the removal unsafe.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A checker reviews one run; held-back evaluation cases test whether selective context still works beyond the incidents that shaped it.</p></div>
