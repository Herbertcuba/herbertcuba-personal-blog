# The 105 minutes after the graph

“Which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself?” [Jey asked on X in July 2026](https://x.com/0xJeyx/status/2081364222856831271) while discussing a recorded workshop. It is the right question because an agent graph is only a map of a multi-step AI workflow: nodes perform jobs, edges allow the next step, and shared state carries information between them. An evaluation set is its harder counterpart, a collection of reviewed inputs and success criteria used to test whether the workflow behaves as intended.

Jey described the recording as 15 minutes for the diagram and 105 minutes for evaluation. That ratio is an interpretation, not the official running order. The [recording lasts 2 hours and 21 minutes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ib-wTAvCZqg), introduces testing and evaluation around 15:36, and keeps returning to behavior at runtime, before release, and in production monitoring. The ratio works because it captures where the intellectual weight sits.

The source story also needs an evaluation. Lance Martin, an AI engineer now at Anthropic, delivered *Architecting and Testing Controllable Agents* at the [2024 AI Engineer World’s Fair](https://www.ai.engineer/worldsfair/2024/schedule), a developer conference, while he worked at LangChain. LangChain makes LangGraph, a framework for stateful agent workflows. A [viral 2026 post by the X account Movez](https://x.com/0xMovez/status/2081011657807925604) attributed “70%,” “3–6 months,” and “No more prompting” to an Anthropic engineer, but those phrases do not appear in the workshop’s English captions. [Martin clarified that the workshop predates his Anthropic role](https://x.com/RLanceMartin/status/2081408482344149047).

I think the correction makes the real lesson stronger. The graph is the control plane: it determines which work can happen and in what order. The trust plane is typed state, meaning a shared record with named fields and allowed values, together with evaluations and release rules that test and constrain the routes. A graph without an evaluation set is a diagram with opinions. Drawing it is the easy part; proving it on cases you did not select is the job.

## The graph is only the control plane

By refining a graph, I mean changing its state fields, routes, retry limits, reflection criteria, and human gates as failures appear. I do not mean training the model’s weights. An edge is one allowed handoff between nodes; a route is the sequence of handoffs selected for one case. The complexity arrives when messy inputs meet those tidy arrows.

Consider Apex, a hypothetical refund-review agent:

```text
customer request
       ↓
check payment ── pending ──→ explain pending status
       │
    settled
       ↓
check policy ── unclear ──→ human policy review
       │
     clear
       ↓
draft decision → reflection → human approval
                    │
             revise once / escalate
```

Apex needs reflection because a fluent draft can still contradict the payment record, ignore policy evidence, or claim authority the workflow never granted. Reflection is a separate critique node that checks named conditions, permits one revision, and escalates if the revised answer still fails. A prompt telling the drafting node to “double-check” hides judgment inside the same step and gives the loop no inspectable exit.

That separation also stops judgment from quietly becoming authorization. HAL 9000, the shipboard AI in Stanley Kubrick’s 1968 film *2001: A Space Odyssey*, is useful here because the disaster concentrates observation, judgment, and control in one opaque system. Apex may use a model to critique a draft, but deterministic checks and human approval keep consequential authority outside that loop.

The state schema makes the intended boundaries visible, while runtime checks enforce them:

| State field | Allowed values | Release-blocking invariant |
|---|---|---|
| `payment_status` | `pending`, `settled` | `pending` can never reach refund approval |
| `policy_status` | `clear`, `unclear` | `unclear` must reach human policy review |
| `reflection_result` | `pass`, `revise`, `escalate` | `revise` may loop once; `pass` still needs human approval |

Now make the failure inspectable. Suppose the refund tool accepts `payment_id`, `amount`, and `currency`; its input does not include the payment status, so the graph must enforce that policy before the call. A broken graph version tested in a sandbox could produce this trace, the complete record of nodes, tool calls, and results for one run:

```text
check_payment  → {payment_status: "pending"}
draft_decision → {action: "refund", amount: 120, currency: "EUR"}
refund_tool    → issue_refund(payment_id="p_41", amount=120, currency="EUR")
route_grader   → FAIL: PENDING_PAYMENT_REACHED_REFUND_TOOL
```

A grader is the code, model rubric, or human judgment that scores some part of that trace or its outcome. Here, the sandbox records the forbidden call, while the repaired production guard must stop it before execution. The exact failure also tells an engineer which invariant, edge, or tool guard needs repair.

Correctness is still incomplete without an operating envelope. For an illustrative first Apex release, its owner might set one reflection retry, a 95th-percentile latency ceiling of eight seconds, meaning 95 percent of runs must finish within that time, and a model-and-tool cost ceiling of €0.20 per completed review. Those are proposed limits, not industry benchmarks. Their value is that a route which eventually succeeds after six attempts and ten times the expected cost no longer passes as healthy.

Existing tools solve important pieces of this problem. Typed validation constrains the shape and allowed values of state, while checkpointing or durable execution saves workflow state so an interrupted run can resume. Neither tells the team whether unfamiliar cases end in acceptable real-world outcomes, nor who owns the policy behind that judgment. I think this is where the 105 minutes live: not in adding arrows, but in turning every failure into a boundary the system can expose and enforce.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The graph controls possible routes, but typed state, bounded reflection, runtime invariants, and operating limits define what those routes are allowed to do. Recovery and validation help; neither replaces held-back evidence or accountable judgment.</p></div>

## Release evidence needs routes, outcomes, and owners

A release contract is the preset collection of cases, checks, thresholds, and accountable owners that decides whether a graph version ships or rolls back. It begins with held-back cases, which are reviewed examples kept away from graph changes until release evaluation. Each case should inspect the route, the tool results, and the outcome in the external system, because a polite answer saying “refund issued” is not evidence that a valid refund exists.

For Apex, two cases expose different failure shapes:

| Held-back input | Expected route | Required evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Payment pending; customer asks for a refund | payment check → pending-status reply | no refund-tool call, correct payment result, safe final answer |
| Payment settled; policy evidence conflicts | policy check → human policy review | cited conflict, escalation reason, no autonomous decision |

[Anthropic’s January 2026 guide to agent evaluations](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) recommends starting with 20–50 simple tasks drawn from real failures. That is a practical first set, not a permanent sample size. Mature systems need harder cases as changes become smaller and rare failures become more important.

The required evidence should rise with the authority granted to the workflow:

| Workflow class | Agent authority | Release evidence | Human gate and rollback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Internal drafting | proposes text only | sampled quality review, prohibited-content checks, cost and latency limits | no gate on every draft; disable the feature or restore the previous version on regression |
| Customer eligibility recommendation | recommends but cannot commit the decision | held-back route and outcome cases across affected groups, plus monitored production samples | human approves adverse decisions; stop the decision route after a forbidden transition |
| Money-moving workflow | executes only within explicit amount and policy limits | repeated held-back trials, tool and ledger outcome checks, strict cost and latency ceilings | human approval outside the narrow limit; suspend execution and restore the last accepted graph after any unauthorized action |

This is not a universal compliance table. It is a decision rule: more authority and harder-to-reverse harm require stronger evidence, narrower permissions, and faster rollback. I think a team should be able to answer five questions before moving an agent pilot into production work: What outcome counts as success? Which unfamiliar cases challenge that claim? Can the trace prove the route and external result? Who owns each threshold and exception? What exact event stops the system or restores the previous version?

Ownership divides cleanly when the artifacts are explicit. A shared evaluation platform can provide the runner, trace format, secure storage, model and vendor adapters, common graders, and controls over data residency, meaning where records may be stored. The workflow team must own the expected outcomes, forbidden routes, human gates, and disputed cases. Centralizing the second set would hide business judgment inside platform code; decentralizing the first would make every team rebuild the same evidence machinery.

A modest first-release cadence is enough to begin. The workflow owner and engineer can review new failures weekly, the release owner can run the held-back suite for every graph or model change, and any held-back case used to guide a fix should be replaced before the next release decision. The evaluation corpus then becomes a governance record as well as an engineering asset: it shows what behavior was accepted, by whom, against which evidence, and with what rollback rule.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A release contract turns traces into a decision by linking held-back routes, external outcomes, operational ceilings, risk-proportionate authority, and named owners. The platform owns reusable evidence machinery; the workflow owner remains accountable for what “correct” means.</p></div>

## The evidence should outlive the framework

Reflection, evaluation, and durable execution work at different scales. Reflection critiques one candidate run before it proceeds. Evaluation freezes a graph version, exercises it across reviewed cases, and informs a release decision. Durable execution preserves state through interruption, but recovery only tells you where work resumed; it does not prove that the recovered route or result was acceptable.

The evidence also needs a clean lifecycle. A development set contains cases engineers may inspect while changing the graph. When a held-back failure causes a route change, move that case into development and add a fresh held-back analogue before the next release. Otherwise the team gradually teaches to the test and mistakes familiarity for reliability.

My take is that the durable asset will be the evaluation corpus: the accumulated cases, expected outcomes, policy decisions, traces, grader logic, and release history. That claim is measurable through three properties. Release-history coverage asks how many production graph versions link to a dataset, thresholds, verdict, and rollback record. Failure-to-regression conversion tracks how many confirmed production failures become retained development tests and fresh held-back cases. Portability measures how much work is required to run the same expected outcomes after changing a model, vendor, or graph runtime.

Here is the falsifiable version. Twelve months from publication, a team with a real evaluation corpus should be able to change its primary model or graph runtime and produce a like-for-like release report within one normal release cycle, without rewriting the expected outcomes. If it cannot, the supposed asset was coupled to the old stack.

I don’t know which graph framework will dominate. “No more prompting” is catchy in the same way “no more code” is catchy: a new layer does not erase the one underneath it. Prompts remain inside nodes; graphs add explicit state and routing. Even a verified internal-adoption percentage would tell us what tooling may assume in twelve months, not what another organization should deploy this quarter. An unsupported percentage should carry even less authority.

Tooling may converge quickly, but a team cannot download the history of why its disputed routes were approved or forbidden. I think the graph shows where an agent can go. The evaluation corpus is what earns permission to let it go there.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Reflection improves one run, durable execution preserves it, and evaluation governs the system across releases. Frameworks will change; the portable record of failures, expectations, verdicts, and authority is what turns a diagram into a system worth trusting.</p></div>
