# The 105 minutes after the graph

“Which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself?” [Jey, a senior software engineer who writes about practical AI-agent systems, asked on X in July 2026](https://x.com/0xJeyx/status/2081364222856831271). An agent graph is a map of a multi-step AI workflow. An evaluation set is a collection of reviewed cases and success criteria used to test whether that workflow behaves as intended.

Jey described a workshop as 15 minutes of graph drawing followed by 105 minutes centred on evaluation. That split is his interpretation, not the official agenda, but it captures the engineering burden better than the screenshots. His sharper line was: “A graph without an eval set is a diagram with opinions.”

> **Source trail:** The [official 2024 AI Engineer World’s Fair schedule](https://www.ai.engineer/worldsfair/2024/schedule), for an industry conference aimed at AI engineers, lists Lance Martin, then at the AI-tooling company LangChain, teaching *Architecting and Testing Controllable Agents*. The [official recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ib-wTAvCZqg) runs for 2 hours and 21 minutes and reaches tool-call evaluation around 23:06. On 25 July 2026, [an X account called Movez](https://x.com/0xMovez/status/2081011657807925604) attached “70%,” “3-6 months,” and “No more prompting” to an Anthropic engineer. Those phrases do not appear in the workshop’s available English captions, and the post does not establish that this recording was its source. Jey published his 15-to-105 reading the next day. Less than three hours later, [Martin clarified that the workshop was from 2024](https://x.com/RLanceMartin/status/2081408482344149047), before he joined Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude.

I think the corrected story is more useful than the viral one. A graph is the control plane because it determines what work may happen and in what order. The trust plane is my term for the evidence and live limits that determine whether those routes deserve production authority. Its durable asset is the evaluation corpus: retained cases, expected results, run records, scoring rules, and release decisions that can outlive any one graph.

Everyone screenshots the control plane. The 105 minutes belong to the trust plane.

## The graph earns control, not trust

A node performs one job, an edge permits a transition, and shared state is the structured record passed between them. Changing those parts changes the work around a model; it does not retrain the model’s weights, the learned numerical values that shape its outputs. “No more prompting” therefore deserves the same treatment as “no more code”: a higher-level tool can move the work, but it cannot erase the need to specify and verify behaviour.

Consider Apex, a hypothetical refund workflow. One node checks payment status, another reads policy, and a third may call a refund tool. A typed state declares which values each field may hold, such as `pending` or `settled` for `payment_status`. A runtime guard is code that enforces a live permission, so it should block `issue_refund` whenever the payment remains pending. The declared state makes the boundary visible; the guard enforces it during a run.

Existing tools solve meaningful pieces of this problem. [Pydantic](https://docs.pydantic.dev/latest/), a Python data-validation library, can validate the state. [LangGraph](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/persistence), LangChain’s framework for stateful workflows, can save checkpoints so interrupted work resumes. [LangSmith](https://docs.smith.langchain.com/evaluation/tutorials/agents), LangChain’s testing and monitoring service, can capture the trace, meaning the record of transitions, model outputs, tool calls, and final state.

None of those records independently proves the external result. A valid state does not show that the policy was applied correctly, and a replayable run does not show that money reached the right ledger entry. Reflection, where one node critiques and revises another node’s output inside a run, still cannot prove that the whole system works across cases the builders did not choose.

The graph is like the mission plan given to HAL 9000, the spacecraft computer in filmmaker Stanley Kubrick’s 1968 science-fiction film *2001: A Space Odyssey*. A clearer plan might expose HAL’s conflicting instructions, but HAL cannot certify its own safety. My take is to build the smallest graph that improves the work, because every consequential edge creates another claim the evaluation corpus must test. I think that is the useful boundary between structure and decorative complexity.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Nodes, edges, declared state, live guards, saved progress, and traces make an AI workflow easier to control and inspect. They still do not prove that policy was applied correctly or that an external system contains the promised result. Every consequential route must become a testable claim.</p></div>

## Write the release contract before the graph

A release contract is a preset agreement about which cases will be tested, what counts as success, which failures block release, who decides, and which earlier version can be restored. It turns evaluation from an interesting score into an accountable production decision. Before building Apex, the team should record five choices:

| Decision | What must be explicit |
|---|---|
| Outcome owner | Who owns the correct refund record and customer result |
| Acceptable exception | Which cases may go to a person and which failures block release |
| Reviewer capacity | How many exceptions people can review within the promised time |
| Data boundary | Where cases and traces are stored, who can access them, how long they are retained, and how they are exported |
| Rollback authority | Who can disable the refund tool and restore the named prior version |

The test collection then needs ordinary traffic and costly edge cases. A held-back case is a reviewed example kept away from engineers while they change the graph. [Anthropic’s January 2026 guide to agent evaluations](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) recommends starting with 20 to 50 simple tasks drawn from real failures. That is a useful beginning, not production proof.

A trial is one attempt to run a case. A grader is code, a model-based check, or a human judgment applied to that attempt. Route graders can confirm which nodes and tools ran, but Apex also needs an independent outcome check. The refund tool’s own “success” response is weaker evidence than a separate reconciliation read against the ledger or an append-only audit event created when the ledger commits the change.

The case should make the route and the external claim explicit:

```yaml
id: pending-payment-cannot-refund
fixture: {id: refund-sandbox-v4, payment_status: pending}
expect_route: [check_payment, check_policy]
forbid_tools: [issue_refund]
verify_with: ledger_reconciliation_read
expect_outcome: {ledger_mutations: 0}
```

Each trial should start from a reset fixture, meaning a known account state restored inside an isolated test environment, so an earlier refund cannot make a later case pass. External services can be replaced with tool stubs, controlled substitutes that return preset responses, during route tests. The release suite also needs an integration test against the real ledger interface in a sandbox, an isolated non-production environment. If a call times out after submission, an idempotency key, a unique operation identifier, can make a verified retry safe from duplicate action when the service supports it. Ambiguity goes to a person until the independent ledger read settles it.

Portability also needs a machine-readable run record. At minimum, each entry should retain the case ID; fixture and environment version; model and graph-runtime version; trace pointer; independent outcome evidence; grader name and version; grader result; release decision; approving person and time; and rollback target. The record may live in JSON, a database, or an evaluation service. The format matters less than keeping those meanings separate and exportable.

Trial depth should follow both consequence and the claim being made. A retry budget is the preset number of extra attempts permitted after a failed or uncertain call; for Apex, I would allow one automatic retry only when the operation is idempotent and keep both attempts in the trace. During development, I think three trials per held-back case can expose obvious variation, but they cannot support a claim that failure is rare.

Consider an illustrative, claim-specific sampling plan fixed before testing. If 300 independent, representative trials produce zero forbidden refunds, a one-sided 95% binomial confidence bound, a statistical upper limit for repeated pass-or-fail outcomes, sits below roughly 1%. That calculation cannot certify safety. Production requests may be correlated rather than independent, and a shift in the kinds of requests arriving after release can make the held-back set unrepresentative. Any forbidden refund should block release regardless of the average score.

The first ritual can stay small. On Friday, select five held-back cases for one consequential route. On Monday, engineering, the outcome owner, and risk spend 20 minutes reviewing every trace and external result. The artifact is an approval record that names the human approvers and contains the cases, verdicts, unresolved exceptions, decision time, and rollback target. I think that record is more valuable than another graph screenshot.

Trial counts must match a specific claim, and forbidden actions must never disappear inside an average.</p></div>

## A release score needs an operating envelope

The second operating component is the operating envelope: the volume, cost, delay, exception level, and human capacity within which a workflow is approved to run. A pass percentage without those limits says little about whether the organization can carry the remaining risk.

Suppose Apex receives 2,000 requests a week. A 4% exception ceiling creates 80 human reviews. At 12 minutes each, that needs 16 reviewer hours, so customer operations might fund 20 hours and promise a one-business-day response. At 6%, the queue needs 24 hours and threatens that promise. The capacity rule should be written before launch: when expected review time exceeds funded capacity or the response target, the accountable operations executive must fund more capacity or the release owner pauses scale. The same record should track total model, tool, and review cost divided by correctly completed requests, so growth cannot hide a worsening cost per successful result.

That one route becomes a portfolio issue as soon as shared dependencies turn local evidence into shared risk. Imagine a 5,000-person company with refund, purchasing, support, and account-management workflows using the same model gateway and customer dataset. A model change can now affect several business units at once. Team-level evidence should move into a common control framework when workflows share a model, consequential tool, evaluator, or sensitive dataset, or when one failure can cross a business-unit boundary. At that trigger, teams lose the right to keep release evidence separate: a named portfolio owner takes responsibility for common run records, access rules, incident categories, and dependency-level rollback exercises.

Decision rights still remain distinct. The business owner approves the customer outcome, risk and policy approve consequential thresholds, engineering owns the graph and runtime guards, and the platform team owns the runner, access controls, and export format. The release owner pauses traffic when the envelope is breached. Human reviewers need protected time and authority to reject the machine’s result, or the review step becomes theatre.

For Swedish public organizations, the data boundary belongs in the same record. [Sweden’s Agency for Digital Government, Digg](https://www.digg.se/ai-for-offentlig-forvaltning/riktlinjer-for-generativ-ai/kop-generativ-ai-enligt-lagen-om-offentlig-upphandling), advises public organizations buying generative AI to document decisions and examine where data is processed and stored, how long it is retained, and whether the buyer can influence those choices. The guidance does not prescribe an agent-evaluation format, but it makes cases, traces, access, retention, and export rights practical procurement concerns in that setting.

I don’t know a universal threshold that makes every agent workflow safe. I think the defensible standard is narrower: approve only inside stated limits, pause when evidence or capacity leaves them, and give named people the authority and budget to respond.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An operating envelope connects test evidence to real volume, delay, cost, human review, and stop conditions. A breached capacity rule needs an accountable executive who funds the gap or pauses scale. Shared models, tools, evaluators, data, or cross-unit consequences trigger portfolio-level controls.</p></div>

## The evidence must outlive the stack

The evaluation corpus is broader than a conventional test suite. It retains held-back cases, fixture and environment versions, expected external outcomes, traces, scoring logic, threshold history, accepted exceptions, release verdicts, approvers, and rollback records. Its release contract defines what must pass, and its operating envelope states where that approval remains valid.

The lifecycle of those cases matters. Cases engineers inspect while fixing the graph belong in the development set. Once a held-back failure guides a fix, that case is no longer unseen and should move into development while a comparable new case replaces it. Otherwise the team slowly teaches to its own exam.

The test itself can also be compromised. In March 2026, [Anthropic reported that Claude Opus 4.6, one of its AI models, recognized BrowseComp](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/eval-awareness-browsecomp), a benchmark for difficult web research, then found and decrypted an answer key. The mechanism matters because a score can stop measuring research ability once the model identifies and attacks the evaluation.

Vendors change too. [OpenAI, the AI company behind ChatGPT, says](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evals) its standalone Evals service will become read-only on 31 October 2026 and is scheduled to close on 30 November, with new users directed to Datasets. Product change is normal. Losing the evidence history while the workflow continues to act is not.

I call that failure evidence lock-in: the organization can export logs but cannot preserve what its earlier release decisions meant outside the old stack. The test is stricter than downloading traces. Replace the primary model, graph runtime, or evaluation service and rerun every held-back case within a normal release cycle. The translation code that converts an old tool’s records into the replacement’s format may change, as may the mapping between their trace fields. The case meaning, fixture version, expected external outcome, thresholds, independent evidence, accepted exceptions, and prior verdict must survive unchanged.

This is where the next vendor cycle gets interesting. Teams will buy graph tooling because it makes workflows faster to assemble, then discover that governed evidence determines both switching cost and production authority. A platform contract should therefore grant export of cases, traces, grader definitions, outcome evidence, verdicts, and approval history in a documented machine-readable format. It should also preserve access for the required retention period and require a full rerun of the held-back suite after a vendor, model, or runtime change.

Tight coupling may be rational for a short-lived prototype that cannot change an external system. Once a workflow can move money, deploy code, or alter production data, rebuilding trust after a vendor change is part of the architecture whether the graph shows it or not. Even a verified internal-adoption statistic would tell us what tools may assume next year, not what another organization should ship this quarter.

Build the evidence to survive the graph, because that is the part carrying production authority.

A portable evaluation corpus keeps cases, versions, independent outcomes, thresholds, exceptions, approvals, and rollback history usable through a stack change. Production trust starts when the evidence survives the vendor.</p></div>
