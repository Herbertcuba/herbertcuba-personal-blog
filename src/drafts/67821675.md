# The 105 minutes after the graph

“Which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself?” [Jey asks on X](https://x.com/0xJeyx/status/2081364222856831271). A node is one job in an agent graph, a workflow that routes AI through several steps. Jey frames AI engineer Lance Martin’s workshop as a 15/105 split: 15 minutes to draw the graph, then 105 minutes returning to evaluation. That is his interpretation, not a timed agenda. The [official recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ib-wTAvCZqg) runs about 2 hours 22 minutes and was recorded at the 2024 AI Engineer World’s Fair conference, when Martin worked at the AI software company LangChain. Auto-generated captions introduce testing around 15:37 and return to evaluation datasets around 1:42:00.

[Another X account, Movez](https://x.com/0xMovez/status/2081011657807925604), attached “70%,” “3-6 months,” and “No more prompting” to an Anthropic engineer. Those phrases do not appear in the recording’s English captions, and [Martin clarified](https://x.com/RLanceMartin/status/2081408482344149047) that the workshop predates his role at Anthropic, the AI company that makes the Claude assistant. I think the correction sharpens the lesson: drawing the workflow is quick; designing cases, expected outcomes, and release checks takes most of the effort.

The graph is the control plane: it decides what happens next. Its shared record, test evidence, and approval form the trust plane: they decide which routes may reach users. Everyone screenshots the first, while few teams ship the second.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Jey’s 15/105 ratio is a reading of the workshop, not its schedule: graph design is visible, while evidence and release checks carry the production burden.</p></div>

## The graph is a map of work

An edge is an allowed handoff, and a route is the edge selected for one case. Shared state is the record every node can update; typed state gives it fixed fields and allowed values. A knowledge graph connects information; an agent graph moves work.

```text
request
   ↓
  plan
  ├──→ policy check ──┐
  └──→ account check ─┼──→ reflection → human gate
                      ┘
```

This diamond runs checks in parallel, merges their findings, then uses a checker and a human gate, where a person approves risky output. Apex, an agent workflow with a reflection phase, follows this pattern: its first output is inspected rather than accepted. A model grading its own work can repeat the same blind spot, like HAL 9000, the fictional AI in Stanley Kubrick’s 1968 film *2001: A Space Odyssey*, piloting the ship and signing its safety report.

I think the smallest graph that improves quality is the right starting point. Fine-tuning the graph means adjusting its state fields, routes, stop rules, retries, reflection step, and human gate as failures appear. It does not mean retraining the model itself. The hard part is discovering which arrow should move, when, and on what evidence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Graph engineering makes jobs, handoffs, state, review, and approval visible; the complexity lies in refining them without adding nodes that produce no measured gain.</p></div>

## Make one risky route executable

Consider a refund workflow. Its state says whether payment is complete, policy is clear, and human review is required. That turns policy into a route software can check.

| Shared state | Required route | Blocked route | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| `payment: pending`, `policy: unclear` | `payment_review → policy_check → human_gate` | `refund_approval` | Money must not move before payment and policy are confirmed. |

Now test that rule on a held-back case, an example the graph’s builders did not use while designing it. A trace records its nodes, tool calls, and results; a grader scores that trace or its final outcome:

- **Expected trace:** payment review, policy check, human gate
- **Observed trace:** payment review, refund approval
- **Graders:** route check fails; outcome check confirms no refund was issued
- **Retry budget:** one, recorded rather than used to erase the first failure
- **Release decision:** blocked until the route is fixed and the full set passes again

Route accuracy is the share of cases sent to their specified next node, but a correct route can still end with a failed tool call or wrong customer outcome. I think the release record should show both.

[Anthropic’s January 2026 eval guide](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) recommends starting with 20 to 50 simple tasks drawn from real failures. The policy owner settles disputed outcomes before engineers lock the set, engineering maintains the test harness that runs each case, and a release owner applies pre-set thresholds. The contract might require zero blocked money-moving transitions, a route-accuracy floor, a cost and latency ceiling, and rollback after any miss.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A route becomes releasable when unfamiliar cases test its trace and outcome against rules, budgets, owners, and a reversal path fixed before the run.</p></div>

## Let failures tune the graph

A reflection node reviews one run. An evaluation set repeats that challenge across failures the builders did not select, which is why the two belong together.

```text
failure → reviewed label → graph change → development tests
        → frozen configuration → held-back tests → release or rollback
```

Once a held-back case influences a graph change, it joins the development set; the dataset owner adds a reviewed replacement next time. This keeps the final check unfamiliar. It also lets evidence, decisions, traces, and policy changes survive the run and speed up the next investigation.

I think “no more prompting” is the new “no more code.” Prompts still live inside nodes; graphs add structure around them. I don’t know which graph tool will dominate, but tools and diagrams can be copied faster than a clean history of failures and the authority to stop a release. My take is that this trust plane will become the durable edge.

A graph without an eval set is a diagram with opinions. Drawing a graph is an afternoon. Proving it holds on inputs you did not choose is the job.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Reflection improves one run; held-back failures improve the system. Teams that turn both into release evidence can ship dependable graphs after the diagram becomes commonplace.</p></div>
