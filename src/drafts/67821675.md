Which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself?

An engineer named Jey asked that on X last week, after watching a two-hour workshop on agent graphs. I think it is the sharpest question anyone has asked about AI engineering this year. Almost nobody heard it, because the same workshop went viral for a completely different reason.

## What actually went viral

The internet passed around a clip and skipped the workshop.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A hype account clipped a Lance Martin workshop from 2024 and framed it as fresh news from inside Anthropic. The spicy numbers are a paraphrase of a paraphrase. The workshop itself says something much more useful.</p></div>

The timeline matters, because the viral version mangles it. Lance Martin, who built much of LangChain's agent documentation and now works at Anthropic, gave a free two-hour workshop on agent graphs at the AI Engineer World's Fair in 2024, before he joined Anthropic. Last week a hype account called Movez clipped the talk and pulled in over half a million views by framing it as current news from inside the lab. Martin laughed about it publicly: the talk is a couple of years old.

The clip's claim: 70% of Anthropic engineers are using self-improving loops, and in three to six months we will all be building graphs to orchestrate self-improving agents. No more prompting.

I tried to verify that number. Movez says 70%. Another widely shared writeup of the same clip says 80%. When two summaries of the same talk disagree on the headline stat, you are looking at a paraphrase of a paraphrase. I don't know what the real figure is, and neither does the account that posted it. What I do know is that a 2024 talk sold as "inside Anthropic, right now" is the exact frame the clip pushed and the facts do not support.

Jey watched the whole thing instead of the clip. His observation is the spine of this article: the graph diagram is finished in the first fifteen minutes. The remaining 105 minutes keep returning to evaluation. The workshop ends with building eval datasets for the graph.

Everyone screenshotted the first fifteen minutes. The other 105 are the story.

## A graph is an afternoon

An agent graph is a flowchart your program actually executes. Nodes do the work. Edges decide what happens next.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>With LangGraph you can sketch a working agent graph in an afternoon. That speed is exactly why the eval matters so much: when the visible artifact is cheap, it feels like the work is done. The work has not started.</p></div>

Some definitions, because the jargon wall is real if you are early in your career. I will use one example throughout: a customer support agent for a small software company.

For our support agent, one node classifies the incoming ticket, one drafts an answer, one decides whether to escalate to a human. LangGraph is a Python library from LangChain for building these graphs. Its main class is StateGraph. You declare a shared piece of state, a bundle of data that flows from node to node, and StateGraph manages the handoffs. You define that state with a TypedDict, a plain Python way of saying "this bundle has a ticket text field, a category field, and a draft answer field, and here is the type of each." If a node writes to a field that does not exist, you find out before the graph runs.

One more term you will meet everywhere: ReAct. It is a pattern where the model alternates between reasoning about what to do next and acting, usually calling a tool, in a loop until the job is done. A graph is what you get when you stop hoping one long ReAct loop behaves and start drawing the loop yourself, with explicit branches.

That is maybe four hours of learning and an afternoon of building. I think this is genuinely wonderful. It is also the trap.

## The other 105 minutes

Evaluation is where the workshop spends its real time, and where your system proves it deserves production traffic.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An eval set is a collection of inputs with known correct outcomes, used to test the graph. If every input in it came from your own head, the set only proves the graph survives your imagination.</p></div>

An eval set sounds abstract, so here is the concrete version for the support agent. You pull two hundred real past tickets where you already know the right outcome. This one should have been classified as billing. This one should have been escalated. This draft answer was wrong and a human rewrote it. Then you run the graph against all two hundred and score it.

The hard part is honesty about coverage. Your eval set needs the tickets you would rather avoid: angry one-liners with no punctuation, a message in two languages at once, a user who is confidently wrong about how your product works, a case where the correct answer is to say no. If your set is five tidy examples you wrote during development, you have shown the graph its own crib sheet.

This is the failure mode I see most in junior teams. The graph looks sophisticated in a demo. The eval is an afterthought. Then production traffic arrives, full of inputs nobody imagined, and the agent fails quietly by producing plausible, wrong output. Plausibility is what makes it dangerous.

<div class="scifi">
<span class="scifi__label">Meanwhile, in science fiction</span>
<p class="scifi__film">Blade Runner, the Voight-Kampff test</p>
<p>In <em>Blade Runner</em>, the Voight-Kampff machine exists because you cannot tell a replicant by looking. It asks questions designed to push the subject outside anything it was prepared for, and measures what happens under that pressure. The whole point is that the test inputs come from outside the subject's own design.</p>
<p>Your eval set is the same instrument. A graph that only ever meets inputs its builder wrote is a replicant that has only ever met its own designer. It will pass every time, right up until it meets the world.</p>
</div>

Back to Jey's question. Which node in your graph has never met an input you did not write yourself? For most systems I have reviewed, the honest answer is: all of them.

## What the 70% is actually telling you

Even a garbled number carries a signal, if you read it at the right distance.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Frontier-lab adoption tells you what tooling will assume in twelve months. Your plan this quarter is smaller: one workflow, one graph, one honest eval set.</p></div>

Say the real figure is somewhere near what the clip claims. Engineers inside a frontier lab are building self-improving loops on graphs. That tells you the frameworks will converge on graph-native abstractions, and the eval tooling around them will get much better, fast. It is a weather report for next year.

It is a bad shopping list for this quarter. The gap between "what a frontier lab assumes internally" and "what a normal team can run safely in production" is, in my experience, one to two years. The people inside those labs are paid to hit the failure modes first. You want to adopt after the postmortems are public.

The "no more prompting" line deserves the same treatment: take it seriously and deflate it at once. It is the new "no more code," and we have heard that song before. Prompting does not disappear. It becomes one node among many, graded by the same eval set as everything else. I think the skill that matters is designing systems where every step's output can be checked.

This connects to something I have written about typed state in agent systems. The graph is the control plane: it routes the work. Types and evals are the trust plane: they define what correct means. A router with no idea what a good packet looks like is just a faster way to be wrong.

The practical move is small and boring. Pick one workflow you already run. Draw the graph; it will take you an afternoon, just like the workshop's first fifteen minutes. Then spend your real time on the eval set: real inputs, known outcomes, the cases you did not choose.

## The question to keep

The ratio in that workshop is the ratio in the industry.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Fifteen minutes of architecture, 105 minutes of verification. The teams that internalize that ratio will quietly outrun the ones collecting diagrams.</p></div>

I think the teams that pull ahead over the next year will not have the prettiest graphs. They will have the most honest eval sets, and the habit of building them before anyone asks.

Jey put the whole thing in one line: a graph without an eval set is a diagram with opinions. So when you finish your next diagram, and you will finish it faster than you expect, ask his question. Which node here has never met an input you did not write yourself?

Then go build the set that introduces them.
