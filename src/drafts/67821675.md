A large context window is easy to mistake for permission to include everything. A [context window](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/tokens) is the finite input-and-output budget of a model call, measured in tokens, the pieces of text and data the model processes. More capacity helps, but every irrelevant page still consumes tokens and can add cost and latency.

Consider an AI workflow handling customer refunds. If intake, policy review, transaction checking, drafting, and approval each receive the full conversation and policy manual, the same material is processed repeatedly. Context engineering is the quieter discipline of deciding what each job needs, what it must never see, and what compact record it should pass forward.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Token efficiency begins with relevance: each model call should receive a deliberate context package, not the largest package available.</p></div>

## Prompt, context, and graph engineering

In an [August 2026 video on graph engineering](https://youtu.be/JWhICz1QR8M), technology entrepreneur and podcast host Greg Isenberg offers a useful separation. Prompt engineering shapes the instruction for one call. Context engineering chooses the information available to that call. Graph engineering designs how several jobs hand work to one another.

| Discipline | Core question | Refund example |
|---|---|---|
| Prompt | What should this call do? | Classify the request against explicit criteria. |
| Context | What should it see? | The request, order record, relevant policy excerpt, and output shape. |
| Graph | What happens next? | Route the record through evidence, decision, review, or human approval. |

A viral July 2026 [X retelling by Movez](https://x.com/0xMovez/status/2081011657807925604) reduced this shift to “No more prompting.” Prompts have not disappeared; they have become components. For every component, write a context contract covering its instruction, allowed evidence, shared fields, expected output, and reserved answer space. Keep stable prefixes eligible for [prompt caching](https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-caching), which reduces repeated processing for unchanged prompt beginnings. Retrieve only relevant policy passages, and pass cited findings instead of an entire chat history.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Prompts direct individual jobs, context supplies their evidence, and graphs coordinate the handoffs between them.</p></div>

## Build the smallest useful graph

An agent graph is a workflow map of jobs and allowed handoffs. A **node** performs one job. An **edge** is an allowed path, while a **transition** is an actual move along it. **Shared state** is the structured refund record exchanged between nodes, and **conditional routing** chooses the next node from that record.

```text
                 ┌→ policy reader ─────┐
intake ──────────┤                     ├→ merge → decision writer → skeptic
                 └→ transaction check ┘                         └→ human gate
```

A sequential path makes sense when one job needs the previous result. Independent jobs can instead **fan out** in parallel and **merge** before the decision; this split-and-rejoin shape is a **diamond topology**. The skeptic is a separate checker node looking for unsupported claims. A human gate is a checkpoint where a named person can approve, redirect, or stop the refund, with stricter gates for higher values or policy exceptions.

A **knowledge graph** records relationships among things such as customers, orders, and policies. An agent graph moves work among jobs; it may query the knowledge graph for evidence. Keep the workflow to its smallest useful graph, because every extra node adds another call, handoff, and way to fail. The complexity lives in tuning these boundaries, not decorating the diagram.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A useful graph separates jobs only when the split improves context, parallelism, checking, or control enough to justify another call.</p></div>

## Make the handoffs durable

Isenberg’s practical maturity ladder is refreshingly unglamorous:

1. **Manual workflow:** run the jobs deliberately and observe what each one needs.
2. **File artifacts:** save structured findings, citations, decisions, and open questions.
3. **Orchestration:** automate routes, retries, and checkpoints once the handoffs are stable.

The files are part of token optimization. The decision writer should receive approved policy findings and transaction evidence, not every intermediate exchange. **Persistent state** saves the current refund record across pauses and retries. **Memory** carries selected information across separate runs; it should remain curated rather than becoming a warehouse for old conversations.

The skeptic also explains the reflection phase in [APEX, the Agentic Production Execution framework](/posts/2026-04-05-apex-framework/). Reflection gives the workflow a distinct moment to compare its proposed refund with the evidence before authority increases. For consequential cases, that review must end in a risk-proportional human gate, not a vague request to “be careful.”

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Manual runs reveal the workflow, durable artifacts reduce repeated context, and orchestration should arrive only after the handoffs are clear.</p></div>

## A checker is not an eval set

There is an important attribution boundary here: Isenberg’s video makes the practitioner case for designing work as a graph, but its transcript does not discuss formal evaluation. The evaluation argument comes from Lance Martin, now at Anthropic, the AI research and product company, and formerly at LangChain, an agent-software company. His 2024 AI Engineer World’s Fair workshop, delivered at a conference for AI builders, focused on architecting and testing controllable agents. In July 2026, the X commentator Jey [summarized its emphasis](https://x.com/0xJeyx/status/2081364222856831271) as roughly 15 minutes on the graph and 105 on evaluation. That ratio is commentary rather than exact chapter timing, but it captures the lesson.

A checker reviews one refund run. An **eval set** repeats that separation across test cases, expected outcomes, and grading rules, including inputs held back from the people who designed the graph. [Anthropic’s guidance for agent evals](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) recommends starting with 20–50 tasks drawn from real failures. That is where teams discover whether policy retrieval misses partial refunds, routing mishandles incomplete records, or the human-review queue exceeds its operating limit.

Measure tokens by node, but tune against those outcomes. Change the context package, rerun the held-back cases, and inspect both errors and review load. A graph without an eval set remains a diagram with opinions. The subtle art is spending less context without spending trust.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A checker challenges one execution; an eval set tests whether the graph’s context and routing choices survive unfamiliar cases.</p></div>
