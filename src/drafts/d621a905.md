A [2026 randomized field study](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535) in *Management Science*, a peer-reviewed journal about management and operations, followed 4,867 developers. The trials ran at Microsoft, the technology company; Accenture, the consultancy; and an unnamed company in Fortune magazine's US top 100 by revenue. Developers given an AI coding assistant completed 26.08% more tasks.

A [2025 randomized study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) by Model Evaluation & Threat Research, an independent AI research group known as METR, found the opposite result in a different setting. Sixteen experienced open-source developers took 19% longer across 246 tasks in software projects they already knew.

Both findings can be true because software work is not one uniform activity. I think the strategic mistake is to turn a mixed productivity record into a simple headcount prediction. The more important question is what becomes worth building when implementation gets cheaper in enough settings.

That question points toward the forward-deployed engineer, or FDE: a software engineer who works directly with an operating team to discover the problem, build the system, connect it to the organization, and improve it in production. I call the economic shift behind this role the **proximity premium**. As implementation capacity becomes more abundant, the scarce value moves toward the person close enough to understand exceptions, authority, data, and what a correct outcome means here.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI does not have to accelerate every developer to change software economics. When implementation gets cheaper in enough settings, engineering value moves toward discovery, integration, evaluation, and direct contact with the work.</p></div>

## Cheap code changes what is worth building

Software demand is not fixed. A narrow internal workflow that could never justify a six-month team may become reasonable when a small group can test it in days, which means cheaper implementation can increase the range of problems companies attempt rather than merely reduce the number of people solving the old set.

I think this creates a paradox. More software can mean more specialized software, and specialized software depends more heavily on local rules, awkward data, undocumented exceptions, and organizational judgment. The coding step may shrink while the work around it becomes a larger share of the result.

A [2024 study](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA2680-1.html) by RAND, a nonprofit research organization, interviewed 65 experienced data scientists and engineers about failed AI projects. Common causes included choosing the wrong problem or success measure, poor fit with the real workflow, inadequate data, and a weak foundation for putting the system into use. Faster code does not repair any of those failures. It can simply make a team implement the wrong system sooner.

This is where the bottleneck moves. An AI coding tool can compress part of the journey from specification to software, but it does not automatically know which system is authoritative, which exception matters, who may approve a decision, or how a specific company recognizes good work. The implementation middle gets cheaper; understanding reality becomes more valuable.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Cheap implementation can make many more specialized systems economically viable. Those systems carry more local complexity, so the scarce work shifts toward deciding what to build and making it function inside a real organization.</p></div>

## Engineering starts before the specification

The FDE title has a specific history. Palantir, a US software company founded in 2003, [says it pioneered the role](https://jobs.lever.co/palantir/1bb19522-3936-4adc-9ced-c3df8b5900b9) by embedding engineers with customers. Its [2020 stock-market filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1321655/000119312520230013/d904406ds1.htm) describes time in the field feeding the continuous improvement of its software platforms.

OpenAI, an AI research and product company, [uses the title today](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/openai/305a4b22-7ff9-4fa5-9229-c6a22c9aa64f) for engineers who own discovery, technical boundaries, system design, building, and production rollout while working with customer engineers and people who know the business domain.

The ingredients are older than the label. Product engineers have long joined technical and product decisions; solutions architects design how systems fit together; implementation consultants adapt products to an organization; and embedded professional-services teams work beside customers. I think AI changes the weight of this work rather than inventing it. When one engineer can test more ideas and revise software faster, learning what to test becomes a greater part of engineering.

The operating loop is simple to state: **Observe → Understand → Build → Evaluate → Learn → Improve.** Evaluation means testing a system against expected outcomes before and after release, not merely checking that the code runs. The FDE starts before a finished specification exists and stays long enough to see where the specification was wrong.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Forward-deployed engineering extends established embedded-delivery practices rather than replacing them. AI makes the loop faster, which raises the value of engineers who can discover the right problem and keep learning from production.</p></div>

## The claim that teaches the system

Consider a deliberately hypothetical insurer that wants to automate part of claims handling. An FDE sits beside an experienced claims handler, a domain expert whose daily work gives them practical knowledge of correct decisions. The AI recommends approving a claim, but the handler rejects it because the damage happened before the policy renewal.

The written process never captured that rule properly. Applying it requires comparing the damage date with the renewal date and reading those dates from the authoritative policy and claims systems. This is **tacit knowledge**: practical knowledge people use without having fully recorded it in a policy, process, or specification. Ikujiro Nonaka, an organizational theorist, explained in a [1994 *Organization Science* paper](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.5.1.14) why organizations improve by turning this kind of tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge that others and their systems can use.

The valuable engineering work begins with the override. The FDE captures a **trace**, the record of what the system read, did, and returned, then creates an **evaluation case**, a saved example with an expected outcome used to test future versions. Here the record names the two dates, the authoritative sources, the expected result of rejection, the claims owner responsible for the rule, and the failed trace in which the AI proposed approval. On replay, the workflow must block that illegal approval transition and move the claim to the rejected state defined by policy.

That case then joins an **evaluation set**, a collection of representative examples that the insurer owns and expands as new exceptions appear. The division of labor is the point: the domain expert knows what works, the FDE turns that knowledge into an operable system, and AI supplies implementation capacity.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The expert override supplies evidence about how the work really functions, so it belongs inside the automation loop. The FDE turns that evidence into an explicit rule, a replayable evaluation, and a safer workflow.</p></div>

## Proximity needs production ownership

Sitting close to the work does not give one engineer unlimited authority. A business sponsor should own the outcome and funding, the claims owner should own policy rules and the evaluation set, and a technology owner should own integration, permissions, logs, incident response, and rollback. The FDE owns the learning loop during delivery, but should not unilaterally redefine claims policy or approve production release.

For European organizations, [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act-explorer), known as the EU AI Act, makes part of this operating reality concrete. When a system is legally classified as high-risk, [Article 14](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-14) requires effective human oversight, including the ability to understand limitations, override outputs, and stop the system safely. [Article 26](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-26) places duties on the organization using such a system, including assigning competent overseers, monitoring operation, retaining automatically generated logs when they are under its control, and informing affected workers and their representatives before high-risk workplace use. A claims tool is not automatically high-risk merely because it uses AI; classification depends on its intended purpose and actual use. The practical lesson is broader: human oversight needs named people, authority, and evidence.

For the hypothetical insurer, I think a bounded pilot is more credible than an open-ended experiment. A six-week test could record the current baseline before starting, then review handling time, override rate, cost per correct decision, and unsupported approval attempts each week. The organization should set its target and risk limits in advance; a critical authorization failure should stop the pilot and route work back to the manual process.

The production gate is a joint decision by the sponsor, domain owner, technology owner, and risk or legal owner where required. Workers' representatives, data-protection specialists, and procurement should join when the workplace, personal data, or AI-supplier contract makes their input relevant. If the workflow passes, a named product or operations owner accepts the evaluation set, monitoring, incident process, and review cadence as ordinary production work. That handoff is part of the build, not paperwork after it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An FDE can accelerate learning without concentrating every decision in one role. Production requires explicit owners, measurable outcomes, human authority, a stop rule, and a handoff into normal operations.</p></div>

## One engineer can direct more capacity

An **AI agent** is software that uses an AI model and tools to carry out a multi-step task. One FDE can increasingly direct agents that research a problem, write code, inspect data, generate tests, or replay workflows. In that limited sense, the engineer arrives with an invisible team and becomes the director of a larger implementation capability.

Capacity is not the same as control. Those agents still need restricted access, visible traces, saved state that can be recovered after a failure, limits on cost and response time, and a way to reverse a harmful change. The [2025 report](https://dora.dev/research/2025/dora-report/) from DevOps Research and Assessment, a program that studies software-delivery performance, describes AI as an amplifier of an organization's existing strengths and weaknesses. More implementation power makes sound operations more valuable, not optional.

I think the consultancy consequence is uncomfortable but useful. Selling implementation capacity becomes weaker when clients can access more of that capacity through AI. A stronger proposition is the ability to enter an organization, understand how it really works, build with its domain experts, and leave behind a functioning system with accountable owners and evidence that it performs.

The proximity premium is a forecast, so it needs a way to lose. If it is right, the next two planning cycles should show more engineering roles measured by workflow outcomes, more budget moving toward integration and evaluation, more domain experts owning test cases, and smaller teams putting specialized systems into production. If companies instead capture AI gains mainly by cutting engineering capacity, while software demand stays flat and teams can solve narrow operational problems without deeper domain participation, the forecast is too strong.

The engineering contract would then keep moving: from “Tell me what to build and I'll code it,” to “Tell me the problem and I'll build it,” and finally to “Show me how your business works and I'll help figure out what we should build.” Scarcity moves toward the engineer who can enter a messy human system and turn abundant implementation capacity into a result that survives contact with reality.

The sharper question is not how many developers we will need when AI writes more of the code. It is what our engineers could do if writing the code was no longer the limiting factor.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The FDE forecast holds only if roles, budgets, and production outcomes move toward proximity, evaluation, and accountable iteration. If code is no longer the limiting factor, the strategic question is what problems engineers can finally stay close enough to solve.</p></div>
