[OpenAI, the company behind the ChatGPT AI assistant, currently describes a Forward-Deployed Engineer](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/openai/305a4b22-7ff9-4fa5-9229-c6a22c9aa64f) as someone who works with customer and domain teams from discovery through system design, building, and production rollout. A forward-deployed engineer, or FDE, is embedded close to a real operation instead of waiting for a finished specification. Domain experts are the people who know how that operation succeeds or fails in practice.

The title has history. [Palantir, a US software company founded in 2003, says it pioneered the position](https://jobs.lever.co/palantir/1bb19522-3936-4adc-9ced-c3df8b5900b9) by embedding engineers directly with customers. In its [2020 filing before becoming a public company](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1321655/000119312520230013/d904406ds1.htm), Palantir said time in the field helped improve its software platforms. What looks new today is the amount of implementation capacity an embedded engineer can bring along.

I think AI is making software dramatically cheaper and faster to build, but that does not eliminate the need for engineers. It changes where valuable engineering work happens. As implementation becomes increasingly automated, the bottleneck moves from writing code to understanding messy real-world problems, discovering what should be built, integrating it into an organization, evaluating it against reality, and continuously improving it. The FDE is one of the clearest signals of that move because the role begins before the code and continues after deployment.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The FDE works inside the distance between a business problem and a functioning system. As AI expands an engineer's ability to build, that proximity becomes more valuable.</p>
</div>

## Cheaper code changes what is worth building

The evidence for faster AI-assisted coding is promising, but it is not universal. A [2026 field study in <em>Management Science</em>, a peer-reviewed research journal](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535), covered 4,867 developers across three large companies and found that access to a coding assistant increased completed tasks by about 26%, with meaningful variation between the experiments.

The counterexample matters. [METR, a nonprofit that studies advanced AI systems](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), found that 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer on 246 issues in software projects they already knew when using the AI tools available in early 2025.

The honest conclusion is that AI can lower implementation time in some work, while adding friction in other work. Still, even an uneven fall in cost changes the range of projects a company can consider. A narrow tool for one claims team or one factory process may become worth attempting when it no longer needs a six-month development team.

I think this expansion is more important than the simple claim that every developer will produce more code. Cheaper implementation can create demand for far more specialized software, and specialized software carries more local rules, exceptions, and human judgment. The opportunity grows, but so does the need to understand the problem properly.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI-assisted coding does not speed every task, but lower implementation cost can make many smaller systems viable. That shifts attention from code volume toward choosing and understanding the problems worth solving.</p>
</div>

## The bottleneck moves into the workflow

[RAND, a nonprofit policy research organization, reported in 2024](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA2680-1.html) that AI projects often failed because teams chose the wrong problem or success measure, did not fit the business workflow, lacked suitable data, or could not support deployment. The study was based on interviews with 65 experienced data scientists and engineers. Its findings point to a practical limit: a capable model cannot repair an incomplete picture of how work really happens.

Consider an illustrative insurance scenario. An insurer wants to automate part of claims handling, so an FDE sits beside an experienced claims handler and watches the process. The AI recommends approving a claim, but the handler rejects it because the damage happened before the policy renewal. That rule is not properly documented; the decision depends on comparing the incident date with the renewal date in the insurer's authoritative records.

This is tacit knowledge: know-how used in real work but not fully written down. The valuable engineering work is to uncover the rule, identify the right data, and add a fixed policy check that returns the same result for the same two dates. The failure becomes an evaluation case, a worked example used to test the system after a change, and joins a larger set of past claims with an error limit set by the claims owner before launch. The AI can recommend an outcome, but the fixed check should block an invalid approval, while that owner keeps the authority to stop or reverse the workflow.

This small example captures the FDE operating loop better than a role description can: Observe → Understand → Build → Evaluate → Learn → Improve. The engineer learns from the expert, turns the lesson into software, watches what happens, and repeats. Implementation is inside the loop, not the whole loop.

## The people closest to the work become more valuable

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology, the Department of Commerce agency that develops technical standards, describes AI systems as [socio-technical](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf). That means outcomes come from the interaction of technology, people, rules, and organizational conditions. Its AI Risk Management Framework therefore calls for domain-expert input, evaluation under real deployment conditions, human oversight, user feedback, and continued monitoring.

That produces a simple working relationship: **Domain expert ↔ FDE ↔ AI**. AI supplies implementation capacity, while the FDE turns real work into connected systems and tests. The domain expert defines what good looks like, where the exceptions hide, and which errors matter most.

I think domain experts become more important in this model, not less. They are no longer asked to approve a specification and return months later for acceptance testing. They help shape the system while it is being built, and their corrections become part of the system's ability to improve.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>In this model, AI capacity becomes useful when domain judgment and engineering translation stay connected. The expert supplies reality, the FDE turns it into a working system, and AI increases the speed at which that system can be changed and tested.</p>
</div>

## One engineer can direct more capacity

An AI agent is software that can carry out a series of steps with tools under a person's direction. One FDE may increasingly direct coding, research, data-analysis, testing, and workflow agents as an invisible engineering team. This is a forecast, not a proven staffing ratio, and more agents also create more ways for a system to fail.

The limits on this invisible team matter. Agents should receive only the access they need, and their actions should be recorded. An approval, payment, or record change should pass a fixed control that a named owner can stop or reverse. The engineer's role then expands from producing code to directing a small AI-powered capability while remaining responsible for what reaches production.

I think this changes what consultancies can sell. If implementation capacity keeps getting cheaper, a consultancy cannot rely mainly on adding more people to produce more code. Its stronger value is the ability to enter an organization, learn how the work actually functions, collaborate with experts, and convert that knowledge into a system the organization can safely run and improve.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>An FDE can bring more implementation capacity into the room, but capacity without control only multiplies risk. The durable value lies in directing that capacity around a real workflow and leaving clear ownership behind.</p>
</div>

In this science-fiction comedy, a supercomputer produces the Answer to Life, the Universe, and Everything, but nobody has properly understood the question. The joke maps neatly to AI-era engineering: producing an impressive answer is not enough when the real problem is still unclear. The FDE creates value by staying close enough to help form the question, test the answer in context, and learn from the result.

## A forecast we can test

Customer-facing engineers, product engineers, and implementation consultants already do parts of this work. AI did not invent the need for proximity. It changes the economics by allowing one embedded engineer to explore, build, and revise more options while the problem is still being understood.

The working contract is moving. Yesterday it was, “Tell me what to build and I will code it.” Today it is, “Tell me the problem and I will build it.” I think tomorrow's stronger version will be, “Show me how your business works and I will help figure out what we should build.”

This forecast should leave evidence. If it is right, by 2029 we should see more engineering roles combine discovery with production responsibility, more budgets move from code production toward integration and evaluation, and more specialized systems reach production with domain experts still involved. If coding output rises but useful systems remain stuck in pilots, or if the FDE remains a niche title used by a few vendors, the forecast has not earned its larger claim.

The scarce resource is shifting from people who can produce code to people who can understand a messy human problem and turn it into a functioning AI system. That makes the FDE one of the most interesting roles of the AI era, and potentially a preview of what software engineering itself is becoming. The title is secondary. The sharper question is what our engineers could do if writing the code was no longer the limiting factor.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Forward-deployed engineering is a testable preview of software work moving closer to human reality. The better question is not only how many developers we need when AI writes more code, but what engineers can do once code is no longer the limiting factor.</p>
</div>
