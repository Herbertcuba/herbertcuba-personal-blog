[OpenAI](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/openai/305a4b22-7ff9-4fa5-9229-c6a22c9aa64f), the AI company behind the ChatGPT conversational assistant, describes a role that begins with discovery and technical scoping, continues through system design and building, and ends with production rollout. The role is called a Forward-Deployed Engineer, or FDE: a software engineer who works close to a customer's operational problem and helps turn it into a functioning system.

The title has roots in [Palantir](https://jobs.lever.co/palantir/1bb19522-3936-4adc-9ced-c3df8b5900b9), a US software company founded in 2003. Palantir says it pioneered the position by embedding engineers directly with customers, and its [2020 public filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1321655/000119312520230013/d904406ds1.htm) says time in the field contributes to continuous improvement of its platforms. OpenAI's use of the title today shows how that model is evolving around AI-enabled systems.

I think this is more than a hiring fashion. It points to what I call **the proximity shift**: as the cost of implementation falls, more value moves to the people who can get close enough to reality to find the right problem, expose hidden rules, and judge whether the resulting system works.

AI is making software dramatically cheaper and faster to build, but that does not eliminate the need for engineers. It changes where valuable engineering work happens. As implementation becomes increasingly automated, the bottleneck moves from writing code to understanding messy real-world problems, discovering what should be built, integrating it into an organization, evaluating it against reality, and continuously improving it. The scarce resource shifts from people who can produce code to people who can understand a messy human problem and turn it into a functioning AI system. That makes the Forward-Deployed Engineer (FDE) one of the most interesting roles of the AI era—and potentially a preview of what software engineering itself is becoming.

The role matters because lower implementation cost raises the relative value of context, judgment, and learning.</p></div>

## Cheaper implementation changes the economics

Evidence that AI can accelerate parts of software development is real, but conditional. A [2026 randomized field study in *Management Science*](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535), an academic journal, covered 4,867 developers at the technology company Microsoft, the consultancy Accenture, and an unnamed company in the Fortune 100 ranking of the largest US corporations by revenue. Developers given an AI coding assistant completed 26.08% more tasks overall, with meaningful variation between the three experiments.

A [2025 randomized study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) by Model Evaluation & Threat Research, a nonprofit that studies advanced AI systems, found a different result. Sixteen experienced open-source developers using the AI tools then available took 19% longer across 246 tasks in software projects they already knew. Together, the studies support a bounded claim: AI can reduce implementation time or increase output in some settings, but it has not made every engineer or every project faster.

The strategic consequence may still be larger than the task-level gain. When a narrow claims tool, a planning system for one factory, or an internal workflow no longer requires a six-month delivery team, more specialized software becomes economically reasonable to attempt. That software reaches further into local processes, where the exceptions are less likely to appear in a clean specification.

I think the proximity shift begins here. Savings in implementation do not simply remove labor; they can increase demand for people who know which small, awkward, company-specific systems are worth building. That demand response is a forecast, not a finding established by the productivity studies.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-assisted coding can improve output in some conditions and slow experts in others. If implementation costs keep falling, the more important effect may be an expansion in the number of specialized problems worth solving.</p></div>

## The bottleneck moves into the workflow

The hard part of enterprise AI often appears before and after code. A [2024 RAND study](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA2680-1.html) from the US nonprofit research organization interviewed 65 experienced data scientists and engineers about failed AI projects. Common causes included misunderstanding the problem, choosing the wrong success measure, poor fit with the business workflow, inadequate data, and weak foundations for deployment.

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology, the federal body that develops technical standards and guidance, describes AI as **socio-technical**: its results depend on the technology, the people operating it, and the organization around it. Its [AI Risk Management Framework](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf) calls for domain-expert input, tests that represent deployment conditions, production monitoring, human oversight, user feedback, and review of human overrides. A domain expert is the person who knows what good work looks like in a particular field, including the exceptions that rarely reach a process document.

Consider an illustrative insurance scenario. An insurer wants to automate part of claims handling, so an FDE sits beside an experienced claims handler while an AI system makes recommendations. The AI recommends approving one claim. The handler rejects it because the damage occurred before the policy renewal date, a rule that requires comparing two dates and was never properly documented.

That missing rule is **tacit knowledge**: practical know-how an expert uses but has not fully put into words or formal instructions. The FDE inspects the **trace**, the recorded sequence of inputs, model or tool actions, and decisions that produced the recommendation. The failure then becomes an **evaluation case**, a repeatable test with a known expected outcome: the two dates are recorded, “do not approve” is the expected state, “approve” is the observed recommendation, and the claims owner supplies the reason.

The engineer encodes the date comparison, makes the workflow block a move to “approved” when the damage predates renewal, adds the case to the evaluation set, and runs the test again. I think this loop of observing, understanding, building, evaluating, learning, and improving is the real unit of AI-era engineering. The valuable output is not only the rule in software; it is a system that can learn from the next expert override without forgetting the last one.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The claims example shows why a correct-looking system can still fail when local knowledge is missing. Embedded observation turns one expert override into an explicit rule, a replayable test, and a safer workflow.</p></div>

## Proximity needs an operating model

An embedded engineer without decision boundaries can create a lively pilot and an orphaned production system. The FDE should run the learning loop, but should not silently become the owner of business value, claims policy, technical operations, and risk at once.

In the hypothetical insurer, a bounded pilot could run for six weeks in **shadow mode**, where the AI recommends an action but cannot change a claim's official state. Before launch, the business sponsor and claims owner could set a baseline and continuation criteria, such as a 20% reduction in median review time with no increase in incorrect approvals. A weekly review would track expert override rate, cost per correctly reviewed claim, unresolved errors, and the time needed to turn a new exception into a passing evaluation case.

Ownership should be equally concrete. The business sponsor owns problem selection and the value baseline; the claims owner owns policy and expected outcomes; the technology owner owns access, integration, logs, reliability, and incidents. Legal, risk, procurement, data owners, and employee representatives join where the workflow, vendor contract, data use, or workplace impact requires them. The FDE connects these decisions and builds against them. An authorized production owner decides whether to continue, stop, or roll back to the last approved workflow.

For a European organization, some of this discipline can also be a legal requirement. The [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, requires effective human oversight when an AI system is legally classified as high-risk, including the ability to override or stop its output. It also places monitoring and record-keeping duties on the organization using the system. For high-risk systems used in the workplace, affected workers and their representatives must be informed before use. Not every insurance claims tool is automatically high-risk; classification depends on the system's intended use and the categories in the law.

I think the handoff into ordinary operations is the decisive production gate. Once the agreed measures are met, the product or operations owner accepts the ongoing budget, review cadence, incident process, supplier obligations, and retirement plan. Proximity remains useful after that gate, but it no longer excuses unclear accountability.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The FDE owns the learning loop, while named business, domain, technology, and risk owners retain decision authority. A time-boxed shadow pilot, explicit success measures, controlled deployment, and rollback turn embedded work into a production model rather than permanent experimentation.</p></div>

## One engineer can direct more capacity

An **AI agent** is software that uses an AI model and tools to carry out a multi-step task. One FDE can increasingly direct coding agents, research agents, data-analysis agents, and testing agents while remaining close to the workflow. In that sense, the engineer arrives with an invisible team, although the reliability and breadth of that team remain a forecast rather than a settled capability.

The team still needs ordinary engineering boundaries. Agents should receive only the permissions required for a task, record their tool calls, preserve enough context to replay a failure, and have no authority to approve a claim unless an authorized owner explicitly grants it. Production teams must also see response time, cost, error rates, and recovery status, and they need a tested way to disable the AI path and restore the last approved workflow.

A skeptical reader can reasonably see familiar work in the FDE: product engineering that joins product and technical decisions, solutions architecture that designs how systems fit together, implementation consulting that adapts a system to an organization, and professional-services teams that work beside customers. I do too. The important change is not the invention of customer proximity; it is how much implementation and evaluation capacity one embedded engineer may be able to direct, and how quickly that person can repeat the learning loop. Integration, access control, data quality, monitoring, incident response, and maintenance remain software and operations work.

That distinction matters for consultancies. I think implementation capacity alone becomes a weaker proposition when clients can produce more code with smaller teams. The stronger proposition is the ability to enter an organization, understand how work really happens, collaborate with domain experts, and convert that knowledge into an AI-enabled system with measurable production results. This is an economic argument, not evidence that every consultancy has already changed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI agents can give an embedded engineer more implementation and evaluation capacity, but they also amplify weak permissions, poor monitoring, and unclear recovery. The durable value lies in directing that capacity around a real workflow while retaining the controls and ownership of ordinary production engineering.</p></div>

## Let the forecast fail

A useful forecast needs a way to lose. If the proximity shift is real, we should see more engineers placed inside operational workflows, more small and specialized systems reaching production, and more teams measured by workflow outcomes and learning speed rather than code volume. Consultancy offers should also move toward problem discovery, evaluation, integration, and production responsibility as implementation becomes easier to buy.

There are clear disconfirming outcomes. Domain experts may become able to build and govern reliable production systems directly, reducing the need for an engineering translator. Or the FDE label may remain a sales-linked implementation title used by a small group of vendors, while most engineering work stays organized around fixed specifications and central delivery teams. If specialized software does not expand as implementation gets cheaper, the economic mechanism at the center of this argument is wrong.

The role itself will vary by company, and the title may not survive. The deeper test is whether engineering moves closer to the place where reality contradicts the specification. Yesterday's contract was “tell me what to build.” The emerging contract is “show me how the work happens, and I will help determine what is worth building, make it function, and keep teaching it.”

The sharpest question is therefore larger than how many developers we will need when AI writes more of the code. What could our engineers do if writing the code was no longer the limiting factor?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The FDE forecast gains credibility only if embedded engineers, specialized production systems, and outcome-based delivery grow together; direct domain building or continued fixed-spec delivery would weaken it. The real shift is engineering's movement toward the human and organizational context that code alone cannot supply.</p></div>
