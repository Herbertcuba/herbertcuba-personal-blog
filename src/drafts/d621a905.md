A [2026 paper in *Management Science*](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535), a management-research journal, combined randomized field experiments with 4,867 developers at software company Microsoft, consultancy Accenture, and one unnamed Fortune 100 company. Access to an AI coding assistant increased completed tasks by 26.08%. Yet a [2025 randomized study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) by AI-evaluation research group METR found that 16 experienced open-source developers were 19% slower on 246 tasks in existing software projects.

I think the disagreement is the useful signal. AI assistance can accelerate implementation, but the result depends on the task and the environment around it.

In some settings, AI-assisted coding makes software dramatically faster to build and lowers cost when implementation time dominates the budget. That does not eliminate the need for engineers. It changes where valuable engineering work happens.

As implementation becomes increasingly automated, the bottleneck moves from writing code to understanding messy real-world problems, discovering what should be built, integrating it into an organization, evaluating it against reality, and continuously improving it.

The scarce resource shifts from people who can produce code to people who can understand a messy human problem and turn it into a functioning AI system. That makes the forward-deployed engineer, or FDE, one of the most interesting roles of the AI era and potentially a preview of what software engineering itself is becoming. An FDE works inside or alongside a customer team to discover the problem, build and integrate the system, watch it operate, and improve it from evidence.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Evidence on AI coding productivity is meaningful but mixed, because faster code does not guarantee successful software. The strategic shift is that engineering value moves toward understanding, integration, evaluation, and learning in the real environment.</p>
</div>

## Cheap code changes what is worth building

The standard automation question asks how many engineers remain if AI produces more of the code. I think that frame changes supply while holding demand still. Lower implementation cost can make a narrow claims exception or maintenance workflow worth building without a six-month team. This remains a forecast, and it depends on AI-assisted systems becoming dependable enough to use.

If that happens, cheaper implementation leads to more software, specialized workflows, and greater exposure to local rules and organizational dependencies. The bottleneck moves into deciding what good means, connecting systems, evaluating results, and changing the surrounding work.

A [2024 study by RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA2680-1.html), a US policy-research organization, interviewed 65 experienced data scientists and engineers. Leading causes of AI-project failure included choosing the wrong problem or measure of success, poor workflow fit, inadequate data, and a weak foundation for putting the system into use. Faster coding does not resolve any of them.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Cheaper implementation may expand the number of problems worth addressing instead of merely shrinking engineering teams. As software reaches narrower workflows, local context and organizational fit become a larger share of the work.</p>
</div>

## The engineer who starts before the specification

The title has a specific history. [Palantir](https://jobs.lever.co/palantir/1bb19522-3936-4adc-9ced-c3df8b5900b9), a data-platform company, says it pioneered the role by embedding software engineers with customers. Its [2020 stock-market filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1321655/000119312520230013/d904406ds1.htm) describes field time feeding continuous improvement of its platforms. [OpenAI](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/openai/305a4b22-7ff9-4fa5-9229-c6a22c9aa64f), an AI research and product company, now uses the title for engineers who discover needs, define technical boundaries, build systems, and put them into live use with customer engineers and people who know the business domain.

The organizations differ, but the operating idea is consistent: the engineer begins before a finished specification exists and stays after the system goes live.

The work follows a loop: observe, understand, build, evaluate, learn, improve. I think evaluation is the hinge. An evaluation is a repeatable test of whether the system produces acceptable results on representative cases. It turns a failure in the field into evidence for the next version.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>An FDE owns the path from problem discovery through production learning rather than waiting for a complete specification. The role becomes valuable because deployment is the start of a learning loop, not the end of a build.</p>
</div>

## The rule that was never written down

Consider a hypothetical insurance scenario, not a reported client case. An insurer wants to automate part of claims handling, so an FDE sits beside an experienced handler. The AI recommends approval, but the handler rejects the claim because the damage predates the policy renewal and is not covered. The rule requires comparing two dates, yet it is not properly documented.

This is tacit knowledge: practical knowledge people use without having fully recorded it in a policy, process, or specification.

The valuable move is to ask why the expert overrode the recommendation, encode the date comparison, add the claim as an evaluation case, and test again. The domain expert knows what a valid result looks like, the FDE converts that judgment into system behavior, and AI supplies implementation capacity. I think that relationship is the real unit of AI delivery.

The [AI Risk Management Framework from NIST](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf), the US standards agency, calls AI systems socio-technical, meaning outcomes depend on technology, operators, organizational context, and human behavior together. It recommends domain-expert input, representative tests, monitoring, documented human oversight, and attention to overrides. The expert's rejection is part of system design, not an inconvenience to automate away.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Real workflows contain judgment and exceptions that no finished specification may capture. Domain experts reveal those rules, FDEs turn them into system behavior and evaluation cases, and AI accelerates the implementation cycle.</p>
</div>

## One engineer can bring a wider capability

This capability is still emerging, and it does not describe every FDE today. One engineer can increasingly work with coding, research, data-analysis, testing, and workflow agents. An AI agent is software that can pursue a limited task through several steps and tools instead of returning one answer to one prompt.

These tools do not behave like a reliable invisible team merely because they are available. [DORA's 2025 report](https://dora.dev/research/2025/dora-report/), from a long-running research program on software delivery, describes AI as an amplifier of the organization's existing strengths and weaknesses. Weak data, unclear ownership, and poor feedback can scale along with code output.

Even with that constraint, I think the FDE can become the director of an AI-powered engineering capability: holding context, directing specialized agents, checking their work, and connecting the effort to an operating result.

If this pattern continues, raw implementation capacity will become less distinctive for consultancies as clients gain access to similar models and tools. The stronger proposition is entering an organization, learning how work really happens with its domain experts, and rapidly turning that knowledge into working AI-enabled systems. That requires access, trust, usable data, and authority to change the workflow.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI agents can widen an FDE's effective capacity, but they also amplify weaknesses in data, ownership, and feedback. The durable value lies in directing that capacity around real organizational knowledge and measurable operating results.</p>
</div>

## Engineering moves closer to reality

The old engineering contract could be summarized as, “Tell me what to build and I will code it.” The emerging version is, “Tell me the problem and I will build something.” The FDE points toward a third: “Show me how your business works and I will help figure out what we should build.”

Deep work in platforms, security, reliability, and models still matters. But the center of gravity for applied engineering moves closer to the people, systems, constraints, and judgments that define whether software works. The pattern can extend beyond engineering as AI handles more execution and people take greater responsibility for context, judgment, evaluation, and coordinating work across humans and agents.

I think the sharper question is not only, “How many developers will we need when AI writes the code?” It is, “What could our engineers do if writing the code was no longer the limiting factor?”

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>As code becomes cheaper, valuable engineering moves closer to the business, where reality can be understood and tested. The FDE matters because the role is designed for that move.</p>
</div>
