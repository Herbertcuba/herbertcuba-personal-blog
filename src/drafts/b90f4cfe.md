Claude's 650 failed ideas are the easy part of this story. Attempt 651 is the leadership problem.

On 10 August 2026, [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/riemann-zeta), the AI company behind Claude, reported that an unreleased research version of its AI system had advanced a mathematical result connected to the Riemann zeta function, which mathematicians use to study prime numbers. The work began in two sessions of Claude Code, Anthropic's environment for AI agents that can use files, scripts and command-line tools. Anthropic reports 31 million output tokens, units of model-generated text used to meter consumption, followed by about a day and a half coordinating roughly 60 subagents. A subagent here is a delegated Claude run assigned part of the work, not a digital employee.

The figures do not show that retries alone caused the result. Anthropic published no controlled comparison across token budgets, agent counts or retry limits, and the successful proof also depended on decades of human mathematics, a conceptual move, software checks and specialist review.

I think the deeper shift is still profound. Anthropic did not measure fatigue, shame or career risk, so those words are an interpretation of human limits rather than findings from this case. Yet a machine can turn another round of failure into a budget request, then produce reasons to continue faster than independent reviewers can test them. Verification becomes the bottleneck.

That changes the rule I would put near the top of any persistent AI campaign: no renewal without independent evidence and fresh budget authorization. The limit should pause the system automatically, before the delivery team starts arguing that one more batch will finally work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Claude's run made extraordinary persistence something an organization can provision and meter, but the scarce resource is the ability to verify what that persistence produces. Attempt 651 needs an evidence gate, a separate budget decision and an enforceable pause.</p></div>

## What actually moved

The Riemann zeta function is a mathematical function whose zeros, the inputs that make its value equal zero, reveal increasingly fine information about how prime numbers are distributed. Some known zeros at the negative even integers are called trivial. The remaining nontrivial zeros are the ones that carry the famous unsolved question.

In 1859, German mathematician Bernhard Riemann proposed what became the Riemann hypothesis: every nontrivial zero should lie on the critical line, a particular vertical line in the function's coordinate system. The hypothesis remains unproved. Claude did not solve it.

Anthropic now links to a [revised 17-page preprint dated 11 August 2026](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/95c246936988e43127bc6b2ceb7077c1dad2d68e.pdf). A preprint is a public research manuscript that has not completed conventional journal peer review. The paper proves that, as zeros are counted in increasingly high ranges, at least two thirds of the nontrivial zeros are both simple and on the critical line. A simple zero occurs once; if a zero repeats, the calculation counts each repetition in the total. That convention matters because the bound is a guaranteed share of a total that counts repeated zeros more than once. An optimized version gives 0.6725 as the lower bound, the minimum share the proof guarantees.

The previous record was 5/12, about 41.7%, and had stood since 2020. The popular “37-year record” description reaches back to number theorist [J. B. Conrey's peer-reviewed 1989 result](https://doi.org/10.1515/crll.1989.399.1) of more than two fifths, but human mathematicians improved the bound between 1989 and 2020. This was a large step in a living research line, not the end of 37 motionless years.

The paper credits earlier work and describes its new ingredient as a different reading of an existing mathematical sum through linear algebra, the study of relationships among vectors and matrices. Anthropic also released a [Lean 4 formalization](https://github.com/anthropics/zeta-23-lean), meaning the proof was encoded for a theorem-proving program to check against explicit logical rules and dependencies. Anthropic says its own mathematicians validated the paper and two outside number theorists examined it on short notice. Those checks strengthen the result, but they are not completed journal peer review or independent reproduction of the entire campaign.

I think the unusually clear finish line is central to the economics. A candidate proof could face a precise theorem, numerical tests, a search for prior work, formal checking and specialist examination. Most strategic questions do not arrive with evidence gates that sharp, so leaders must build the gate before they fund the persistence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Claude raised a specific proven minimum from 5/12 to at least two thirds; it did not prove the Riemann hypothesis. Prior human mathematics and unusually strong ways to test the result made this a favorable case for persistent AI search.</p></div>

## Persistence became a budget

Anthropic's account describes an instrumented search rather than 650 copies of one prompt. The agents issued 2,400 shell commands, wrote hundreds of scripts, ran numerical checks, looked for counterexamples and downloaded 54 papers from arXiv, a public repository for research manuscripts, to look for prior work. Persistence came with branching work, tools, retained records and review.

The public record still cannot reproduce the whole campaign. Anthropic has not disclosed the unreleased model build, complete prompt and work history, total model input, the settings used to generate responses, full financial cost or comparable runs at smaller budgets. We therefore know the scale of selected activity, but not how another unit of search changed the odds of success.

My take is that persistence has become a metered operating input, while each new stage behaves like an investment option: the organization can fund another bounded round after seeing new evidence, but it is not obliged to do so. I don't know whether half the tokens would have found the same proof or whether twice the review would expose a problem. Token volume cannot answer either question.

Evidence from ordinary work reinforces that caution without settling the mathematics case. A [2025 peer-reviewed study](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044) in *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*, an economics journal, examined 5,179 customer-support agents and found that access to an AI assistant increased issues resolved per hour by 14% on average, with much larger gains for less experienced workers. In another setting, [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), a nonprofit that evaluates AI systems, ran a 2025 randomized study in which 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer on 246 tasks when allowed early-2025 AI tools. METR later said newer tools probably helped more, but its 2026 follow-up could not produce a dependable estimate because participation and time measurement had changed. The results differ because the people, tools and work differ. Activity alone tells us very little about value.

I think tireless systems add a second economic risk. They can manufacture polished continuation arguments faster than reviewers can verify them, so the campaign begins to use its own output as evidence that it deserves more output. The useful measure is full cost per independently accepted result, including expert review and the next-best project that lost the same capacity.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agent activity is now easy to meter and purchase, but it is not economic value. Persistent search should be funded in bounded stages and judged by full cost per independently accepted result, because verification capacity can run out before machine output does.</p></div>

## Make stopping enforceable

The stopping rule belongs inside the portfolio and risk machinery the organization already has. The [European Union's 2024 Artificial Intelligence Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng#art_9), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, offers a concrete control precedent for systems it classifies as high risk: lifecycle risk management, testing against predefined measures, logs that record system activity and human oversight able to interrupt a system. Not every research campaign falls into that legal category. The useful design lesson is narrower: combine the control with existing procedures, keep evidence over time and make the human right to stop technically real.

I think a Tireless Intelligence Charter can turn that lesson into a one-page campaign record completed before launch:

- **Question and alternative:** the decision owner, observable value and next-best project competing for the same capacity.
- **Frozen evidence version:** the acceptance test, disconfirming evidence, novelty test, independent evaluator and required replication.
- **Stage envelope:** model and tool budget, token and time limits, reviewer-hour capacity and next decision date.
- **Authority and record:** delivery owner, budget owner, kill authority, archive owner, named signatories and any procurement, security or employee-representation review required before activation.

The orchestration layer, the software that assigns agent work and releases quota, must enforce the Charter. Every campaign receives an unchangeable identifier tied to its evidence version and trace record. When any quota or review date is reached, one system action moves the campaign from ACTIVE to PAUSED, blocks new tasks and counts work already in flight against the stage limit. Repeated pause signals leave it paused. Late results can enter the evidence record, but they cannot release more work.

Reactivation requires both a passed check from the independent evaluator and a new authorization from the budget owner. The delivery team cannot renew itself, and the system rejects that transition. If evidence fails or funding is refused, the campaign closes, loses permission to launch tasks and preserves its prompts, tests, accepted evidence, rejected paths and decision history for later audit.

A concrete pilot could ask an AI campaign to find a factory-scheduling change that cuts simulated energy use by 8% without reducing throughput, then pass a rerun on data hidden from the delivery team. Its first stage might receive eight weeks, a €50,000 model-and-tool cap, 80 reviewer hours and a prebooked decision date, with a monitoring upgrade named as the competing project. Those numbers are illustrative. Their job is to expose the opportunity cost before enthusiasm hides it.

The Charter needs its own stopping rule too. In a 90-day portfolio test, require at least 20% fewer renewals unsupported by independent evidence, no increase in false stops, meaning campaigns closed despite already meeting their frozen test, and a lower full cost per accepted result. If the Charter merely transfers unmeasured work to reviewers or cannot beat the existing review process, retire or redesign it.

People still contribute technical ideas, domain knowledge, interpretation, relationships and accountability. The added leadership duty is to choose which question deserves institutional persistence, what evidence can earn another round and when refusal protects more value than continued search.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A credible stopping rule is a system control, not a sentence in a policy. Quotas must trigger a real pause, independent evidence and a separate budget owner must govern renewal, and the Charter itself must reduce unsupported renewals without raising false stops or total cost.</p></div>
