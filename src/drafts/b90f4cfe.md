On August 10, 2026, [Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude](https://www.anthropic.com/research/riemann-zeta), reported an unusual mathematical result. Across two sessions in Claude Code, its environment for AI agents that can use files and software tools, an unreleased research version of Claude used a total of 31 million output tokens. An output token is a metered unit of text generated by the model.

Anthropic says Claude first tried 650 ideas and none worked. After another prompt, it spent a day and a half coordinating about 60 subagents, delegated AI runs that explored and checked parts of the problem. The seductive explanation is that Claude simply outlasted mathematicians. I think that story is too clean.

The public record contains no controlled comparison showing that the attempt count, subagent count, or token volume caused the result. The proof also rests on decades of human mathematics, a new conceptual move, software checks, and specialist examination. Anthropic did not measure fatigue, shame, or career risk. Those words describe human frictions that make another failed idea difficult to defend, not findings from this project.

Yet the economic change is real enough to examine. An organization can now provision persistence, meter it, and decide whether to fund another stage rather than asking one person to absorb every dead end. When the system never gets tired, leadership moves toward choosing which questions deserve that capacity, what will count as evidence, and who may say stop.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Claude's result does not establish that persistence caused a breakthrough. It shows that repeated search can become a funded input, making the choice to renew or stop a central leadership responsibility.</p>

## What Claude actually proved

The Riemann zeta function is a mathematical function whose zeros, the inputs where its value equals zero, help describe how prime numbers are distributed. Its nontrivial zeros lie inside a band of the mathematical plane. The Riemann hypothesis, proposed by German mathematician Bernhard Riemann in 1859, says all those zeros sit on one vertical line called the critical line. Nobody has proved that hypothesis, and Claude did not prove it.

The [current version of Claude's preprint, dated August 11, 2026](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/95c246936988e43127bc6b2ceb7077c1dad2d68e.pdf), is a research paper released before conventional journal peer review. It establishes a lower bound, meaning a proven minimum: at least two thirds of the nontrivial zeros are simple and lie on the critical line. A simple zero occurs once; when the paper counts all zeros with multiplicity, a repeated zero is counted once for each repetition. The claim is asymptotic, so it concerns the share as the count extends without limit rather than a finite census, and it does not classify the remaining third.

The previous record was 5/12, about 41.7%, reached in 2020. The new basic theorem raises that floor to two thirds, about 66.7%, a move of 25 percentage points. The popular “37-year record” framing reaches back to number theorist J. B. Conrey's [1989 peer-reviewed result](https://doi.org/10.1515/crll.1989.399.1) that more than two fifths lie on the line, but mathematicians improved the bound between 1989 and 2020. The new paper also credits a [2024 peer-reviewed precursor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04799) by four number theorists: Baluyot, Goldston, Suriajaya, and Turnage-Butterbaugh. Claude applied a new linear-algebraic reading, using the mathematics of vectors and matrices to connect machinery that this earlier research had built.

Anthropic's [published process transcripts](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/8a0d1add3c637b858a9a181e98c40e9548c3f44f.pdf) show one concrete pivot. A branch tried to extract the needed mathematical guarantee from zeros away from the critical line, found no cases its test could use, and dropped that route. It then focused on the part of the test whose positive values could certify zeros on the line, first proving a one-half lower bound; a follow-up run pushed the proof to two thirds. The transcripts make this change of route inspectable, but they omit the model's private reasoning and do not provide the complete settings and metadata needed to reproduce the run.

The validation record is meaningful but unfinished. Anthropic says two of its own mathematicians studied and validated the paper, while number theorists Conrey and Daniel Goldston examined it on short notice. Claude also produced a [Lean 4 formalization](https://github.com/anthropics/zeta-23-lean). Lean 4 is a theorem-proving programming language, and formal verification here means software checked a precisely encoded theorem and proof against declared logical rules. That improves auditability, but it does not verify the search process, the economic argument, or broad acceptance by the mathematical community. I think the result deserves attention precisely because those boundaries remain visible.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Claude advanced a specific lower bound rather than proving the Riemann hypothesis. The result builds on human research and several layers of checking, while remaining a fresh company-released preprint without broad independent replication.</p>

## Persistence is now a budgeted input

The 31 million output tokens record substantial activity, not a public price. Anthropic did not publish the input-token total, model price, infrastructure or energy cost, reviewer cost, opportunity cost, or returns from comparable campaigns. I don't know whether this scale was necessary, and the available evidence cannot answer that question.

Still, the operating mechanism matters. A leader can approve a bounded search, observe what it produces, and choose whether to buy another stage. I use “attempt 651” as shorthand for that next funded stage, not as a literal attempt numbered by Anthropic. Its chronology says 650 initial ideas failed before a later multiagent effort went deeper.

This is where I think the Three Crucibles framing becomes testable. In that framing, productive capacity begins to behave like capital when an organization can fund it, allocate it among competing uses, and preserve the option to invest again. Stage budgeting alone does not earn the stronger claim. Across a portfolio, governed campaigns would need to produce more independently validated value per full cost than open-ended campaigns and the best alternative projects. If they do not, the capital analogy fails.

Evidence from ordinary work shows why the task and measure matter. A [2023 working paper from the U.S. National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161), an economics study circulated before final journal publication, found a 14% average increase in issues resolved per hour among 5,179 customer-support workers given an AI assistant. A [2025 randomized study by the independent research group METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) found that 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with the early-2025 AI tools tested; METR now labels that result out of date for newer tools. Neither study concerns mathematical research. Together they show why output volume and user confidence cannot replace measurement inside the work receiving the investment.

My take is that cheap retries can lower the cost of exploring an idea, but they can also make a weak idea expensive by extending its life. Persistence becomes an economic capability only when each stage preserves a credible option to stop.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Persistent AI search becomes economically interesting when another stage is a real investment choice rather than an automatic renewal. The stronger capital claim must survive a portfolio test based on independently validated value, full cost, and credible alternatives.</p>
</div>

## Write attempt 651 down in advance

I think every persistent AI campaign needs a Tireless Intelligence Charter before it starts. This is a proposed governance control, not an Anthropic practice, a legal requirement, or an implemented software workflow. It fixes the question and the evidence contract, meaning the predeclared tests for success, disconfirmation, and independent verification. It also freezes the limits, independent evaluator, renewal authority, and stopping rule before a persuasive stream of output can move those boundaries.

The charter should fit inside an existing portfolio review. Active, paused, renewed, and stopped become useful states only when permissions and a durable event record enforce them. The delivery team must never be able to renew its own campaign.

Consider this deliberately fictional completed charter for a 5,000-person Swedish manufacturer. It uses the company's normal portfolio council rather than creating another committee.

| Charter field | Hypothetical entry |
|---|---|
| Question and ownership | Can an AI agent find a maintenance rule that improves the current equipment-failure forecast? The chief operating officer sponsors it; the maintenance-analytics product owner delivers it. |
| Evidence contract | At least 5% improvement on two datasets withheld from the search, reproduced by a plant reliability lead outside the delivery team. |
| Stage limits | Fourteen days; one approved model; read-only access in an isolated test environment; 150,000 Swedish kronor tool cap; 80 reviewer hours. |
| Decision forum | A prebooked day-14 review in the existing portfolio council with finance, enterprise architecture, and the AI-governance lead. |
| Outcome and state | After 92,000 kronor of model and tool use and 62 reviewer hours, the reliability lead cannot reproduce the threshold. The state changes from active to paused. |
| Renewal and archive | The delivery team's requested `active → renewed` transition is rejected because it lacks authority, and that rejection is logged. Only the council may approve a version 2 with a new falsifiable hypothesis. Prompts, tool calls, intermediate outputs, failed hypotheses, costs, objections, and decisions are retained. |

The numbers are illustrative; the control logic is the point. Full cost includes model and tool use, expert review, replication, and the value of the best alternative delayed by another stage. To calculate cost per independently validated result, the organization must retain usage records, reviewer time, failed reproductions, state changes, and rejected renewal attempts. Otherwise the metric is only a slogan.

This logic fits the [U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology's AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework), voluntary guidance released in 2023, with a generative-AI profile added in 2024. Its functions are Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. That framework does not prove a return on persistent search, but it supports the basic discipline of assigning authority, mapping risk, measuring evidence, and managing the decision to continue.

A stopped campaign can still leave a useful asset when its failed routes and evidence are searchable. The archive can keep a later team or agent from paying to rediscover the same dead ends. I think the hardest leadership act will be refusing another stage when the evidence has not improved, even though the system is perfectly willing to continue.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A Tireless Intelligence Charter fixes evidence, limits, independent review, and stopping authority before the search begins. Its decisive control is simple: the team running the campaign cannot authorize its own next stage.</p>
</div>
