A research budget can now buy something that used to be constrained by human endurance: hundreds of serious attempts at the same hard question. On 10 August 2026, [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/riemann-zeta), the AI company behind Claude, reported that an unreleased research version of its model had produced a new result in number theory after 650 initial ideas failed.

The work ran across two sessions in Claude Code, Anthropic's environment for agents that can use files, scripts and command-line tools. Anthropic reports 31 million output tokens, meaning metered units of text generated by the model. After the unsuccessful ideas, Claude spent about a day and a half coordinating roughly 60 subagents, which were delegated Claude runs working on parts of the search and checking one another's work.

I think the important shift is larger than this theorem. An organization could allocate more machine search after failure without asking whether a researcher still had the energy, confidence or career freedom to continue. Fatigue, shame and career risk were not studied here; they are part of my interpretation of the human limits that metered search may loosen. What the record does show is that persistence itself can enter a budget.

That creates a leadership problem. When another batch of attempts is easy to authorize, someone must decide whether the question still deserves them, what new evidence would justify renewal, and who has the power to end the search.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Claude's run makes large-scale persistence an allocatable input, while leaving its causal role and full price unresolved. The scarce judgment moves toward choosing the question, defining evidence and controlling renewal.</p></div>

## The mathematics had an unusually clear finish line

The Riemann zeta function is a mathematical function whose zeros, the inputs where its value becomes zero, help describe the distribution of prime numbers. Its nontrivial zeros exclude a known set at the negative even integers and are the ones that matter to this story. In 1859, German mathematician Bernhard Riemann proposed what became the Riemann hypothesis: every nontrivial zero should lie on the critical line, a particular location in the coordinate system used for complex numbers. The hypothesis remains unproved.

Claude's [technical preprint](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/564f962e60643842f5fcb4a17c9dbc8f608f1c37.pdf), a manuscript released before conventional journal peer review, addresses a narrower question. In increasingly high ranges, it proves that at least two thirds of the nontrivial zeros are both simple, meaning not repeated, and on the critical line. When a zero repeats, the underlying count includes each occurrence. An optimized version reports 0.6725 as the lower bound, the minimum share the proof guarantees.

The previous record was 5/12, or about 41.7%, and had stood since 2020. The popular 37-year framing reaches back to number theorist J. B. Conrey's peer-reviewed 1989 result of more than two fifths, but human mathematicians improved the bound between 1989 and 2020. Claude therefore advanced a long research line; it did not end 37 years without progress.

The preprint also credits earlier mathematical work and identifies its new ingredient as a linear-algebra reading of a sum inherited from that work. Linear algebra is the branch of mathematics concerned with vectors, matrices and the relations between them. Anthropic released a [Lean 4 formalization](https://github.com/anthropics/zeta-23-lean), which translates the claims into a theorem-proving language so software can check a precisely stated proof and its declared assumptions. Formal verification makes the argument more inspectable, although it does not replace normal scholarly scrutiny. Anthropic says internal mathematicians validated the paper and two outside number theorists examined it on short notice.

I think this matters economically because mathematics offered a rare advantage: the system could test ideas against a sharp claim, numerical checks, prior literature and a formal proof. Most strategic questions arrive without anything close to that finish line.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Claude advanced a specific lower bound from 5/12 to at least two thirds; it did not prove the Riemann hypothesis. Decades of prior mathematics and unusually strong verification made this a favorable setting for persistent search.</p></div>

## Search capacity is not economic value

Anthropic's account describes far more than repeated prompting. The agents issued 2,400 command-line operations, wrote hundreds of scripts, ran numerical tests, searched for counterexamples and downloaded 54 papers from arXiv, a public repository for research manuscripts, to look for prior work. Persistence was joined to tools, memory, branching work and checks.

Yet the disclosure does not include a controlled comparison showing that 650 failures caused the breakthrough. It also omits a complete financial denominator: model and tool charges, input tokens, infrastructure, expert-review time and the value of work displaced by this campaign. Thirty-one million output tokens show scale. They do not tell us the cost per validated result.

This distinction appears in ordinary work too. A [2023 working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161), a study circulated before journal peer review, came from the U.S. National Bureau of Economic Research. It followed 5,179 customer-support agents and found that access to an AI assistant raised issues resolved per hour by 14% on average. In a different setting, [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), an independent AI-evaluation organization, reported in 2025 that 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer on 246 tasks when allowed to use early-2025 AI tools, even though they believed they were faster. The second study is now a historical snapshot of older tools, but the contrast remains useful: activity and confidence cannot settle productivity.

My take is that productivity is becoming capital in a literal managerial sense. More attempts can be purchased, run in parallel and redirected. I don't know how the zeta campaign would have performed with half the tokens or twice the expert review, because Anthropic published no such comparison. Without that curve, tirelessness is a capability to govern rather than a return to assume.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Tokens, agents and commands measure search activity, while economic value depends on validated outcomes and the full resources sacrificed to obtain them. The available evidence gives no license to treat a larger loop as a better investment.</p></div>

## Authority should be designed before persistence is purchased

A tireless system can keep weak ideas alive by producing another analysis, another simulation and another confident memo. I think leaders should require a short Tireless Intelligence Charter before funding that behavior. It is a written agreement about four decisions, not a general instruction to keep trying.

**Choose the question and owner.** Name the decision, theorem or experiment, the observable result that would make it valuable, and the person accountable for asking it. “Find something useful” is not a search target because almost any output can be declared a success afterward.

**Freeze the evidence test.** Define success, disconfirming evidence, novelty and the required independent check before results begin to arrive. The verifier should fail differently from the system doing the work, whether that means a domain expert, external data, a controlled test or formal proof.

**Fund stages against a full denominator.** Each stage needs limits for model and tool spend, elapsed time, tool permissions and reviewer hours. Renewal should depend on a new testable hypothesis or improvement on a validated measure, while cost per validated outcome should include infrastructure, replication and the next-best project that lost the resources.

**Separate renewal from delivery.** The team running the search may recommend another stage, but a named budget owner should authorize it and an independent reviewer should be able to block it when the evidence gate fails. The charter should also stop the work when the same failure returns without a new hypothesis, verification cannot reproduce the claim, or the search demands authority beyond its original scope. Saving rejected paths and their evidence can preserve value from a stopped effort and keep the next team from paying for the same dead ends.

I think this is what leadership becomes when execution and persistence are easier to buy. Human contribution does not collapse into administration; people still supply ideas, domain knowledge, interpretation and accountability. But the ability to refuse another round becomes as important as the ability to launch one.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A persistent AI search needs a named question owner, a frozen evidence test, staged funding, independent review and separate renewal authority. Precommitted stops protect the portfolio from turning output volume into its own justification.</p></div>

The mapping is repetition joined to retained evidence and a criterion for progress. Claude's discarded proof paths had value only when checks could redirect the next search; another round without a new hypothesis would merely consume resources. The human responsibility is to decide which day deserves repeating, what would count as escape, and who has the authority to end the loop.
