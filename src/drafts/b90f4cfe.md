On August 10, 2026, Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude, [reported](https://www.anthropic.com/research/riemann-zeta) that an unreleased research version of its system had generated and tried 650 ideas that did not work. The run used 31 million output tokens, units of model-generated text used to meter usage, and about 60 subagents, delegated AI processes assigned narrower parts of the work. It took place across two sessions in Claude Code, Anthropic's environment for agents that can use files, scripts, and command-line tools.

The result concerns the Riemann zeta function, a mathematical object whose zeros reveal deep patterns in the distribution of prime numbers. In 1859, German mathematician Bernhard Riemann proposed that all of its nontrivial zeros, special inputs where the function's value becomes zero, sit on the critical line. That line is a precise position in the complex-number map used by the function, where the real part of each number is one-half. Claude did not prove this Riemann hypothesis. Its [paper](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/564f962e60643842f5fcb4a17c9dbc8f608f1c37.pdf) raised the unconditional lower bound, the minimum share mathematicians can prove lies on that line without assuming the hypothesis, from 5/12 to at least 2/3. The proof now guarantees two-thirds; the claim that every zero lies there remains open.

Persistence clearly mattered, but it was one part of a larger mechanism that included decades of human mathematics, a new interpretation of earlier work, computation, and several kinds of checking. I think the economic change is more important than the heroic story. A large search that once would have competed directly with scarce human attention can now be allocated and metered as computing capacity. That moves the leadership problem toward choosing which questions deserve such persistence, what evidence will count, and when the spending must end.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The mathematical advance is real but narrower than a solution to the Riemann hypothesis. Its strategic importance lies in making sustained search a fundable resource, which puts question choice, evidence standards, and termination in the hands of leaders.</p></div>

## What the 650 failures actually bought

Anthropic says the agents issued 2,400 shell commands, wrote hundreds of Python scripts, checked calculations against known zeta zeros, searched for counterexamples, and downloaded 54 papers from arXiv, an open repository where researchers share papers before journal review. This was an instrumented search rather than a stream of guesses. Each failed path could produce information for another path, while tools made parts of the work testable.

Scale does not explain the result by itself. Number theorist J. B. Conrey's peer-reviewed 1989 paper established that more than two-fifths of the zeros lie on the critical line, and later human work improved that guarantee to 5/12 by 2020. The new preprint, a research paper released before journal peer review, says its distinctive step was a “linear-algebraic reading” of an earlier result about how zeta zeros are spaced. In plain language, it used algebra to reinterpret an existing finding and open a route to a stronger proof. The popular 37-year frame therefore marks the time since Conrey's paper, not a period in which mathematicians stood still.

The checks mattered as much as the retries. Anthropic reports numerical tests, searches for counterexamples, review by its own mathematicians, and examination by two outside number theorists on short notice. It also released a [Lean 4 formalization](https://github.com/anthropics/zeta-23-lean). Lean 4 is a theorem-proving programming language, and formal verification means its proof assistant checks a precisely encoded theorem and its dependencies under declared logical assumptions. That makes the proof easier to audit, although it does not establish novelty or replace normal journal peer review.

I don't know whether half the agents or a tenth of the tokens would have reached the same result. Anthropic published no controlled comparison, public invoice, or denominator showing how often similar campaigns fail completely. I think the defensible conclusion is narrower: an organization can now provision a large, bounded search around a clear target and strong checks. This case demonstrates possibility, not a reliable return on every 31 million tokens.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The search budget paid for breadth, tools, and repeated checks, while the successful proof still depended on a human-built research frontier and a conceptual move. With no controlled comparison or cost ledger, the case establishes a new capability rather than an expected rate of return.</p></div>

## When tirelessness becomes a liability

Mathematics is unusually friendly to persistent agents because a theorem can be stated precisely and a proof can be checked. Business questions usually arrive in softer forms: improve the product, find a market, make the organization faster. If leaders cannot say what observable result would support or reject the hunch, a tireless system can produce polished reasons to continue until activity begins to impersonate evidence.

Workplace studies show why output volume cannot serve as a shortcut for value. A [2023 working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161) from the National Bureau of Economic Research, a U.S. economics research organization, studied 5,179 customer-support workers and found that access to an AI assistant raised issues resolved per hour by 14% on average, with larger gains among less experienced workers. In a different setting, a [2025 randomized study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) by METR, an independent group that evaluates AI capabilities, found that experienced open-source developers took 19% longer on its measured tasks when allowed to use early-2025 AI tools. The studies cover different work, people, and systems. Together they show that value depends on the task and must be measured where the work happens.

Anthropic did not measure fatigue, shame, or career risk in the zeta project. Those words describe human pressures that can make repeated failure hard to sustain; they are an interpretation of the economic shift, not a finding from the research. The firmer point is that retries can increasingly be bought with computing capacity instead of drawing entirely on human endurance. I think this is what it means to say productivity is becoming capital: capacity can be provisioned, but judgment still has to allocate it.

My take is that tirelessness magnifies the quality of the objective. A precise question with independent checks can benefit from another thousand attempts. A weak hunch with no test can become an expensive campaign that manufactures its own confidence. A stopping rule protects the search from that failure by forcing the organization to define progress before the persuasive reports begin to arrive.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Enterprise questions rarely come with the clean acceptance tests available in mathematics. Persistent AI therefore creates value only when progress is observable; without that discipline, cheap retries can turn an untested hunch into a costly source of false confidence.</p></div>

## Leadership begins before the first attempt

A persistent-agent campaign should begin with a written charter. Name the decision, theorem, design, or experiment that the search will serve, then state what success would look like, what evidence would prove the idea wrong, and how novelty will be checked. “Find something useful about this market” grants permission to keep spending. A bounded question gives the work an answerable purpose.

The charter also needs an independent verifier with a different failure mode. A proof assistant suited the zeta theorem; a product claim might require a controlled test, external data, or a domain expert who did not generate the answer. I think a second agent using the same model, sources, and assumptions may reproduce the first agent's confidence rather than check its claim. Verification has to fit the way the original work could be wrong.

Funding should arrive in stages. Set limits for output tokens, attempt paths, elapsed time, tool permissions, and human review, then release another batch only when the search clears a declared evidence gate or produces a new testable idea. Measure cost per validated outcome, including reviewer time, false positives, and failed replication, rather than celebrating messages or agent-hours. Save rejected paths with their tests and sources so a later system does not pay to rediscover the same dead ends.

Stopping conditions belong in the charter as well. Pause when the same failure returns without a new idea, independent review cannot reproduce the result, the system asks for authority beyond its original scope, or the next batch has less expected value than another candidate in the portfolio. The [2023 AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) from the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, the federal standards agency, offers useful verbs for this discipline: govern, map, measure, and manage. In ordinary terms, leaders set authority, identify risk, test evidence, and change or end the work as results arrive.

Humans still contribute ideas, domain knowledge, interpretation, relationships, and moral accountability. Yet as another attempt becomes cheaper, the decisions around the attempt become more consequential. In Doug Liman's 2014 science-fiction film *Edge of Tomorrow*, a soldier repeats the same fatal battle and uses each reset as feedback. AI can supply the resets, but repetition becomes learning only when evidence changes the next move and someone remains accountable for ending the loop.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Write the evidence, budget, verifier, and stopping conditions before a tireless search begins, then judge it by validated outcomes and preserve what its failures teach. A tireless system can always ask for another attempt; only accountable leadership can decide that it no longer deserves one.</p></div>
