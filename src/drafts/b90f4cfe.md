On August 10, 2026, [Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude](https://www.anthropic.com/research/riemann-zeta), reported an unusual mathematical result. Anthropic says an unreleased Claude research system first tried 650 ideas that failed. It then spent a day and a half coordinating about 60 subagents, delegated AI runs that explored parts of the problem and checked one another's work.

The result emerged across two sessions in Claude Code, Anthropic's tool-using coding environment, and involved 31 million output tokens. An output token is a metered unit of text generated by a model. It records activity, not a public invoice.

The easy interpretation is that Claude beat mathematicians through stamina. I think stamina is part of the strategic surprise, but the evidence does not show that retries alone caused the result. The work also depended on decades of human mathematics, a new conceptual move, software checks, and specialist examination. Anthropic did not measure fatigue, shame, or career risk; those are human frictions that make repeated failure hard to absorb, not findings from this project.

Yet the economic possibility has changed. An organization can now provision repeated search as a bounded, metered capacity rather than asking one person's nervous system to carry every dead end. My take is that leadership becomes more important at that point, because someone must decide which question deserves attempt 651, what would justify another stage, and who can say no.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Claude's result does not establish that persistence caused a breakthrough. It shows that repeated search can become a funded input, turning attempt 651 into an explicit leadership decision.</p>

## What Claude actually moved

The Riemann zeta function is a mathematical function whose zeros, the input points where its value equals zero, help describe patterns in the distribution of prime numbers. It has a fully understood sequence of trivial zeros at the negative even integers: minus two, minus four, minus six, and so on. Its other zeros are called nontrivial zeros, and they sit inside a vertical region of a mathematical coordinate plane known as the critical strip.

German mathematician Bernhard Riemann proposed in 1859 that every nontrivial zero lies on the line halfway through that strip, known as the critical line. This is the Riemann hypothesis. Nobody has proved it, and Claude did not prove it.

The [revised Claude preprint dated August 11, 2026](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/95c246936988e43127bc6b2ceb7077c1dad2d68e.pdf), a research paper released before conventional journal peer review, establishes an asymptotic lower bound. In plain English, as mathematicians count farther through the nontrivial zeros, at least two thirds are proven to be simple and on the critical line. A simple zero occurs once, while multiplicity means that a repeated zero is counted according to its number of repetitions. The theorem does not classify the remaining third.

Anthropic's public announcement uses 67.2% as its headline figure. The revised paper gives a general result of at least two thirds, or about 66.7%, and an optimized calculation of 0.6725, or 67.25%, using the Montgomery–Taylor window, a particular test function chosen for that calculation. These are related layers of the result, which is why a serious description should name both rather than slide between them.

The previous record was 5/12, about 41.7%, reached in 2020. Moving from 5/12 to two thirds raises the proven minimum by 25 percentage points. The popular “37-year record” line reaches back to number theorist J. B. Conrey's [1989 peer-reviewed result](https://doi.org/10.1515/crll.1989.399.1) that more than two fifths of the zeros lie on the critical line. Human mathematicians kept improving the bound between 1989 and 2020, so 37 years does not mean 37 years without progress.

The causal picture is richer than brute force. The Claude paper builds on a [2024 peer-reviewed number-theory result](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04799) about pair correlation, a way of studying how the zeros are spaced in relation to one another. Its new ingredient recasts part of that work as a structured calculation about relationships among quantities, the kind of problem mathematicians call linear algebra. Anthropic also reports 2,400 shell commands, hundreds of Python scripts, numerical checks, searches for counterexamples, and a review of 54 research papers for prior work.

The checks form an evidence ladder rather than one final stamp. Anthropic published the process account and preprint, says two internal mathematicians validated the paper, and says two specialist number theorists examined it on short notice. Claude also produced a [Lean 4 formalization](https://github.com/anthropics/zeta-23-lean). Lean 4 is a programming language for machine-checkable proofs; formal verification means the software checked a precisely encoded statement and proof against declared logical rules. It improves auditability, but it does not reproduce the discovery process, provide independent replication, or replace broader scrutiny by the mathematical community. I think the result becomes more credible when those boundaries remain visible.

## The economics begin at attempt 651

Thirty-one million output tokens tell us that the search was large. They do not tell us its total cost or return. Anthropic did not publish the input-token total, model price, infrastructure and energy costs, reviewer costs, or a denominator of comparable campaigns that produced nothing. I don't know whether this scale was necessary, and the public evidence cannot answer that question.

“Attempt 651” is my shorthand for the renewal decision, not an attempt Anthropic numbered. After a bounded stage, the relevant question is whether another batch is likely to produce a stronger testable hypothesis, clear an evidence gate, or preserve useful knowledge. The economic unit is the option to buy another stage, not the impressive volume of the stage already consumed.

In my Three Crucibles framing, productivity becomes capital-like when an organization can fund capacity in advance and reuse what it produces over time. Persistent search earns that description only when it leaves durable assets: stable tests, verified methods, and a searchable record of failed hypotheses. An archive matters because a stopped campaign can still prevent the next system from paying to rediscover the same dead ends.

Evidence from ordinary work shows why activity cannot stand in for value. A [2023 working paper from the U.S. National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161), an economic research organization, studied 5,179 customer-support workers and found a 14% average increase in issues resolved per hour with an AI assistant. In a different setting, a [2025 randomized study by METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), an independent AI-evaluation research group, found that 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with the early-2025 tools tested. METR now labels that result historical rather than representative of newer systems. Neither study concerns mathematical discovery. Together, they show why every productivity claim needs a defined task, full costs, and an acceptance measure.

I think attempt 651 should face a portfolio test among comparable campaigns with the same success rule. A new stage must promise more independently accepted value after model, tool, review, and delay costs than the strongest project that would otherwise receive those resources. Without that comparison, persistence may be purchasable, but we have not shown that it is productive capital.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Attempt 651 is an investment decision. Persistent search becomes capital-like only when it leaves reusable evidence and beats the best alternative use of the same model, tool, review, and time budget.</p>
</div>

## Leadership before the loop

I think a persistent AI campaign should begin with a Tireless Intelligence Charter: a one-page agreement that fixes the question, evidence, operating limits, budget, and decision rights before the system starts producing persuasive reasons to continue. This is a proposal, not an operating standard demonstrated by the Claude case. The task evidence contains no enterprise pilot showing that the charter improves a real renewal or stop decision.

| Charter part | What must be fixed before launch |
| --- | --- |
| Question and evidence | The observable question and the tests for success, disconfirmation, novelty, and independent acceptance. |
| Operating boundaries | Allowed models, tools, data, licensing, storage, contractual limits, supplier exit, and required human oversight. |
| Budget and review | Limits for elapsed time, model and tool spend, reviewer hours, a prebooked review date, and the strongest portfolio alternative. |
| Decision rights | The sponsor, delivery lead, independent verifier, renewal authority, archive owner, evidence version, and named approvers. |

The mechanism is precommitment. The delivery team's agents may run the search through named service accounts, machine identities whose permissions and actions can be audited, but neither those accounts nor the delivery lead may change a campaign from paused to renewed. A verifier signs a fixed evidence package, and a separate portfolio authority decides whether another stage beats the alternative. The record must preserve failed routes, the evidence version, the decision, and the approvers in a form that makes later changes visible.

Consider a hypothetical first test. One campaign receives a week, a capped model-and-tool budget, and 20 reviewer hours. At the review it has no independently accepted result, only one new testable hypothesis. A renewal submitted by the delivery system is rejected because the verifier and portfolio approvals are missing. The verifier can then test the hypothesis against the predeclared rule, while the portfolio authority compares another week with the strongest unfunded candidate. Persistence remains possible, but self-renewal does not.

Stopping authority can also be wrong. Frontier work needs one bounded appeal when a challenger brings new external evidence, a genuinely new testable hypothesis, or a flaw in the original acceptance rule. The archive should record a false stop when a terminated route later clears that original rule under comparable conditions. Otherwise leaders will measure runaway spending while remaining blind to premature termination.

The charter should earn its own budget. Pilot it within one comparable class of campaigns using a frozen acceptance rule. Report accepted and zero-result stages, reviewer effort, elapsed time, and reusable artifacts. Track unsupported renewals, accepted errors, false stops, and the project that received any released capacity. I think that is the first honest test of whether the charter changes decisions or merely gives disciplined funding checkpoints a new name.

Humans are not reduced to budget police in this model. They still contribute domain ideas, interpretation, relationships, values, and accountability. But when a tireless system can build a polished case for almost any hunch, the irreducibly human responsibility is to make persistence answer to purpose before momentum starts speaking as if it were evidence.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A Tireless Intelligence Charter separates the right to search from the right to renew, fixes evidence before launch, preserves failures, and permits one bounded appeal. Its value must be tested on comparable campaigns rather than assumed.</p>
</div>

<p>Doug Liman's 2014 science-fiction film follows Major William Cage, a soldier who dies in combat and returns to the same day carrying knowledge from earlier repetitions. He improves because information survives each reset and changes what he tries next.</p>
<p>The mapping to persistent AI search is direct: repetition creates value only when evidence carries forward, failed routes remain visible, and an exit condition exists before the next cycle begins. The machine can own the repetition. Leadership owns whether another day is worth buying.</p>
