A failed idea is rarely just an entry in a spreadsheet. For a person, it consumes attention and can bring frustration, embarrassment, or professional risk. On August 10, 2026, Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude, described a mathematical search in which the next attempt no longer depended on one person absorbing all of that friction.

According to [Anthropic’s account](https://www.anthropic.com/research/riemann-zeta), an unreleased research version of its Claude AI system generated and tried 650 initial ideas, and none worked. Across two sessions in Claude Code, Anthropic’s coding-agent environment for working with files and command-line tools, the project used 31 million output tokens. An output token is a metered unit of text generated by a model. The later search coordinated about 60 subagents, meaning delegated instances of Claude working on separate tasks, over roughly a day and a half.

Those numbers do not show that persistence alone caused the result. Anthropic published neither a full cost ledger nor a controlled comparison with a smaller search. The proof also depended on decades of human mathematics and a new conceptual step, followed by several kinds of checking. My take is narrower: persistence is becoming a budget choice. I think the popular story about machine stamina misses the harder question of what deserves that capacity.

Anthropic did not measure fatigue, shame, or career risk, so I am not treating them as findings from this case. They describe human pressures that can end a search before another idea is tested. Compute does not remove the cost of failure; it changes who bears it and where the decision appears.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Anthropic’s figures document a large, tool-using search, not proof that retries alone produced the theorem. The strategic change is that another attempt can now be funded as system capacity instead of being limited entirely by one person’s willingness to continue.</p></div>

## The result is narrower than the headline

The mathematics begins with the Riemann zeta function, a complex mathematical function whose zeros carry information about the distribution of prime numbers. A zero is an input where the function equals zero. The readily classified “trivial” zeros occur at the negative even integers. The remaining “nontrivial” zeros are the difficult ones connected to German mathematician Bernhard Riemann’s 1859 work on primes.

For complex-number inputs, the critical line is the vertical line where the real part equals one half. The still-unproved Riemann hypothesis says every nontrivial zero lies on that line. Claude did not prove it.

Claude’s [2026 technical paper](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/564f962e60643842f5fcb4a17c9dbc8f608f1c37.pdf), released by Anthropic as a preprint rather than a conventionally peer-reviewed journal article, raises a lower bound from 5/12 to at least two thirds; an optimized version reaches 0.6725. A lower bound is a proven minimum share, not a complete account of every zero. More precisely, as the counting range moves higher, the theorem says at least that share of the total count consists of simple zeros on the line. A simple zero occurs once. The total comparison includes repeated zeros according to their multiplicity, or number of occurrences.

The “37-year” description also needs care. Number theorist J. B. Conrey’s [peer-reviewed 1989 paper](https://doi.org/10.1515/crll.1989.399.1) proved that more than two fifths of the zeros lie on the critical line. Human researchers continued to improve the result, reaching the prior 5/12 record in a [paper appearing in a 2020 journal volume](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40687-019-0199-8). The 37 years run from Conrey’s paper to the 2026 result; the bound was not frozen throughout that period.

The new argument also credits a [2024 peer-reviewed paper](https://doi.org/10.4064/aa230612-20-3) by the number theorists Baluyot, Goldston, Suriajaya, and Turnage-Butterbaugh. Their work concerns pair correlation, a way of studying how pairs of zeta zeros are spaced. Claude’s paper describes its new ingredient as a linear-algebraic reading of a sum from that earlier result. I think the achievement becomes more interesting when stated this precisely: the system searched a frontier built by people and found a new way to connect existing parts.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The preprint raises the proven minimum share of the total zero count on the critical line from 5/12 to at least two thirds, with an optimized value of 0.6725; it also proves that at least two thirds are simple and on the line. It does not prove the Riemann hypothesis, and it builds on a human research line that continued to advance between 1989 and 2026.</p></div>

## Persistence becomes a capital decision

This was not a chatbot repeating the same answer 650 times. Anthropic reports 2,400 shell commands and hundreds of Python scripts, along with numerical checks and searches for counterexamples. The agents also downloaded 54 papers from arXiv, the open repository researchers use to share preprints, to check whether the result was already known.

Anthropic also released a [formalization in Lean 4](https://github.com/anthropics/zeta-23-lean), a theorem-proving language that lets a proof assistant check a precisely encoded theorem and its declared dependencies. This process, called formal verification, improves auditability but does not decide the theorem’s importance or replace wider scrutiny. Anthropic says its own mathematicians validated the paper and that two outside specialists examined it on short notice; this is still different from completed journal peer review or independent replication.

I don’t know how often a search of this size will produce a useful discovery. Anthropic gives us no count of comparable campaigns and no experiment that varies the number of agents or tokens. This case therefore supports a modest conclusion: a large, organized search can produce a checkable result in a field with unusually clear tests. It does not establish an average return on tireless AI research.

In my Three Crucibles framing, productivity increasingly becomes capital. By capital, I mean capacity that an organization can buy and assign to a chosen problem, rather than effort available only through additional human hours. I think this result makes that shift concrete. The 31 million output tokens are a usage measure, not economic value or a public invoice. They show that persistence can enter a budget alongside coordination and reviewer time.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The economic change is the ability to allocate measured search capacity with verification built around it. The return remains uncertain, and the full cost remains undisclosed.</p></div>

## Write the stopping rule first

Purchasable persistence creates a new temptation. An organization can give an executive hunch another batch, then another, until the system produces a persuasive trail of supportive material. The model does not need to become more correct for the campaign to become more convincing. I think this is where leadership can fail: activity is easy to count, while validated value is harder to define.

Results from ordinary work already warn against treating AI use as a universal productivity gain. A [2023 working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161) from the National Bureau of Economic Research, a U.S. research organization, studied 5,179 customer-support agents and found a 14% average increase in issues resolved per hour. In a different setting, the independent AI research group METR [reported in 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) that 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with the early-2025 tools tested. METR now labels that result historical because the tools have changed. The studies show why output must be judged inside a specific task and workflow.

Before a persistent search begins, leaders need a short decision contract:

1. **Name the question.** State the claim or decision and give it an observable acceptance test. “Find something useful” cannot govern a search.
2. **Define the evidence.** Write down what would count as success and what would disconfirm the claim. Add a novelty check and an independent verifier with a different failure mode from the generator, such as a domain expert or controlled test.
3. **Fund the work in stages.** Set initial limits for tokens, elapsed time, tool access, and reviewer hours. Release the next budget only when the search clears the evidence threshold set at the start.
4. **State the stop and preserve the failures.** Pause when the same error returns without a new hypothesis, a reviewer cannot reproduce the claim, authority grows beyond the brief, or another problem offers a better expected return. Save rejected paths so a future search does not pay to rediscover them.

This discipline is consistent with the [AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) released in 2023 by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, the federal measurement agency. Its voluntary guidance asks organizations to govern the system, map its context, measure results, and manage risk. It cannot prove that a search will pay off, but it keeps the basis for continuation visible.

Humans are not reduced to approving budgets. Experts still contribute ideas and domain judgment while remaining accountable for consequences. The distinct leadership obligation is to make continuation earn its funding instead of allowing tirelessness to become its own argument.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A persistent AI campaign should begin with a precise question, a prewritten evidence standard, and a stop tied to staged funding. Measure cost per validated outcome, and preserve failed paths so ending a campaign still leaves useful knowledge.</p></div>
