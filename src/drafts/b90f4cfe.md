Thirty-one million output tokens sounds like a price, but it is only a quantity. An output token is a unit of text generated by an AI model, useful for metering activity but insufficient for calculating the full cost or value of the work.

In August 2026, [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/riemann-zeta), the AI company behind Claude, reported that an unreleased research version of its system had tried 650 unsuccessful mathematical ideas. The work ran across two sessions in Claude Code, Anthropic's environment for agents that can use files and software tools over many steps. Anthropic says the effort consumed 31 million output tokens and, during roughly a day and a half, coordinated about 60 subagents, meaning delegated AI runs assigned to narrower parts of the problem.

The public account gives us no complete invoice, controlled comparison, or standard definition of an “idea.” It therefore cannot show that the number of retries caused the result. It does reveal a change in what an organization can fund: a large search no longer has to fit inside one person's patience, working day, or willingness to absorb another dead end. I think the durable leadership question is who gets to buy that persistence, for which problem, and under what conditions it must end.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Anthropic reported an unusually large and successful AI search, but its activity figures are neither a cost ledger nor proof of causation. The important shift is that repeated attempts can now be commissioned in bounded batches.</p>

## What the run actually produced

The result concerns the Riemann zeta function, a mathematical function whose zeros reveal fine patterns in how prime numbers are distributed. In 1859, German mathematician Bernhard Riemann proposed what became the Riemann hypothesis: all of the function's relevant, or nontrivial, zeros should lie on a particular line called the critical line. The hypothesis remains unproved.

A lower bound answers a narrower question. It establishes the minimum share of those zeros that can be proved to sit on the critical line, without claiming that all of them do. The [Claude-authored preprint](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/564f962e60643842f5fcb4a17c9dbc8f608f1c37.pdf) raises that minimum from 5/12, about 41.7%, to at least 2/3, about 66.7%, and reports an optimized value of 0.6725. The basic theorem is a 25-percentage-point advance, but it does not solve the Riemann hypothesis.

The popular “37-year record” framing reaches back to [number theorist J. B. Conrey's 1989 paper](https://doi.org/10.1515/crll.1989.399.1), which proved that more than two fifths of the zeros lie on the line. Mathematics did not stand still afterward. Later work pushed the record to 5/12 in 2020, and the new argument also depends on a [peer-reviewed 2024 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04799) by four number theorists about relationships between the spacing of zeros. Claude's paper adds a new way to reorganize that relationship using linear algebra, the mathematics of vectors and matrices.

Its evidence status matters. The paper came from an unreleased system and has not completed conventional journal peer review. Anthropic says company mathematicians validated it and two outside specialists examined it on short notice. A public [Lean 4 formalization](https://github.com/anthropics/zeta-23-lean) adds another layer: Lean 4 is a theorem-proving language that checks a precisely encoded statement and each logical step. That is strong protection against a hidden gap in the encoded proof, while significance, novelty, and independent replication still require scrutiny from the field.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Claude advanced a specific proven minimum rather than solving the Riemann hypothesis. The result combines decades of human mathematics, a new conceptual step, extensive search, formal checking, and early expert review, so “persistence alone” is too simple an explanation.</p>
</div>

## Persistence moves the expense downstream

Anthropic's account describes more than repeated prompting. The agents wrote and ran scripts, tested numerical cases, searched for counterexamples, reviewed one another's work, and downloaded 54 papers from arXiv, an open repository for research preprints, to check whether the idea was already known. This was an instrumented search in which failed routes could inform later work, not 60 digital employees independently guessing at an answer.

For a human researcher, each extra route consumes attention and time and may carry emotional or professional costs. Anthropic did not measure fatigue, shame, or career risk, so the case cannot turn those experiences into an economic finding. The narrower change is operational: model attempts can be allocated through a compute budget even when a person would have had to move on.

That moves scarcity rather than removing it. Candidate ideas can arrive faster, but independent judgment, specialist attention, and authority to act do not automatically scale with them. If an organization funds generation without reserving capacity to reject, reproduce, and interpret the results, it creates a verification queue. A tireless system can then produce both the proposal and an endless stream of polished reasons to continue pursuing it.

The useful unit of account is therefore not tokens, agents, or attempts. It is the full cost per independently accepted outcome, including review, replication, data access, and the opportunity cost of work that was not funded. Failed routes also belong in that account. Preserved with their tests and source trail, they can prevent the next campaign from paying to rediscover the same dead ends.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI makes candidate search easier to buy, while verification and judgment remain scarce. Leaders should measure the full cost of accepted outcomes and preserve rejected routes, rather than treating model activity as productivity.</p>
</div>

## Make the next batch earn its budget

A persistent AI campaign needs an admission test before it needs a stopping rule. The sponsor should be able to name the decision the work could change, the current measurable baseline, evidence that would disprove the working hunch, an independent reviewer with enough time, the permitted data and tools, and the best alternative use of the same budget. If those answers are vague, the question is not ready for a swarm.

Authorization should then cover a finite batch of attempts, time, tool access, and reviewer hours. Renewal requires a declared change in evidence: a metric improves under a fair comparison, an independent check survives, a meaningful uncertainty disappears, or a genuinely new falsifiable route emerges. More output and a better-written case for continuation do not qualify.

The stopping rule also needs a real enforcement point. Before launching another subagent or tool action, the system should check a current authorization held outside the delivery team's control. An expired authorization, a risk veto, or an attempted self-renewal blocks new work; already-running tasks follow a pre-agreed rule to finish safely or be cancelled. For enterprise data, the same authorization should name classification, storage location, retention period, supplier exit conditions, and the person with authority to stop the campaign.

This review belongs in an existing portfolio forum, where the business sponsor owns the value claim and an independent domain expert owns acceptance of the evidence. Its small dashboard should track accepted outcomes, full cost, reviewer delay, renewals granted without new evidence, and stopped searches later judged worth reopening. The last measure matters because a stopping regime can become timid too. Good governance has to learn from premature stops as well as runaway campaigns.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Fund persistent search only when the question has material value, a measurable baseline, disconfirming evidence, independent review, bounded access, and a better return than the next portfolio option. Let authorization expire, and renew it only when the evidence changes.</p>
</div>
