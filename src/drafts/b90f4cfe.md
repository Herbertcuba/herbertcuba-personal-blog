Six hundred and fifty ideas failed. Anthropic then let an unreleased research version of Claude keep working. On 10 August 2026, the AI company [reported](https://www.anthropic.com/research/riemann-zeta) that the resulting search had advanced a mathematical bound connected to the Riemann hypothesis, one of the most famous open problems in number theory, the branch of mathematics concerned with whole numbers and primes.

The work ran over two sessions in Claude Code, Anthropic's environment for AI agents that can use files, scripts and command-line tools. Anthropic reports 31 million output tokens, metered units of text generated by the model, and about a day and a half coordinating roughly 60 subagents. A subagent here means a delegated Claude run working on one part of the search, not a digital employee.

Calling this a victory by stamina alone would erase the conceptual step in the proof, the decades of human mathematics beneath it and the work required to check it. It would also claim more than the evidence allows: Anthropic published no controlled comparison showing that 650 failed ideas caused the result.

Still, I think the organizational change is profound. The experiment did not study fatigue, shame or career risk, but those are real human pressures that can narrow a long search. A machine campaign converts part of that endurance constraint into a metered operating input, so the scarce leadership question becomes which problems deserve another round and who can refuse it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Claude's run makes extraordinary persistence something an organization can allocate, without proving that more retries cause more value. Leadership therefore has to govern the question, the evidence and the right to continue.</p></div>

## What the theorem actually changed

The Riemann zeta function is a mathematical function whose zeros, the inputs where its value becomes zero, reveal increasingly fine information about the distribution of prime numbers. Its nontrivial zeros are the ones relevant here. In 1859, German mathematician Bernhard Riemann proposed what became the Riemann hypothesis: all those zeros should lie on the critical line, a specific vertical line in the function's coordinate system. The hypothesis remains unproved.

Claude did not prove it. Anthropic's live post was updated on 13 August to link a [revised 17-page preprint dated 11 August](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/95c246936988e43127bc6b2ceb7077c1dad2d68e.pdf). A preprint is a research manuscript released before conventional journal peer review, and this one is authored as Claude and published by Anthropic, the organization that ran the experiment.

The paper proves that, in increasingly high ranges, at least two thirds of all nontrivial zeros, counted with multiplicity, are both simple and on the critical line. Simple means a zero is not repeated; counted with multiplicity means that every occurrence of a repeated zero belongs in the underlying count. An optimized version gives 0.6725 as the lower bound, the minimum share the proof guarantees.

The previous record was 5/12, about 41.7%, and had stood since 2020. The popular “37-year record” reaches back to number theorist J. B. Conrey's peer-reviewed 1989 result of more than two fifths, but later human work improved the bound before 2020. This was a major advance in a living research line, not the end of 37 motionless years.

The revised paper credits earlier mathematical work and describes its new ingredient as a linear-algebraic reading of an existing sum. Linear algebra is the part of mathematics that studies vectors, matrices and the relations between them. Anthropic also released a [Lean 4 formalization](https://github.com/anthropics/zeta-23-lean), meaning the proof was encoded so a theorem-proving program could check the stated claims and dependencies. That is a serious validation layer, while remaining different from journal peer review; Anthropic says its own mathematicians studied and validated the work and two outside specialists examined it on short notice.

I think this setting gave persistent search an unusually clear finish line. An idea could face numerical checks, a precise theorem, prior literature, specialist examination and formal verification. Most strategic questions do not arrive with evidence gates that sharp, which is why the economic lesson begins with evidence design rather than agent count.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Claude raised a specific lower bound from 5/12 to at least two thirds; it did not prove the Riemann hypothesis. Prior human mathematics and unusually strong ways to test the result made this a favorable case for persistent AI search.</p></div>

## Persistence is a metered operating input

Anthropic's account describes an instrumented search rather than 650 copies of one prompt. The agents issued 2,400 shell commands, wrote hundreds of scripts, ran numerical checks, looked for counterexamples and downloaded 54 papers from arXiv, a public repository for research manuscripts, to check for prior work. Persistence was combined with tools, branching work, memory and review.

The public record still cannot reproduce the entire campaign. Anthropic's [transcript document](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/8a0d1add3c637b858a9a181e98c40e9548c3f44f.pdf) gives annotated records for two selected subagents that developed the successful route, but its introduction says private reasoning is missing and most tool calls are represented by summaries. The unreleased model build, total input-token use, generation settings, complete branches and comparable runs at other budgets are also absent from the disclosure. Even “650 ideas” is a category Anthropic defined; the disclosure does not show how those ideas were divided, related or counted across the search.

My take is that persistent search has become a purchasable operating input. When it is funded in stages, leaders can buy the next batch only if the evidence improves. That does not turn productivity into an accounting asset. Thirty-one million output tokens show volume, but the public account gives no complete cost ledger for models, tools, infrastructure, expert review, replication or the next-best project that lost those resources.

I don't know whether half the tokens would have found the same proof, or whether twice the expert review would have exposed a hidden problem. Anthropic did not publish those comparisons. The useful measure is therefore full cost per independently accepted result, not tokens consumed or attempts survived.

Evidence from ordinary work reinforces that caution. A [2023 working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161) from the U.S. National Bureau of Economic Research studied 5,179 customer-support agents and found that an AI assistant increased issues resolved per hour by 14% on average. In another setting, the independent evaluation group METR found in 2025 that 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer on 246 tasks with early-2025 AI tools. In a [February 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/), METR said newer tools probably helped more, but selection and time-measurement problems made its newer estimate unreliable. These results cannot be averaged into a universal AI productivity rate; they show why value must be measured inside the actual task and workflow.

This creates an AI-specific danger. Tireless systems can generate renewal arguments faster than people can verify them, so every failed gate can produce another analysis, revised hypothesis or persuasive memo asking for more budget. Reviewer capacity becomes the bottleneck, and a campaign can start using its own volume as evidence that it deserves to continue.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agent activity is now easy to meter and buy, but it is not economic value. The decision metric is full cost per independently accepted result, especially when machines can manufacture renewal arguments faster than reviewers can test them.</p></div>

## Make stopping an enforceable decision

I think ordinary stage gates need one harder boundary for tireless systems: the search cannot cross its own budget or evidence limit on the strength of its own recommendation. A Tireless Intelligence Charter should therefore be a one-page control record, not a slogan. Before launch, it freezes four things:

- **Question and value.** Name the question owner, the observable outcome that would matter and the next-best project competing for the same resources.
- **Evidence version.** Record the exact success test, disconfirming evidence, novelty test and independent evaluator before results begin to arrive.
- **Stage envelope.** Set limits for model and tool spend, tokens, elapsed time, permissions, reviewer hours and the decision date. Existing data, procurement, security and worker-consultation gates remain prerequisites when relevant.
- **Authority and archive.** Name the delivery owner, budget owner, independent evaluator, person with kill authority and owner of the final evidence record.

The control becomes real at the orchestration layer, the software that assigns work and releases the next budget. A campaign begins as PROPOSED and becomes ACTIVE only after the required owners sign. Hitting any cap or review date moves it automatically to PAUSED. Only a passed independent evidence check plus authorized funding can reactivate it; a delivery team's attempt to renew itself is an invalid transition that the system rejects. Otherwise the campaign becomes CLOSED, and its prompts, tests, accepted evidence, rejected paths and decision history enter a write-once archive that later users can read but not alter.

Consider a clearly hypothetical 5,000-person company. Its portfolio committee approves an eight-week AI research campaign with a €50,000 model-and-tool cap, 80 reviewer hours, a frozen acceptance test and a prebooked decision date. At the first limit, the orchestration system pauses the work. The independent evaluator then accepts the result, requests one bounded replication or rejects it; only the budget owner can release a second stage, while closure moves the remaining capacity to the named competing project. The amounts are illustrative. The enforceable pause is the point.

The Charter must earn its own continuation too. One falsifiable pilot would compare matched AI campaigns under ordinary stage gates with Charter campaigns across two 90-day portfolio cycles. Before starting, require at least 20% fewer renewals unsupported by independent evidence, no increase in false stops, meaning projects later shown to have met their frozen test, and a lower full cost per independently accepted result. Track reviewer hours per accepted result as well. If the Charter merely moves work onto reviewers or fails to beat the baseline, retire or redesign it.

The weekly portfolio view can remain simple: unsupported renewals, reviewer load, false stops, accepted results and full cost per accepted result. Those measures reveal whether persistence is producing validated learning or only a better case for its own survival.

I think the human responsibility shifts toward the places where accountability cannot be delegated. People still contribute technical ideas, domain knowledge, interpretation and relationships, but they must also decide which question is worth institutional persistence, what evidence can change the decision and when refusal protects more value than another run.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A stopping rule becomes credible only when the system pauses automatically, independent evidence governs renewal and the delivery team cannot fund itself. The Charter should then be tested against ordinary stage gates on unsupported renewals, reviewer burden and full cost per accepted result.</p></div>
