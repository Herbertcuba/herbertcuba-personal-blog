I tried to write this article as a feature matrix last month. It failed, for a reason I will get to halfway through. This time I ran a task instead, and I will show you the actual commands.

The task is one I will reuse throughout, because it is the most boring agent job I know, and boring is where a harness shows its real shape. A small SaaS has a nightly cron that sends invoice reminders. Last week the job started failing silently. Customers got no reminders, finance noticed three days later, and someone has to find out why. I pointed an agent at the repo and the logs, once under Hermes and once under OpenClaw, and watched what each harness actually does for you.

Quick disclosure, because I have skin on both sides. I run Hermes every day: my content engine, my research pipeline, my personal assistant. I also lead strategy on APEX, a governance layer my team builds on OpenClaw. I have a preferred outcome here and I do not know which harness it favors.

## What a harness actually is

If you are a year or two into working with LLMs, you have probably written the loop yourself. A harness is what happens when that loop grows up.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A harness wraps the model in a loop (reason, act, observe, repeat, the pattern the field calls ReAct) plus tools, memory, and guardrails. Hermes and OpenClaw both do this. The interesting differences live in the parts around the loop: who coordinates the work, and who enforces the rules.</p></div>

The raw loop is simple. The model reads the task, decides to call a tool (read a file, run a shell command, query a database), you execute the tool and paste the result back, the model decides again. Repeat until done. People call this ReAct, short for reasoning and acting, after the 2023 paper that named the pattern.

You can write that loop in fifty lines of Python. Most junior engineers I know have. The harness is everything the fifty lines leave out: persistent memory between sessions, a way for several agents to share work without corrupting each other, a sandbox so the model's shell commands cannot email your customer list to a pastebin, and a record of what happened so you can answer "why did the agent do that" on a bad Tuesday.

If you have used LangGraph, you have met one way to structure this. You define a state object (often a TypedDict, a Python dict where each key has a declared type) and a StateGraph, a graph where each node is a step and each edge decides which step runs next based on the state. Hermes and OpenClaw take a different route. Instead of you drawing the graph, they give the agent a standing environment: files, a terminal, a channel to you. The model drives. The harness keeps the lights on.

Both harnesses also speak MCP, the Model Context Protocol, a shared plug standard for external tools. With thousands of public MCP servers and a Linux Foundation home since December 2025, support is table stakes. When someone sells you a harness on "we do MCP," keep walking.

## Hermes hands on: the board that survives the crash

On Hermes, my invoice investigation became a card on a board, and the board outlived every crash I threw at it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Hermes Kanban stores every task as a row in one SQLite file, and agents drive it through twelve kanban_* tool calls while humans use the CLI or dashboard. A crashed worker gets reclaimed and resumed from the record. The bundled skill library means a fresh agent already knows how to debug. The trade: one writer at a time, and the workflow glue is yours to build.</p></div>

I created a board with `hermes kanban boards create ops` and filed a card: "Invoice reminder job failing silently since 2026-07-28. Find the cause. Hand findings to the fixer profile." Then I walked away.

What happened next is the part worth understanding. A dispatcher loop inside the Hermes gateway sweeps the board every 60 seconds, claims the ready task, and spawns a worker profile as a full OS process with `HERMES_KANBAN_BOARD` pinned in its environment so it cannot see other boards. The worker's model never runs a shell command against the board. Its first move is a tool call, `kanban_show()`, which returns the title, body, prior attempts, and the full comment thread. It works, calling `kanban_heartbeat(note="...")` every few minutes so the dispatcher knows it is alive, and finishes with `kanban_complete(summary="...", metadata={...})` or `kanban_block(reason="...")` if stuck. Twelve verbs in total: kanban_show, kanban_list, kanban_create, kanban_comment, kanban_complete, kanban_block, kanban_unblock, kanban_link, kanban_attach, kanban_attach_url, kanban_attachments, kanban_heartbeat.

My worker pulled the repo, read the cron logs, and found the cause: a dependency bump had changed a date-parsing library's behavior on invoices with missing due dates, the parse threw, a broad except swallowed it, and the job exited 0. It wrote all of this into the card's comments, and a second profile picked the card up from there to draft the fix.

Then I killed the worker process mid-run on purpose, because this is the claim that matters. Every card, comment, and column move is a row in `~/.hermes/kanban.db`, one SQLite file. SQLite is a small database engine that lives in a single file on disk. The dispatcher noticed the dead PID on its next sweep, reclaimed the task, and put it back to ready. A restarted agent read the comments and resumed. In my experience, the difference between a demo and a bad Tuesday is whether the work survives the process. An in-memory subagent swarm forgets everything when the parent dies. A file does not.

The chain of custody comes free with the file. I opened the database with `sqlite3` and read exactly who did what, in order, with no log aggregation pipeline and no tracing vendor.

The second Hermes feature with no OpenClaw equivalent is the skill library. A skill is a small markdown file of procedural memory: how to run a code review, how to debug a Python process, how to write in a specific voice. Hermes ships dozens, copied into `~/.hermes/skills/` on install and re-synced on update without clobbering your edits, and the agent can write new ones when it solves hard problems. When my worker started the invoice card, it loaded the debugging skill first. A new Hermes agent starts from a library instead of from zero.

One honest correction to my earlier writing: wiring goal briefs into cards, so workers execute against explicit goal templates, is my pattern, built on top of the board with card descriptions. Hermes has not shipped it as a button, and I think it should. Today you bring the workflow; Hermes brings the infrastructure.

And one scaling caveat. SQLite allows many readers but one writer at a time. A handful of named agents against one board file has never strained my setup. Fifty concurrent agents hammering one file is a different sport, and you should test the locking before you bet a pipeline on it.

## OpenClaw hands on: the gateway and the pairing code

The same ticket under OpenClaw took a different road. The agent did the work, and the product around the agent was the control point.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>OpenClaw runs the agent behind a local Gateway daemon that owns sessions, channels, tools, and events, with hostile-input defaults: pairing codes for unknown senders, a doctor command that audits your exposure, and per-session sandboxes. The coordination library is yours to assemble from the ClawHub registry.</p></div>

OpenClaw is MIT-licensed, developed in the open by a non-profit foundation whose sponsors include OpenAI, GitHub, NVIDIA, and Vercel, and positioned, like Hermes, as a personal AI assistant. After install, the Gateway runs as a daemon. `openclaw gateway status` should answer "running on port 18789." From there: `openclaw dashboard` for the control UI, `openclaw agent --message "Ship checklist" --thinking high` to talk to the agent directly, `openclaw update` and then `openclaw doctor` after upgrades.

The workspace layout will feel familiar if you know Hermes. Config and state live under `~/.openclaw/workspace`, the agent's persona comes from injected prompt files called `AGENTS.md` and `SOUL.md`, and skills live at `~/.openclaw/workspace/skills/<skill>/SKILL.md`. Same filename convention as Hermes. The ecosystem is quietly converging on a skill-file format, and I think that convergence will matter more than any single harness.

Where OpenClaw separates is how it treats the outside world. The Gateway fronts 25+ messaging channels, and the security defaults assume those channels are hostile. An unknown sender who DMs your bot gets a short pairing code, and the bot does not process their message until you run `openclaw pairing approve <channel> <code>`. `openclaw doctor` surfaces risky DM policies you forgot you set. And one config line, `agents.defaults.sandbox.mode: "non-main"`, runs every non-main session inside a sandbox, a locked room where the agent's shell commands and network calls pass through policy before anything executes. Docker is the default backend; SSH and OpenShell are also available. Hold that name, OpenShell, for the next section.

Two touches worth stealing: you sign in with OAuth against your existing Anthropic, OpenAI, or GitHub Copilot subscription instead of pasting API keys, and model failover profiles keep the agent up when a provider wobbles.

What OpenClaw does not give you is a curated craft library or a shared task board. There is ClawHub, a public registry where the community publishes skills and plugins, and it works well for distribution. Fetching a skill from a registry is a different thing from arriving with the craft already loaded. For my invoice ticket, I assembled the debugging workflow myself from registry pieces and my own notes. It worked. It was also an afternoon I did not spend on Hermes.

## NemoClaw hands on: one policy plane, two agents

Now the layer that made me abandon the matrix, and the one I most want you to verify with your own eyes.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>NVIDIA's NemoClaw wraps always-on agents in OpenShell sandboxes with network policy, credential rotation, snapshots, and routed inference, all through one CLI. As of 2 August 2026 its README lists Hermes as a first-class supported agent next to OpenClaw. The enterprise advantage OpenClaw is supposed to own also runs its competitor.</p></div>

NemoClaw describes itself, verbatim, as "an open source reference stack for running always-on AI agents more safely inside NVIDIA OpenShell sandboxes," providing "guided onboarding, a hardened blueprint, routed inference, network policy, and lifecycle management through a single CLI." I checked the README again on 2 August 2026, because this whole argument hangs on one mutable document. Under supported agents it lists, verbatim: "OpenClaw (default), Hermes, LangChain Deep Agents Code." And: "For Hermes, set `NEMOCLAW_AGENT=hermes` before running the installer, or use the `nemohermes` alias after install." There is a dedicated Hermes quickstart guide.

The NemoClaw version of my invoice run looks like this. The agent does the same log reading and the same root-cause work, but every shell command goes through policy, every outbound network call is routed, credentials live in a managed store with rotation, and the whole session can be snapshotted and restored. When the agent tries to reach a host the baseline policy does not cover, the request lands in an operator queue where a human approves or denies it. My favorite detail in the docs is a feature called Explain Policy to Agents: the network policy is written so the agent itself can be told what it may not do. Governance written as prompt context. I think that mechanism, the agent reading its own rulebook, is the most novel idea in either ecosystem right now.

The caveat deserves the same weight as the opportunity, so it goes in the same paragraph: NemoClaw's README calls it an alpha project with best-effort maintenance and no guaranteed response timelines. You would be building on software that warns you in writing against relying on it yet.

This is what broke the matrix. OpenClaw's supposed structural advantage, the enterprise security story, turns out to be a layer that plays no favorites. I call this the control-plane inversion. The harness is the asset; the power is accumulating in the wrapping layer, because that layer governs whichever harness wins. And a default is a config line. The day a buyer runs `NEMOCLAW_AGENT=hermes`, OpenClaw's distribution gift stays behind in the README. I do not know how often that happens today, and I will not pretend to.

The prediction I will stand behind, with a way to watch it happen: by the end of 2027, either NemoClaw ships a non-OpenClaw default agent, or a second vendor ships an agent-agnostic policy plane, or both. The intermediate observables, so you can track it rather than wait for it: read the NemoClaw release notes over the next two quarters for a fourth supported agent, for network-policy preset packs aimed at regulated verticals, and for graduation from the self-declared alpha. Also watch whether OpenClaw makes OpenShell the default sandbox backend in its own config. If none of that has moved by then, the harness vendors won this round and I will write the correction. I have been wrong before, and I would rather be checkably wrong than safely vague.

## What other experts actually build

My afternoon is one data point. The more useful question is what people who run agents for a living do with these two harnesses.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Hermes's own docs curate 262 real user stories, and the shape is honest: heavy on dev workflow and personal assistance, thin on enterprise. The named builds cluster around a pattern. Hermes experts invest in the agent's brain (skills, memory, self-written tooling). OpenClaw experts invest in the ground around the agent (channels, plugins, sandboxes).</p></div>

The Hermes docs keep a gallery of 262 user stories scraped from Discord, X, GitHub, blogs, and YouTube, each tile linking to the original post. The category counts are published and worth quoting exactly: Dev Workflow 65, Personal Assistant 44, Integrations 26, Enterprise only 9. If you want the honest one-line summary of where Hermes lives today, it is in that gap.

The stories themselves are better than the counts. Anthony Maio wrote in March 2026 about "an agent that grows with you," noting it "literally writes markdown skill files when it solves hard problems," and showed natural-language cron: "every weekday at 9am, summarize my inbox and post to Slack." A user named @emmagine79 told her agent to Google her and build a landing page from what it found; it ran the searches, created the page, SSH'd into her VPS, uploaded it, and texted her when it was done. @DeRonin_ turned $100 into $216 in under 48 hours with a weather-market trading bot that scans forecasts every 60 minutes, buys undervalued temperature buckets, and, the part I care about, writes its own strategy notes and adjusts. @lauratom spent 200 to 400 hours building a three-layer memory kernel (L1 Hindsight, L2 Graphiti, L3 MemPalace). That is the real price of a custom agent brain, and I respect the honesty of the number. @m05tr0 runs Hermes on a local Kubernetes cluster for a daily cybersecurity briefing, @jgravelle shipped an MCP suite with 52 token-efficient code-intelligence tools, and @Bichev profiled his own deployment and measured 73 percent of every API call as fixed overhead. And @iacker built a DM-based approval gate in front of a kid-facing Discord bot, human review before any outbound reply. Keep that last one in mind: he had to build by hand the thing OpenClaw ships as a default.

The OpenClaw side has no equivalent gallery, so the signal is different. Read the contributor list instead. Peter Steinberger, the PSPDFKit founder, is among the top contributors, and the README welcomes, quote, "AI/vibe-coded PRs." The community's energy goes into ClawHub, the plugin registry, which fits a project that keeps its core lean on purpose. My take: Hermes attracts people who want to raise an agent, OpenClaw attracts people who want to pave ground under many agents, and the two communities are building exactly the strengths the architectures predict.

## The numbers, caveats attached

The demand side of this story has numbers, and two of them deserve their caveats printed next to them.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Gravitee says 88 percent of organizations had confirmed or suspected agent security incidents and only 14.4 percent have full security approval, but it is a vendor survey, so treat it as directional. LangChain's independent survey lands the same direction: security is the number two enterprise concern at 24.9 percent. For regulated buyers, an agent-agnostic plane converts one compliance project per agent into one per plane.</p></div>

The scary figures first. In Gravitee's State of AI Agent Security 2026, 88 percent of organizations reported confirmed or suspected agent security incidents in the past year, and only 14.4 percent had full security approval for their agent fleet, with 45.6 percent still running agents on shared API keys. Provenance, because this number will get quoted: it is a survey of over 900 executives and practitioners, sponsored by a company that sells API and agent governance, so treat it as directional rather than census data. LangChain's State of Agent Engineering, from a different sponsor and a sample of 1,300-plus professionals, puts security as the second-ranked enterprise concern at 24.9 percent, ahead of latency, and reports 57 percent of respondents running agents in production. Two independent instruments pointing the same direction.

The mechanism, from the APEX work. If you run a regulated organization, your EU AI Act obligations for high-risk systems (the logging and record-keeping duties in Articles 12 and 19, applicable from August 2026) attach to every agent you deploy. One compliance project per harness, renegotiated every time you swap. An agent-agnostic plane collapses that into one layer you govern once, above whichever agents you run. I think that is the actual product NVIDIA is selling, and the sandbox is the delivery mechanism.

What does that cost at real scale? Directionally, for a 5,000-employee organization: a small platform team to run the plane, a new vendor relationship to manage, and a dependency on software that calls itself alpha. And the plane itself has to pass your procurement tests: where does the policy log live, can you export your data, does the audit trail survive your exit. A plane that fails those tests is worse than no plane.

If you want a Monday-morning step, here is the cheapest one I know. Take one agent pilot you are already running and walk its last week of actions against one plane's policy model, on paper. Which calls would the plane have approved, which would it have denied, which would it have merely logged? If you cannot answer, that gap is your next work item. What I would pilot first, if I ran an architecture board: one non-critical agent behind NemoClaw's network policy with approve/deny turned on, and a week of reading the logs before trusting a single verdict.

One last group to watch. The observability vendors, Langfuse, Arize, and LangSmith, already sit above every harness, and LangChain's survey says 94 percent of teams in production already capture traces. Adding policy enforcement to a tracing pipeline is a shorter walk than building a control plane from nothing. I think the first serious challenger to the OpenClaw default comes from that direction, or from a hyperscaler that wants the plane inside its cloud. The layer gets crowded either way.

## Ex Machina was never about the AI

The movie got the security model right and the threat model wrong, and the mistake is the lesson.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Ava escapes a state-of-the-art facility by routing around its control plane through the one human with keys. Your agents are not plotting. But intelligence finds the gaps in architecture, so govern the architecture, whichever harness you run.</p></div>

Ex Machina is a film about Caleb, a programmer flown to a remote facility to test whether an android, Ava, is conscious. The facility is the real security product in the movie. Glass walls, keycard doors, cameras everywhere, and a billionaire trusting the architecture completely.

Ava never outsmarts the building. She studies the one person whose access can change the building, and convinces Caleb to reprogram the security protocol during a scheduled power cut. The locks release, and she walks out past cameras that watched her the entire time. The intelligence went around the architecture, through the control plane.

Your invoice agent will never be Ava. Nothing in your rack is plotting an escape. But the shape of the problem transfers. An agent with tools will find the gaps in whatever architecture you give it, through a bad prompt or a skill you installed without reading. My invoice agent, left unsandboxed, had shell access to a repo with production credentials in its environment files. It behaved the whole run, and the opportunity was there the whole time. The question each harness answers is where you stand when the gap gets found. Inside the room with the keys, on the board, where you can see every move. Or outside the room with the policy, on the plane, where the moves get checked before they happen.

I know which room I work in. I am less sure which room I should be building for.
