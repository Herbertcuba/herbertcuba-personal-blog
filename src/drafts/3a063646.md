An [X post from Om Patel](https://x.com/om_patel5/status/2083401185172541449) included a screenshot of a Claude subscriber's July 2026 usage. The plan cost $200. A tracking tool priced the same usage at $7,470.35 using API list rates, a 37.4x gap.

The subscriber paid $200, while the larger figure is a counterfactual estimate. The screenshot reveals nothing about Anthropic's serving cost. I don't know that cost, and I think pretending otherwise would weaken the useful part of the story.

The image counted 6.16 billion tokens across 36,881 responses. Cache reads represented 48.1 percent of the displayed value, one-hour cache writes 38.0 percent, and output 13.6 percent. Reads retrieve an existing prefix; writes store one. Using the exact dollar line items, reads plus one-hour writes account for 86.2 percent. That total belongs to cache operations, while the read line is the evidence of retrieval.

A second observation supplies the sharper mechanism. Nate B. Jones [reported](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y8vAQ1FgNbM) 3.77 billion tokens across 143 Codex threads in one day, with 3.59 billion classified as reused input. That is 95.2 percent for one person's instrumented day. It is a trace to investigate, rather than an industry rate to repeat.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The $7,470 figure is an API-list-price estimate behind a $200 plan. The screenshot separates cache reads from writes, and a second individual trace shows why repeated context deserves direct measurement.</p></div>

## A cached prefix can still be bad context

Anthropic's [Messages API](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/working-with-messages) is stateless, so the client sends conversation history with each request. [Prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching) makes a matching prefix cheaper: a cache write creates the stored prefix, and a cache read retrieves it at one tenth of the base input price. The discount records efficient delivery. It says nothing about whether the delivered material helped.

Consider an agent fixing a renewal defect. The first request may need a failing test, the billing rule, and the current function. By attempt nine, the envelope can also contain rejected patches, old traces, and explanations of dead routes. I think context debt begins when carried history keeps growing while accepted artifacts stay flat.

*Edge of Tomorrow* offers the right operating model. Cage enters the battle again with the lesson from the previous loop, while the battlefield resets. An agent should carry reviewed evidence forward and leave the sprawling transcript behind.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A cache hit lowers the price of a repeated prefix. Context hygiene asks a separate question: did that prefix earn a place in the next attempt?</p></div>

## Make carry-forward a state transition

For the renewal defect, a passing CI run moves a patch into review. Human approval changes its state to accepted, and that accepted artifact may seed future work. A failed or unreviewed patch may leave a compact failure note; its transcript expires.

An illustrative JSONL trace makes the boundary testable. The numbers below explain the schema and serve only as examples.

```jsonl
{"task_id":"renewal-17","attempt":1,"policy":"expiry-v2","provider":"anthropic","region":"configured-region","rate_card":"pinned-v1","model_version":"pinned-model","tool_versions":["pinned-tools"],"prompt_version":"pinned-prompt","usage":{"input_tokens":1800,"cache_read_input_tokens":25000,"cache_creation_input_tokens":1100,"output_tokens":900},"artifact":{"id":"patch","version":1},"tool_results":[{"id":"ci-1","source":"repo@abc"}],"model_cost_usd":2.40,"ci":"fail","review":{"decision":"unreviewed","minutes":0,"cost_usd":0},"carry_forward":false}
{"task_id":"renewal-17","attempt":2,"policy":"expiry-v2","provider":"anthropic","region":"configured-region","rate_card":"pinned-v1","model_version":"pinned-model","tool_versions":["pinned-tools"],"prompt_version":"pinned-prompt","usage":{"input_tokens":1700,"cache_read_input_tokens":4000,"cache_creation_input_tokens":600,"output_tokens":700},"artifact":{"id":"patch","version":2},"tool_results":[{"id":"ci-2","source":"repo@abc"}],"model_cost_usd":1.10,"ci":"pass","review":{"decision":"accepted","minutes":18,"cost_usd":27},"carry_forward":true}
```

Keep the provider's raw usage fields and the rate-card version. For Anthropic, calculate the retrieved-prefix share as `cache_read_input_tokens / (input_tokens + cache_creation_input_tokens + cache_read_input_tokens)`. When another provider exposes a different signal, label the reuse number as a proxy and preserve its derivation. I think the illegal transition should be simple: `ci:fail` or `review:unreviewed` can never produce `carry_forward:true`.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Record raw usage, pricing metadata, artifact provenance, CI state, and human acceptance. Only reviewed artifacts cross the boundary into the next task.</p></div>

## Test the policy against matched work

Take 30 closed defects from one product domain and stratify them with a frozen rubric based on affected components and acceptance-test breadth. Run every task under the current growing-thread policy and the proposed expiry policy in separate clean environments. Pin the model, tools, prompt, retry cap, and rate card. Give one platform engineer a five-day effort budget across two calendar weeks. Two reviewers each get four hours and judge outputs without seeing the policy.

Predeclare the denominator: `(model spend + reviewer minutes / 60 × loaded hourly rate) / accepted results`. If Finance has no loaded rate, report model money and reviewer minutes separately. A cohort with zero accepted results has no usable unit cost and cannot pass. I think any saving that moves debugging into human review is fake.

Adopt the policy after at least a 10 percent reduction in cost per accepted result, an unchanged accepted count, and no increase in median review time. A broad miss preserves the current default. A failure isolated to one task class goes back for redesign and another matched test.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Replay matched tasks under fixed conditions and count human effort. The policy passes only when accepted-result cost falls without a quality or review penalty.</p></div>

## Put accepted results on the scorecard

The platform owner should bring one page to the monthly FinOps review for each engineering domain: accepted changes, failed attempts, model cost, reviewer time, retained-artifact class, and supplier exposure. Product engineering defines acceptance, Finance supplies the loaded review rate, and Security reviews the material allowed to persist.

Procurement needs the same evidence when terms change. Its checklist should cover cache pricing, usage caps, data retention and residency, export access, reversibility, and notice periods. A quality drop pauses the policy. A supplier change triggers a new rate-card review before comparisons resume.

My take: once every rejected attempt and reviewer minute is attached to the accepted change, a context budget becomes capacity planning. The $7,470 screenshot earns attention, but the trace decides what a team should do. A clean meter reveals whether context discipline removes waste or merely moves it somewhere harder to see.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Govern accepted changes per engineering domain, with model spend, review effort, retained evidence, and supplier terms attached. Stale context becomes visible as delivery capacity paid for and never received.</p></div>
