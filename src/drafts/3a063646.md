On August 1, 2026, founder Om Patel [posted a screenshot on X](https://x.com/om_patel5/status/2083401185172541449), the social platform, from `claude-cost`, a community command-line dashboard for AI usage. It showed 36,881 responses and 6,156,261,087 tokens during one month of Claude Code, the coding agent made by the AI company Anthropic. Patel paid $200 for the Claude Max subscription. The dashboard valued the same July traffic at $7,470.35 using Anthropic's public application programming interface, or API, prices.

The ratio is 37.4 to one, but the two numbers describe different accounts. Patel's cash charge was the flat subscription fee. The larger number was API-list-price exposure, meaning the value assigned by public per-token rates; it was neither his invoice nor a disclosure of Anthropic's cost to serve him. I think that boundary makes the screenshot more useful, because we can study the request pattern without pretending we have Anthropic's internal ledger.

A token is a small unit of text that a model reads or writes. In Patel's traffic, only 33 million of 6.16 billion tokens were output. Most of the list-price estimate came from writing reusable input into a prompt cache and retrieving it again on later requests. That request pattern is the part an engineering team can control.

Coding agents keep bringing instructions, conversation, code, tool results, and prior mistakes into the next model call. A flat subscription can hide the cash effect for a while, but repeated traffic still consumes plan limits and can add latency, review work, or failed attempts. A later pricing change merely makes one part of that load visible in cash. I think the right response is to govern what crosses the request boundary, then measure the cost of work that someone actually accepts.

## Read the meter before judging the price

The screenshot divides the estimate into four useful buckets. Cache retrieval, which reads a stored prompt prefix on a later request, accounted for $3,595.52. Cache creation, which writes that reusable prefix, added $2,841.89 for one-hour entries and $2.83 for five-minute entries. Together, cache operations represented $6,440.24, or 86.2% of the $7,470.35 estimate. Output contributed $1,015.97, while fresh input contributed $14.14.

That breakdown is internally consistent with [Anthropic's published prompt-caching prices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing). Claude Opus 5 and Claude Fable 5 are model families available through Anthropic's API, with listed cache-read rates of $0.50 and $1 per million tokens respectively. Dividing the screenshot's $3,595.52 cache-read estimate by 5,878.394 million cache-read tokens gives about $0.61 per million, between those two price tiers. The check supports mixed use of the two model families; it does not reveal a negotiated enterprise rate or the provider's compute bill.

It also does not prove that 86.2% of the request was waste. A cache hit, meaning a successful retrieval, can contain stable system instructions that every call needs or a stale conversation that should have ended ten turns ago. Cache activity tells us where repeated text is moving. Only task-level evidence can tell us whether that text improved the result.

I don't know what Anthropic paid to serve Patel's month, and nobody outside the company can derive it from a retail price sheet. Anthropic also applies [rolling five-hour windows, weekly limits, and discretionary caps](https://claude.com/pricing) to paid plans, so the $200 fee is not a permanent promise of unlimited traffic. I think calling the entire $7,270 gap a cash subsidy would weaken the argument. The defensible claim is already striking: one flat-price seat carried token traffic valued at 37.4 times its fee under the public API schedule.

Those fields locate repeated input, but they do not prove that the input was removable or reveal Anthropic's serving cost.</p></div>

## Repetition is the workload

Anthropic's [Claude Code cost guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs) explains the loop. Claude Code sends the full conversation with each request, then sends another request after a tool call so the model can read the tool result. The prompt therefore grows as the agent inspects files, runs commands, receives errors, and tries again. Prompt caching makes an identical prefix cheaper to retrieve, while the text still consumes context and still has to reach the model in a useful order.

A second observation shows how extreme that pattern can become. In a [July 29, 2026 video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y8vAQ1FgNbM), AI practitioner Nate B. Jones reported 3.77 billion tokens across 143 threads in one working day inside his Codex coding-agent workspace. His tracker classified 3.59 billion tokens as reused input, its name for request material the model had already seen. The rounded figures produce 95.2%, or almost 96%.

That percentage belongs to one self-instrumented workspace on one day. It is not an industry benchmark. I think it belongs in the argument because its direction agrees with two differently scoped observations.

Patel's dashboard recorded about 186 total tokens for every output token. The engineering team behind [Manus, an AI-agent product](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus), separately reported an average input-to-output ratio of about 100 to one in a 2025 article. Cache operations, tracker-defined reused input, total-to-output ratio, and input-to-output ratio are different measurements, but each points to input movement as a major production load.

More input can also make a model less reliable. *Lost in the Middle*, a study first released in 2023 and published in the journal *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics* in 2024, tested how language models find relevant information in long inputs. [Nelson F. Liu and his co-authors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172) found that performance often fell when the needed material sat in the middle rather than near the beginning or end. The study tested retrieval and question answering, not coding-agent cost, so it does not prove that shortening a coding thread will improve a patch. It does establish the mechanism that matters here: a context window is the limited text space a model can use in one request, and placement and competition affect what the model can recover from it.

The practical question is therefore narrower than “How do we send less?” Teams need to know which prior material still earns a place in the next request. Clearing everything destroys useful evidence; carrying everything lets old evidence compete with the current task. Context hygiene is the policy that decides between those failures.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Almost 96% reused input was a one-day observation from one Codex workspace, not a universal rate. Claude Code's documented resend loop, Patel's 186-to-one total-to-output ratio, and Manus's roughly 100-to-one production ratio are different measures. Together they show why repeated input is a major part of agent work and why excess context can compete with relevant evidence.</p></div>

## Give context an admission rule and an exit

A context item is any discrete piece of material that an application may send to the model: an instruction, file excerpt, tool result, decision, summary, or error. The request boundary is the final point inside the application before those items become the provider payload. That boundary is where context hygiene becomes enforceable, because a rule applied after the request can explain cost but cannot prevent it.

Start with a small state machine, a set of allowed statuses and transitions, rather than a better summarization prompt. A candidate item needs a source, a current task, a reason for admission, an owner, and an expiry condition before it becomes active. It permits `candidate → active`, `candidate → rejected`, and `active → retired`; terminal states cannot return to the envelope, and activation fails when an admission field is empty. In plain operational terms, a rejected or completed item must enter as a new candidate, with a new reason and expiry, before another task can use it.

The policy can fit in a compact typed contract. This version uses a timestamp as the expiry condition so the boundary can evaluate it directly:

```ts
type State = "candidate" | "active" | "rejected" | "retired";

type ContextItem = {
  id: string;
  state: State;
  source: string;
  taskId: string;
  purpose: string;
  owner: string;
  expiresAt: number;
  contentHash: string;
  content: string;
};

const allowed: Record<State, readonly State[]> = {
  candidate: ["active", "rejected"],
  active: ["retired"],
  rejected: [],
  retired: [],
};

function transition(item: ContextItem, next: State, now: number): ContextItem {
  if (allowed[item.state].includes(next) === false) throw new Error("illegal transition");
  const admission = [item.source, item.taskId, item.purpose, item.owner];
  const invalidExpiry = Number.isFinite(item.expiresAt) === false || item.expiresAt <= now;
  if (next === "active" && (admission.some(value => value.length === 0) || invalidExpiry)) {
    throw new Error("missing admission field");
  }
  return { ...item, state: next };
}

function buildEnvelope(items: ContextItem[], now: number) {
  const blocked = items.find(
    item => item.state === "active" ? item.expiresAt <= now : true
  );
  if (blocked) throw new Error("inactive or expired context item");
  return items.map(({ id, contentHash, content }) => ({ id, contentHash, content }));
}
```

Tests should establish all three boundaries: a complete, unexpired candidate can move to active; a retired item cannot move back to active; and an inactive or expired item cannot enter the envelope. A content hash, a compact fingerprint of the bytes, helps detect an exact duplicate; it should never be treated as proof that two differently worded items mean the same thing.

The same instrumentation path should open one request event when the payload is sent and close it when the provider response arrives, before raw usage is normalized away. Anthropic's [Messages API usage object](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/beta/messages/create) separates four fields that should remain distinct:

| Anthropic field | Preserve as |
|---|---|
| `input_tokens` | Ordinary input tokens |
| `cache_creation_input_tokens` | Input tokens written to the prompt cache |
| `cache_read_input_tokens` | Input tokens retrieved from the prompt cache |
| `output_tokens` | Tokens generated by the model |

Attach those raw values to the internal `task_id`, `request_id`, `attempt_id`, parent attempt, context-item identifiers, model identifier, prompt and tool versions, price-schedule version, timestamps, and eventual outcome. Retry lineage means the parent-child chain connecting every attempt made for the same task; without it, failed calls disappear and successful calls inherit too little cost.

A provider adapter, meaning the translation layer between raw provider data and an internal reporting table, can create common names while keeping the source record available. Anthropic's cache-read field is not interchangeable with Jones's tracker-defined reused-input category, and combining them under one convenient label would manufacture a comparison the evidence cannot support. No verified request-level trace is available from these cases, so the next pilot needs to publish one rather than backfill a plausible example. I think this is the minimum viable form of context hygiene: a state transition you can reject, a request you can reconstruct, and an outcome you can join without counting the same usage twice.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Context hygiene becomes enforceable at the final request boundary. Admit only sourced, owned, task-specific, unexpired items; forbid rejected or retired items from silently returning; and preserve Anthropic's raw cache and token fields alongside task, version, context, and retry identifiers.</p></div>

## Change one rule, then try to disprove it

None of these sources provides a controlled team benchmark showing that context hygiene beats a model change on cost per accepted result. A paired evaluation, meaning a comparison of a baseline policy and one changed policy over the same kind of tasks, can produce the missing evidence without turning the entire platform into an experiment. Each task should be a replayable fixture, a saved starting state with permitted tools, required checks, and a fixed acceptance event. Run each fixture under both policies from the same starting state, randomize the order where possible, and prevent artifacts from the first run from leaking into the second.

The test card should fit on one screen and be complete before the first task runs:

| Field | Predeclared decision |
|---|---|
| Task cohort | One recurring task class, with a sampling rule that prevents the team from selecting only easy wins. A cohort is simply the group of tasks being compared. |
| Fixed system | The same model version, system prompt or standing instruction set, tools, repository state, retention settings, and provider region when it is configurable. |
| Treatment | One context rule, such as retiring completed tool results after their verified artifact has been stored. |
| Acceptance event | For a code change, a concrete event such as predefined tests passing and a reviewer approving the change. Define it before seeing the output. |
| Attribution window | From the first request until acceptance or abandonment, with every retry connected through its lineage. |
| Effort envelope | A named owner, caps stated in implementation and reviewer hours, a calendar limit, and a decision date. |
| Guardrails | Quality, security, reviewer time, cache-read share calculated from provider-native input fields, and 95th-percentile latency, meaning the latency that 95% of requests stay below. |
| Decision rule | Scale only if a predeclared minimum improvement in accepted-result cost is met without a guardrail breach; otherwise hold or stop, with an explicit rollback trigger. |

A single task cannot establish whether the policy works. For agents whose outputs vary between runs, repeat enough fixtures to show that variation rather than hiding it in an average. Report the sample count, the spread of accepted-result cost, the 95th-percentile latency, and an uncertainty range around the difference between policies. Publish both a win and a regression trace, meaning a trace where the policy made the outcome worse, when they occur. Sensitive content can be removed while request lineage and field definitions remain intact. That evidence packet should pin the adapter version and the dated provider price schedule so a later field-structure or price change does not rewrite the past.

Ownership has to follow the evidence. Platform Engineering owns the envelope builder, telemetry, and rollback. FinOps, the practice that connects technical usage to financial control, owns price versions and any rule for allocating a flat seat fee. Security, Legal, and the data protection officer, or DPO, approve logged data classes, retention, and regional handling. Procurement owns provider terms and change-notification requirements, while the engineering or product owner defines acceptance. My take is that these gates belong in the pilot, because adding them after scale gives every team a different denominator.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Run a bounded paired evaluation over one representative task class while holding the model and tools fixed. Change one context rule, then define acceptance, attribution, effort, guardrails, ownership, rollback, and uncertainty before observing results. Preserve enough versioned evidence for another engineer to reproduce both improvements and regressions.</p></div>

## Put accepted work in the denominator

An accepted result is an output that reaches a predeclared business or engineering state, rather than one the model merely finishes. For coding work, that could mean a change that passes named tests and receives review approval within the attribution window. The definition will vary by task, but it cannot vary after the result is known.

For direct API use, full cost per accepted result is straightforward in principle:

```text
(provider charges + platform operating cost + reviewer labour cost)
--------------------------------------------------------------------
                         accepted results
```

A subscription needs a predeclared allocation rule for the seat fee, applied consistently to baseline and treatment. If reviewer time is not converted into money by an agreed rate, report hours per accepted result separately rather than adding unlike units. One-time implementation effort should also remain visible or be spread across results by a rule chosen in advance. If no result is accepted, report the run as a failed run with its total attributed cost instead of dividing by zero or dropping it.

API-list-price exposure should remain a separate comparison metric rather than being added to cash. Token volume should also stay split into provider-native fields. This preserves four different questions: what cash left the company, what public API rates would have assigned, what the model read and wrote, and how much human work was required to accept the outcome.

Every failed attempt remains in the numerator. So do retries, abandoned branches, reviewer corrections, and the platform work required to operate the policy. A smaller context can reduce tokens while causing more failures; a larger model can cost more per call while raising acceptance enough to lower the cost of accepted work. The metric forces those trade-offs into the same decision without pretending they are the same kind of evidence.

Context hygiene also has a boundary. One-shot tasks and difficult reasoning problems may need broad source material, while aggressive retirement can remove the clue that explains a failure. In those cases, the test should reject the policy. The goal is to make every carried item earn its place through measured contribution to accepted work, not to minimize context regardless of the result.

The $7,470.35 estimate remains a powerful warning because it shows how far a flat seat can separate price from production behaviour. Yet the number that survives a subscription change, a new model family, or a revised cache rate is the cost of the result your system can defend. Even if a $200 seat is the best commercial deal on the page, engineering control begins only when the team can show what it cost to produce work another human accepted.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Calculate full cost per accepted result from provider or allocated seat cost, platform operating cost, and reviewer labour cost, while reporting API-list-price exposure and provider token fields separately. Keep every retry and failed run in the numerator. A seat price tells you what the vendor charges; cost per accepted result tells you whether the system produces defensible work economically.</p></div>
