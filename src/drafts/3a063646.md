A $200 AI subscription hid a month valued at $7,470.35 under public pay-per-token prices.

In August 2026, [Om Patel](https://x.com/om_patel5/status/2083401185172541449), an engineer who tracks the cost of his own AI coding usage, published a `claude-cost` report on X, the social network formerly called Twitter. The local tracking tool applies public prices from Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude, to recorded usage. It counted 36,881 responses and 6.16 billion tokens during July work in Claude Code, Anthropic's terminal-based coding agent. A token is a small unit of text that a language model reads or writes.

The subscriber paid $200 for [Claude Max](https://claude.com/pricing), Anthropic's flat monthly plan. Anthropic's application programming interface (API) is the metered connection that software uses to call Claude. Holding the recorded workload and model mix fixed, the same activity through that API carried a list-price value of $7,470.35. That is 37.4 times the plan price. The invoice stayed at $200.

The larger figure is a counterfactual, a what-if calculation based on retail API rates. Anthropic's private serving cost could be much lower. I think the shock still reveals a serious engineering problem because flat access removes the price feedback that teaches a team to stop carrying stale files, failed attempts, and old replies into every new model call.

Context engineering is the deliberate choice of what a model receives and what deserves to survive into the next request. Its useful outcome measure is cost per accepted result, the full model and human-review spend required to produce one output that passes a stated technical check and reviewer decision. The subscription story supplies the price signal. A second engineer's measurement supplies a clue about the repeated input behind it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>One July workload would have cost $7,470.35 at public API rates under unchanged behavior, compared with a $200 flat plan. The counterfactual exposes a missing feedback loop: engineers can consume enormous volumes of repeated context without seeing the cost per result they actually accept.</p></div>

## Flat access hides the price signal

Three equally heavy months would carry $22,411.05 of public API value. Claude Max would cost $600 over the same quarter, leaving a $21,811.05 list-price gap. That extrapolation assumes unchanged behavior, even though a metered customer would probably adapt, negotiated rates could be lower, and Anthropic already applies rolling five-hour limits plus weekly caps. I don't know Anthropic's serving cost, and the screenshot cannot reveal it.

The report still shows where the counterfactual value accumulated. Cache reads represented $3,595.52, or 48.1 percent. One-hour cache writes added $2,841.89, or 38.0 percent, while generated output accounted for 13.6 percent. [Prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching) is Anthropic's system for storing an unchanged opening block of a request, called a prompt prefix. A cache write creates that stored prefix; a cache read retrieves a matching prefix on a later call.

Together, the two cache lines account for $6,437.41, or 86.2 percent of the estimate. They describe storage and retrieval operations. Reused input is a separate classification, so combining those cache lines does not produce a reuse ratio. A stored prefix can contain useful instructions, stale history, or both.

The screenshot also supports a limited price check. It lists Claude Fable 5, Claude Opus 4.8, and Claude Opus 5, Anthropic model variants with different public rates. Cache reads cost $1 per million tokens for Fable 5 and $0.50 for the two Opus versions. Dividing $3,595.52 by 5.878 billion cache-read tokens produces an implied blended rate of about $0.61 per million. That sits inside the published range and makes the total plausible, although the missing request records and cache allocation by model prevent a full reproduction.

I think a shadow meter can restore the missing lesson. A shadow meter is an internal report that applies a dated public price sheet to provider usage while the actual subscription bill remains unchanged.

For a board, $7,470 is a warning that most of the workload's counterfactual value sits in cache traffic and repeated input rather than in fresh work. A subscriber's list-price counterfactual is a signal of traffic worth reviewing, not enterprise invoice exposure, and the monthly review should put actual provider spend, acceptance, reviewer time, record retention, and supplier terms beside it. The two contracts also deserve separate treatment: a flat consumer subscription hides usage value from one subscriber, while an enterprise on metered billing sees provider charges directly and faces a different governance problem.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The $21,811 quarterly gap is a bounded list-price extrapolation, while the cache breakdown shows where the traffic value accumulated. A request-level shadow meter can preserve flat access and restore the feedback needed to change behavior.</p></div>

## Why old text keeps returning

Patel's report establishes the hidden price signal. A second observation helps identify the repeated input that can produce it. In a July 2026 video, [Nate B. Jones](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y8vAQ1FgNbM), an AI practitioner who publishes analysis of coding agents, reported a day that his tracker measured in Codex, OpenAI's coding agent.

Jones counted 3.77 billion tokens across 143 threads. His tracker classified 3.59 billion tokens as reused input, its label for text sent again from prior work. That is 95.2 percent for one day in one workspace.

That percentage has a narrow scope. Jones sells a context-management framework, and his public material leaves the raw export, collection code, model versions, and exact rule used to label each record as reused input unpublished. Anyone can reproduce the division of 3.59 by 3.77. Only Jones can currently reproduce the collection. His result is a useful signal with limited reproducibility; an industry benchmark would need measurements from many disclosed workloads.

The repetition has a documented mechanism. Anthropic's [Messages API](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/working-with-messages), the endpoint applications use to send conversations to Claude, is stateless. The service keeps no conversational memory between calls, so the client sends the history again. Context is the complete working bundle sent on one call, including instructions, prior messages, selected files, and tool results. The context window is the maximum bundle a model can receive at once.

Consider an agent fixing a checkout bug. The first request carries operating instructions and the bug report. The second carries those items again, followed by the first answer and an opened file. By turn twenty, one new question arrives behind nineteen earlier exchanges, repository text, and retry history. Every new turn pays to transport the earlier turns.

Caching lowers that delivery price. Anthropic charges a matching cache read at one tenth of base input, but the discount only proves that a prefix matched. It says nothing about whether the prefix helps the current task. A stale cache hit can be cheap per token while consuming scarce context and returning on thousands of later calls.

The same extra text can also weaken the output. [*Lost in the Middle*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172), a 2023 study by researchers at Stanford University and the University of California, Berkeley, tested how language models retrieve evidence from long prompts. Performance often fell when useful information appeared in the middle. The work appeared in the peer-reviewed journal *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics* in 2024. Its finding is precise: position and length can make relevant evidence harder to use even when a model supports a long context window.

Context debt is a useful name for this accumulation: text that once helped, keeps returning on later calls, and no longer improves the result. The name becomes clearer with a concrete picture, and one well-known time-loop film supplies it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>One engineer's tracker labeled 95.2 percent of a single day's traffic as text sent again from prior work. Stateless calls explain the repetition, while cache discounts lower delivery price without deciding whether retained material improves the result.</p></div>

## Meanwhile in sci-fi

Context debt is easier to feel through a film than through a formula, because the mechanism is a reset that only one side experiences.

<div class="scifi"><span class="scifi__label">Meanwhile in sci-fi</span><p class="scifi__film">Edge of Tomorrow (2014)</p><p>In the film, Major William Cage, a soldier trapped in a time loop, relives the same battle and keeps only the lesson from each failed run while the battlefield resets around him. A model call gets the same reset: the model remembers nothing from the previous turn. The engineering system plays Cage's role, and it decides whether the next call receives the extracted lesson, an accepted artifact with its approval reference, or the entire battlefield transcript of every earlier exchange.</p></div>

I think that mapping is the whole argument in miniature. Selective retention after a stateless reset is what separates a learning loop from an expensive one.

## Give carry-forward an admission rule

Short input is a weak target by itself. Context engineering starts with an admission decision: which evidence may enter the next request, which decision will it inform, and which accepted artifact supports its inclusion. Evidence here means a requirement, source excerpt, test result, or other task input with a known origin. An artifact is the patch, answer, or decision record produced by the work. An accepted artifact has passed a named technical check or a reviewer decision.

Return to the checkout bug. Continuous integration (CI) is the automated build-and-test process used before code enters a shared repository. Once a patch passes CI and a reviewer approves it, later work may need the patch, the requirement it satisfies, and the passing test identity. The twenty-turn debate can expire. If a changed requirement supersedes the patch, the system should close the old artifact and link its replacement.

[Claude Code's cost documentation](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs) describes automatic compaction, a shortened summary of earlier conversation created as a thread approaches its context limit. Compaction is a useful safety valve. A retention policy acts earlier, while the system can still separate accepted evidence from a failed path.

The operating hierarchy should stay clear. The policy decides which evidence qualifies for another call, a lifecycle enforces that decision, and a separate evidence package supports any efficiency claim the team publishes. A compact lifecycle can use four enumerated states:

| State | Meaning | Eligible for another request? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `candidate` | Evidence or an artifact awaiting its acceptance decision | No |
| `carried` | An accepted artifact or linked evidence selected under a policy version | Yes |
| `rejected` | An artifact or evidence item that failed its acceptance rule | No |
| `expired` | An item closed by task completion, supersession, or a retention deadline | No |

Each record needs stable identities. A `task_id` groups one job, a `request_trace_id` identifies one model call, and an `artifact_id` identifies the resulting patch, answer, or decision record. An approval event is a log entry created when the CI system records a passing check or a reviewer tool records a human approval. Its unique `approval_event_id` links that decision to the artifact, task, request trace, time, and active policy version.

That link makes the central transition executable. A runtime validator, the software gate that checks each proposed state change, permits `candidate` to move to `carried` only when its `approval_event_id` points to a passing check or reviewer approval for the same artifact and task. A `rejected` item never moves to `carried`: corrected work enters as a new candidate with a new artifact identity. When the task closes, every remaining carried item expires, and when a requirement supersedes an artifact, the system expires the old one and records the replacement `artifact_id`, so supersession becomes a testable event instead of a free-text intention. An `expired` item cannot return to `carried` either; renewed need creates a new candidate, which keeps the record an honest history rather than a revolving door.

The same rule fits in a few lines. A minimal record carries the identifiers, the state, and the policy version:

```json
{
  "artifact_id": "art_91f3",
  "task_id": "task_checkout_118",
  "request_trace_id": "req_7c2d",
  "state": "carried",
  "approval_event_id": "appr_4417",
  "policy_version": "v3"
}
```

A runtime validator then enforces the one transition that matters, the gate from candidate to carried:

```python
def can_carry(record, approval_events, active_policy_version):
    return any(
        e.artifact_id == record.artifact_id
        and e.task_id == record.task_id
        and e.result == "pass"
        and e.policy_version == active_policy_version
        for e in approval_events
    )
```

Applied to the checkout task, the policy changes what one request contains. The trace shrinks because a decision chose what survived, not because a summarizer compressed everything:

```text
turn 3  carries: instructions, bug report, failing test output
turn 21 carries: accepted patch (approval appr_4417), requirement id, test id
        expired: 18 intervening exchanges, 2 failed patches, opened-file dumps
```

Existing tooling already covers important parts of this discipline. Schema validators check that a record carries the required fields before application code accepts it, and durable workflow engines record completed steps so interrupted work can resume from known state. Temporal, a durable workflow engine, is a concrete example: it can guarantee that a transition happened and in what order, but it cannot decide which accepted evidence deserves another model call. The retention policy supplies exactly that choice, plus the later outcome that proves the choice helped.

Internal enforcement and external reproducibility remain separate tracks. Patel's screenshot and Jones's video lack the raw request records required for a full replay, which is why their numbers remain signals. A team publishing its own ratio should preserve a redacted JSONL export, a file with one JSON object per line, plus the model versions and a provider-field mapping that states which response field feeds each metric. It should also keep the dated price sheet, collection window, exclusions, and calculation script. A small replay harness, meaning a script that reruns the calculation from the saved records, should reproduce every public percentage without exposing private request content. This article applies the same standard to its own opening claims: until those raw records exist publicly, the two headline numbers stay signals, and a team should publish its own evidence package rather than forward either figure as a verified measurement.

Stable identities, a logged approval event, and explicit rejection and expiry rules turn carry-forward into a state transition the system can enforce and an outside reader can audit.</p></div>

## Practice while access is flat

The policy becomes useful when daily behavior follows it. Keep one thread around one job. Once the checkout patch is accepted, open a clean thread for the next task and carry the artifact with its approval reference. That boundary prevents unrelated history from becoming permanent input.

Focused retrieval, stable prefixes, and bounded output reduce different parts of the same loop. Searching for the relevant lines reduces irrelevant fresh input. Keeping unchanging instructions and tool definitions at the front preserves prompt-cache matches, while appending current evidence later protects the stable prefix. Asking for only the output the next step needs prevents today's answer from returning tomorrow as oversized input.

The same acceptance rule should guide model choice. Classification and summarization can go to the least expensive model that clears the task's gate, with failed cases escalating to a stronger model. Difficult reasoning or broad research may justify more live context from the start. The record should show which choice produced the accepted artifact.

Measurement closes the loop. Anthropic's response usage record separates fresh input after the final cache breakpoint as `input_tokens`, newly stored input as `cache_creation_input_tokens`, and retrieved input as `cache_read_input_tokens`. A cache breakpoint is the boundary where a stored prompt prefix ends. For Anthropic traffic, the retrieved-input share is:

`cache_read_input_tokens / (input_tokens + cache_creation_input_tokens + cache_read_input_tokens)`

This is a provider-specific traffic measure, rather than a universal reuse metric. Join it to task identity, retry count, retention events, acceptance, and reviewer minutes before judging efficiency. The operating routine matters as much as the dashboard. The platform team can own request telemetry, the monthly cost report, and the policy version itself, so a rule change ships like a code change: versioned, reviewed, and reversible. Domain engineers own acceptance, Finance supplies an hourly reviewer cost that includes pay and employment overhead, and Procurement checks how long request records are kept, the region where suppliers store or process them, and changes to cache or usage-field terms.

The cadence keeps the ownership honest. Teams learn the retention states during onboarding, review exceptions each week, and return any task class that misses its guardrails to a named escalation owner. A task class is done when its acceptance holds and its cost per accepted result stays under its guardrail for a full monthly review period; until then it remains a pilot with a named owner.

For European buyers, record retention is already a legal question as well as an operating one. Article 12 of [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/12/), the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act, which entered into force in August 2024, requires high-risk AI systems to allow the automatic recording of event logs over the lifetime of the system. Trace retention is therefore a statutory duty in a growing share of deployments, not an optional audit extra, and the teams that treat their request records as durable infrastructure now will meet that obligation with less rework.

I think the best use of flat access is to practice as if each request already appeared on the bill. The shadow meter preserves the bargain while showing which retained items improve acceptance and review time.

## Price the result that survives review

An accepted result is useful work that clears a fixed technical gate and human review. For a code patch, CI can supply the technical gate, while reviewer approval confirms that the change solves the intended problem. My take is that the operating metric should be:

`cost per accepted result = (model spend + loaded review cost + material tracing and storage cost) / accepted results`

Loaded review cost combines pay and employment overhead for the time people spend checking outputs. Tracing means recording each model request and its linked outcome; tracing and storage belong in the numerator when they could change the decision. This full measure catches a model saving that returns as extra retries, longer review, audit work, or privacy controls.

A paired replay can test a retention policy before rollout. Take 30 closed defects and create two fresh task copies of each, with no inherited thread state. Run one copy under the current growing-thread process and the other under the candidate carry-forward policy. Keep the model, prompt, tools, retry cap, test environment, and acceptance rubric fixed; the rubric is the scoring rule for a pass. Two reviewers should assess all 60 outputs without seeing which policy produced each one. Thirty is an illustrative size chosen to fit a small review budget, not a universal standard. A team should size its own replay around the smallest cost saving and acceptance change it needs to detect, because a sample too small to measure those differences cannot support the decision.

The review budget is easy to undercount. At four minutes per output, each reviewer spends four hours on 60 outputs. The pair consumes eight reviewer-hours, excluding the time they spend aligning on the scoring rules and resolving disagreements. If `R` is the team's loaded hourly reviewer rate, the base review cost is `8 × R`. Add the alignment and disagreement-resolution time, model spend for 60 runs, replay-environment setup, and material request-record storage to get the full pilot cost.

Set the decision limits before seeing the replay, one for cost, one for quality, and one for review time. Resampling, a statistical method that repeatedly rebuilds a comparison from the observed cases, can produce a 95 percent confidence interval for each difference. The interval is a range of plausible values around the measured change. The cost condition passes when the lower bound of the savings interval clears the team's minimum. The quality condition passes when the lower bound of the acceptance-rate interval stays above its floor. The review condition passes when the upper bound of the reviewer-time increase remains below its limit.

Adoption requires all three conditions to pass. Uncertain intervals keep the pilot on hold, while a failure in acceptance, privacy, trace retention, or regional processing sends the policy back for redesign. This discipline gives a 5,000-person organization a way to compare the control's operating overhead with its verified savings before expanding it.

Broad context sometimes earns its cost. One-shot research and difficult reasoning tasks can need more live evidence, while regulated work can require longer request-record retention and more audit effort. In those cases, aggressive removal may save model spend and raise total operating cost. Cost per accepted result catches that transfer because reviewer coordination, storage, and compliance work remain visible.

I don't know whether the first commercial change will be lower subscription limits or broader metered billing. A provider could also start pricing retained context itself, charging for what a system remembers rather than only for what it computes. I think a team with reproducible usage records and enforced retention events will be ready for any of these. Providers can change the price of a token; the team still controls how much traffic, in requests and retries, one accepted result requires.

A paired replay with predeclared cost, quality, and review-time limits shows whether context discipline creates cheaper accepted results or merely moves expense and risk elsewhere.</p></div>
