An AI subscription can make a machine look cheap by hiding the meter. In August 2026, [Om Patel](https://x.com/om_patel5/status/2083401185172541449), an engineer who tracks the cost of his own AI coding usage, published a report from the `claude-cost` tool on X, the social network formerly called Twitter. It counted 36,881 responses and 6.16 billion tokens during his July work in Claude Code, Anthropic’s terminal-based coding agent. A token is a small unit of text that a language model reads or writes.

Patel paid $200 for Claude Max, Anthropic’s flat monthly plan. His report valued the same traffic at $7,470.35 under the company’s public pay-per-token rates, 37.4 times the subscription price. The larger number was neither an invoice nor a claim about Anthropic’s serving cost. It was a counterfactual: what the recorded traffic would have cost through an application programming interface, or API, the metered connection software uses to call a model.

The easy reaction is to celebrate the bargain or predict the end of the subsidy. I think the more useful reaction is to ask what the flat price has stopped us from learning. When every extra turn feels free, a bloated agent can look productive even while it repeatedly sends old instructions, stale tool output, abandoned guesses, and completed work back to the model.

That makes this an architecture problem before it becomes a procurement problem. Context engineering means deliberately choosing what the model receives now and what is allowed to survive into the next request. The discipline needs an economic test as well: cost per accepted result, meaning the full model and human-review spend required to produce one output that passes a stated technical check and reviewer decision.

<p>The $7,470.35 figure is a list-price estimate behind a $200 plan, not a hidden invoice. Its value is the question it creates: what does flat access conceal about the way an agent carries context, and how much of that traffic helps produce work we actually accept?</p>

## Flat access removes the engineering signal

The report’s composition matters more than the headline. Cache reads accounted for $3,595.52, or 48.1 percent of the estimate. One-hour cache writes accounted for another $2,841.89, or 38.0 percent, while generated output represented 13.6 percent. Prompt caching stores an unchanged opening block of a request, called a prompt prefix. A cache write creates that stored prefix; a cache read retrieves a matching prefix on a later call.

Those two cache lines add up to $6,437.41, or 86.2 percent of the list-price estimate. That does not mean 86.2 percent of the tokens were reused input, because cache creation and cache retrieval are different events. It does show that the commercial value assigned to moving and preserving input dominated the report.

The price sheet offers a useful audit. Patel’s report listed Claude Fable 5 and two Claude Opus model versions. Anthropic priced cache reads at $1 per million tokens for Fable and $0.50 per million for those Opus versions. Dividing the reported $3,595.52 by 5.878 billion cache-read tokens gives about $0.61 per million, which falls inside that published range. The arithmetic does not prove the underlying request log, but it shows that the screenshot is internally consistent with the prices in force.

Three equally heavy months would represent $22,411.05 at public API rates against $600 in subscription fees, a gap of $21,811.05. That extension assumes unchanged behavior, and Anthropic already limits paid plans through rolling five-hour windows and weekly caps. I don’t know Anthropic’s private cost for serving Patel’s workload. Nobody outside the company can derive it from retail API prices, so the 37.4-times figure should remain a traffic signal rather than a claim about provider losses.

A platform team can restore the missing signal with a shadow meter, an internal report that prices recorded usage without treating the result as an invoice. I think every such report should place three numbers beside one another: actual provider spend, public list-price value under a dated price sheet, and cost per accepted result. A subscriber may see only the second as a warning about hidden traffic, while an enterprise on metered billing will compare all three against its contract and workload.

That distinction also sets the governance boundary. Finance owns actual spend, Procurement checks cache pricing and retention terms, and the platform team owns the usage fields and task outcomes. Reviewing the three together prevents a spectacular counterfactual from being mistaken for enterprise exposure while still forcing engineers to explain why the traffic exists.

<p>Cache activity represented 86.2 percent of Patel’s list-price estimate, but cache writes and cache reads cannot be collapsed into a reuse ratio. A defensible shadow meter keeps counterfactual value, actual spend, and accepted outcomes separate.</p>

## Most agent traffic is yesterday’s text

A second measurement points toward the mechanism. In a July 2026 video, [Nate B. Jones](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y8vAQ1FgNbM), an AI practitioner who publishes analysis of coding agents, reported one day measured by his tracker in Codex, OpenAI’s coding agent. It recorded 3.77 billion tokens across 143 threads and classified 3.59 billion as reused input. That is 95.2 percent for one day in one self-instrumented workspace.

The scope is essential. Jones sells a context-management framework, and he has not published the raw export, collection code, model versions, or full labeling rule needed for an independent reproduction. Anyone can verify the division; only Jones can currently verify the collection. The figure is a strong reason to measure other workloads, not permission to call 95.2 percent an industry average.

There is still useful corroboration. In a 2025 production postmortem, [Yichao “Peak” Ji](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus), a builder of the Manus general-purpose AI agent, reported an average input-to-output ratio near 100 to 1. Patel’s screenshot showed 6.16 billion total tokens against 33 million output tokens, roughly 186 consumed for every one generated. Different systems and definitions prevent a clean comparison, but all three observations say that input volume deserves first-class engineering attention.

The architecture explains why. Anthropic’s Messages API is stateless, which means the service keeps no conversation memory between calls. The client sends the working history again with each request. Context is that complete bundle: system instructions, conversation history, tool definitions, retrieved documents, intermediate results, and the current question. The context window is the maximum bundle the model can receive in one call.

Caching makes a repeated prefix cheaper when it matches, sometimes at one tenth of standard input pricing, but it does not remove that material from the model’s logical context. The system can therefore improve its cache bill while still surrounding the current task with stale evidence. Price efficiency and context quality overlap, but they are not the same test.

Quality can fall before the window is full. [“Lost in the Middle,”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172) a 2023 study by researchers at Stanford University and the University of California, Berkeley that appeared in the journal *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics* in 2024, found that language models often performed worse when relevant information sat in the middle of long inputs. Position and length made useful evidence harder to use even for models designed for long context.

Claude Code’s own cost documentation describes automatic compaction, a shortened summary of earlier conversation produced as a thread approaches its limit. That is a sensible safety valve. I think it arrives too late to serve as the full policy, because the agent has already accumulated the material and a general summary must guess which details deserve to survive. An engineering system should make that decision when evidence is tested, while the reason is still visible.

## Decide what deserves another turn

Suppose an agent is migrating a service from static credentials to short-lived identity tokens. During the work it may inspect outdated documentation, try a configuration that fails, discover the correct audience field, produce a passing patch, and receive a security reviewer’s approval. Sending that entire trail into every later request preserves chronology, but chronology is not the same as useful state.

The smaller carry-forward bundle should contain evidence and artifacts. Evidence is a test result, source passage, or reviewer decision that can support a claim. An artifact is a concrete output such as a patch, specification, or migration note. An accepted artifact has passed the check defined for its task, which might include continuous integration (CI), the automated build-and-test process used before code enters a shared repository, plus a named reviewer decision.

That distinction becomes enforceable when every carry-forward item has a state:

| State | Meaning | Allowed next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `candidate` | New evidence or an artifact awaiting its task-specific check | `carried` after approval, or `rejected` after failure |
| `carried` | Verified material eligible for later model requests | `expired` when the task closes or the material is superseded |
| `rejected` | Material that failed its check | Terminal; corrected work gets a new identity |
| `expired` | Material that was once valid but no longer belongs in active context | Terminal |

The policy permits `candidate` to move to `carried` only when its `approval_event_id` points to a passing check or reviewer approval for the same artifact and task. A `rejected` item never moves to `carried`: corrected work enters as a new candidate with a new artifact identity. When the task closes, every remaining carried item expires, and when a requirement supersedes an artifact, the system expires the old one. An `expired` item cannot return to `carried` either.

That policy needs a record, not a prompt convention. At minimum, store `task_id`, `artifact_id`, `state`, `approval_event_id`, `policy_version`, `content_hash`, and `expires_at`. The hash ties the approval to exact content, while the policy version explains which rule admitted it. I think this is the minimum honest history for a system that may later need to explain why a model saw a particular fact.

A small runtime gate can enforce the important transition:

```python
def can_carry(item, event):
    return (
        item["state"] == "candidate"
        and event["outcome"] == "pass"
        and event["task_id"] == item["task_id"]
        and event["artifact_id"] == item["artifact_id"]
        and event["policy_version"] == item["policy_version"]
    )
```

The gate should reject the transition by default and log the refusal. A failing trace in which a rejected artifact attempts to re-enter active context is especially valuable because it proves the forbidden path is enforced, not merely described. The same test should fail when the approval refers to another task, an earlier content hash, or a different policy version.

Durable workflow engines already solve part of this problem. Temporal, for example, is a workflow system that persists an ordered event history so a process can resume after failures. It can prove that a transition happened and in what order. The application still has to decide whether the evidence attached to that transition deserves another model call, which is why context admission belongs in the agent policy rather than in workflow durability alone.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Replace conversational memory with an auditable four-state policy. Only a candidate tied to a passing check for the same task and artifact may become carried context; rejected and expired material remain in the record without returning to the prompt.</p>
</div>

## Build a meter you can defend

Once context has states, usage telemetry can show whether the policy changes traffic. Anthropic reports fresh input after the final cache breakpoint as `input_tokens`, newly stored input as `cache_creation_input_tokens`, and retrieved input as `cache_read_input_tokens`. A cache breakpoint is the boundary after which the provider checks whether the earlier prompt prefix matches stored material.

For that provider, a useful traffic measure is:

```text
retrieved input share = cache_read_input_tokens
                        / (input_tokens
                           + cache_creation_input_tokens
                           + cache_read_input_tokens)
```

Cache writes belong in the denominator because they are input first seen and stored on that request. They stay out of the numerator because the request did not retrieve them from an existing cache entry. If a provider changes the meaning of these fields, the formula stops being comparable, so the collector must save the provider, model, field schema, and price-sheet date with every report.

The evidence package matters as much as the percentage. Publish or retain a redacted JSONL export, a text format that stores one JSON record per line, together with the collection code version, request and task identifiers, model names, cache settings, and the rule used to label accepted results. Add a replay harness, meaning a repeatable script that can run the same closed task under both the baseline and the new context policy. Without that package, a ratio remains a screenshot with good arithmetic.

For an enterprise pilot, keep the setup narrow enough to inspect. Assign one platform owner, use one provider export, select one task class, record the actual engineering hours required to connect outcomes to usage, and review it monthly beside the supplier invoice. This avoids inventing a universal setup estimate and gives Finance a real labor cost for the next decision.

Supplier terms deserve their own check. Procurement should track cache price changes, retention duration, storage region, export availability, and any field-name change that would break the meter. The platform owner should preserve the policy version and admission events alongside the usage record. Article 12 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act that entered into force in August 2024, requires high-risk AI systems to support automatic event logging over their lifetime. A context ledger can support that obligation, although compliance still depends on the system’s legal classification and the rest of its controls.

I think the strongest dashboard row is deliberately boring: task class, accepted results, actual model spend, retrieved input share, review hours, policy version, and cost per accepted result. It gives an engineer enough detail to investigate while keeping the management question visible.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A context metric is credible only when its provider fields, denominator, schema version, and task outcomes are visible. Start with one owned workload, record setup labor, and preserve enough evidence to reproduce the report when pricing or field semantics change.</p>
</div>

## Expect the subsidy to change shape

Flat access is useful because it gives teams room to learn, but it also trains them to ignore marginal traffic. GitHub Copilot, Microsoft-owned GitHub’s coding assistant, offers a warning from the recent past. Technology critic [Ed Zitron](https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-more-you-buy-the-more-you-lose/) reported that its heaviest users consumed more than 25 times their monthly subscription fee before a shift toward token-based billing produced a user revolt. Whatever one thinks of his broader argument, the mechanism is familiar: behavior formed under one unit of price becomes painful when the vendor changes the unit.

The current model price sheets already separate fresh input, cache creation, and cache retrieval. That creates another possible move. Retained context means stored prompt material that remains available for later requests; a provider could price its duration or volume, tighten the amount included in a flat plan, or sell stronger retention controls as an enterprise tier.

My take is that by August 2027 at least one major model provider will make retained-context duration or volume a paid control, or materially tighten an included reuse quota. I expect the first move in enterprise API contracts because those buyers already negotiate storage, regions, audit fields, and usage commitments. If no major provider exposes or restricts such a unit by then, the prediction fails.

I don’t know whether the first commercial change will appear as a cache-retention fee, a lower subscription ceiling, or a new memory product. The practical response is the same: use today’s flat access to learn which context earns its place. A team that waits for a price announcement will discover its dependency only after the contract has named it.

The working routine can stay simple:

1. Start each task with an acceptance test and a compact brief rather than an inherited thread.
2. Retrieve evidence for the current decision and load large tool definitions only when the next action needs them.
3. Keep stable instructions at the front of the request so caching can work, but change the prefix when the policy itself changes.
4. Bound output length and retries, then use the least expensive model that still passes the task’s checks.
5. Close the task by promoting verified artifacts, recording rejected attempts outside active context, and expiring material that no longer applies.

Automatic compaction remains useful underneath this routine. It protects the window when a thread grows unexpectedly. The admission policy does a different job earlier: it prevents unverified or obsolete material from becoming the default memory in the first place.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Pricing will not stay fixed around today’s agent behavior. Teams should treat flat access as a learning period, reduce context dependence now, and watch contracts for retention duration, reuse quotas, and cache terms that turn hidden architecture into an explicit bill.</p>
</div>

## Count only the result that survives

Token cost is useful for debugging, but it is a poor final denominator. A cheap run that produces a patch nobody accepts has created rework. An expensive run that resolves a difficult defect on the first review may be the better system. The comparison becomes honest when both land on the same unit: an accepted result.

Use this calculation:

```text
cost per accepted result =
    (all model charges
     + loaded human-review cost
     + task-attributable tracing and storage cost)
    / accepted results
```

Loaded human-review cost means the reviewer’s time including salary, benefits, and the overhead Finance uses for internal planning. “All model charges” includes failed calls and retries, because they were part of producing the surviving result. Tracing and storage belong in the numerator when they are material and attributable; otherwise teams can make model cost look better by moving work into an uncounted system.

A paired replay can test whether context hygiene improves that number. Choose a set of closed tasks with known outcomes, then run each task twice: once with the current agent and once with the state-based carry-forward policy. Hold the model, base prompt, tools, retry cap, and acceptance rubric constant. A rubric is the written set of criteria reviewers apply. Hide the condition labels from reviewers so their expectations do not decide the result.

Predeclare the adoption rule before seeing the data. The new policy should reduce cost per accepted result by a chosen margin, keep technical acceptance within an agreed quality tolerance, and avoid increasing reviewer time beyond its guardrail. The exact limits belong to the task and organization; presenting one universal threshold would only replace measurement with ceremony.

Report uncertainty too. A confidence interval is a range showing how much an estimate may move with another sample. Resampling, a statistical method that repeatedly rebuilds samples from the observed cases, can produce that range when the task set is modest. If the interval is too wide to support the adoption rule, call the result exploratory and collect more cases. A small test can guide the next experiment without pretending to settle the question.

Ownership closes the loop. The platform team maintains telemetry and policy code, domain engineers define acceptance checks, Finance supplies loaded review cost, and Procurement watches the contract fields that could change the calculation. Review the metric monthly by task class. A workflow leaves pilot status only after its acceptance rate and cost per accepted result stay inside their guardrails for the full review period, with a named owner accountable for drift.

The $200 subscription story matters because it reveals how easily price can disappear from engineering judgment. The 95.2 percent day matters because it shows where a large share of hidden traffic may live. Neither number tells a team what to change until its own accepted outcomes are connected to its own request records.

The meter will eventually become visible, whether it arrives through an invoice, a quota, or a retention term. The teams prepared for that moment will be the ones that already know why every carried token is there and which accepted result paid for it.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Judge context policy with paired tasks, blinded review, predeclared limits, and the full cost of work that survives. Tokens measure traffic; accepted results measure value, and that is the denominator that will still make sense when flat access changes.</p>
