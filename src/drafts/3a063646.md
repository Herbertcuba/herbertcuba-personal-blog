On August 1, 2026, software founder Om Patel [posted a screenshot](https://x.com/om_patel5/status/2083401185172541449) from `claude-cost`, a community analytics tool for Claude Code. Claude Code is the terminal-based coding agent made by Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude. The report covered July: 36,881 responses, 6,156,261,087 tokens, and a total of $7,470.35 when the recorded traffic was valued at Anthropic's public API list prices. Tokens are the small units of text a model reads and writes. Patel's post paired the report with a $200 payment for Claude Max, Anthropic's high-usage subscription tier, without supplying the raw provider records needed to audit the workspace behind it.

The ratio is 37.4 to one. Yet $7,470.35 was not the subscriber's invoice. Anthropic's [Claude Code cost guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs) says its local dollar display applies standard API rates to token counts and can differ from the customer's actual charge; included subscription usage is not billed that way. Public API list prices are retail rates for companies buying metered access, not a disclosure of Anthropic's internal cost. I don't know what Anthropic spent to serve the reported month, and nobody outside the company can derive it from this screenshot.

That boundary makes the observation more useful, not less. The screenshot is a traffic map. Most of the displayed value came from creating and retrieving cached context, while AI commentator Nate B. Jones reported that his tracker classified almost 96% of the tokens across 143 threads in one working day inside his Codex coding-agent workspace as reused input. I think the strategic problem is the missing meter between those two facts: flat pricing hides the amount of old material an agent carries, and a low seat price gives teams little reason to ask whether that material still earns its place.

The engineering response is context hygiene, meaning deliberate control over what enters, remains in, and leaves each model request. It should be judged by cost per accepted result, where an accepted result is work that meets predeclared tests and survives human review within a defined time window. That measure keeps the focus on useful work even when subscription rules, model prices, and cache discounts change.

## The receipt is a traffic map

The line items explain why the total became so large. Prompt caching is a provider feature that stores a matching prefix of a request so later calls can retrieve it more cheaply than processing it as fresh input. Cache creation writes that prefix; cache retrieval reads it on a later request. The posted screenshot listed $3,595.52 for 5.88 billion cache-read tokens, $2,844.72 for two kinds of cache writes, $1,015.97 for output, and $14.14 for ordinary fresh input. The exact cache line items therefore contributed $6,440.24, or 86.2% of the displayed estimate; their individually rounded percentages add to 86.1%.

That is cache activity, not proof of waste. Some repeated input is essential: system instructions, tool definitions, the current plan, and the evidence needed to make the next decision. Caching also lowers its price. Anthropic's current [API price sheet](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing) lists cache retrieval at one tenth of ordinary input price. The screenshot's cache-read total works out to about $0.61 per million tokens, which falls between the listed $0.50 rate for Claude Opus models and $1 rate for Claude Fable 5. That coarse check makes the report internally plausible, although it cannot reconstruct individual requests or replace raw provider records.

Volume can overwhelm a discount. The report recorded roughly 186 total tokens for every output token; that is a total-to-output ratio, not an input-only measure. Jones gives the separate observation behind the 96% headline in a July 2026 [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y8vAQ1FgNbM): his tracker saw 3.77 billion tokens across those 143 Codex threads and classified 3.59 billion as reused input, or 95.2% from the rounded figures. The video promotes Jones's own Ringer context-management framework, and no raw tracker schema accompanies the claim, so this is one self-reported workspace-day rather than an industry benchmark.

A production account offers a third view. In a July 2025 engineering note, the team behind [Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus), a general AI agent, reported an average input-to-output ratio of about 100 to one. The three observations use different categories and cannot be merged into one universal percentage. They do, however, show the same operating shape: agent systems can spend far more traffic reconstructing the situation than expressing the answer.

I think “96%” is useful only when its boundary travels with it. It is a tracker-reported token share from one day, not a spend share, an industry average, or the percentage a team can safely remove. The design question starts after the headline: which repeated tokens are active evidence, and which are merely still present?

## Context needs a lifecycle

A coding agent does not receive “the conversation” as an abstract memory. On each turn, its application assembles a request envelope: the exact package of instructions, conversation history, tool descriptions, retrieved files, and prior tool results sent to the model. Anthropic documents that Claude Code sends the full conversation with each request and sends another request after tool use containing the results. It also uses automatic compaction, which summarizes history as the context window, the model's finite working-text limit, approaches capacity.

Those defaults solve real problems. Full history preserves continuity, prompt caching makes stable prefixes cheaper, and compaction prevents a long session from simply hitting a wall. But none of them decides whether an old failed approach, a superseded requirement, or a giant diagnostic log remains useful to the present task. The application keeps carrying material because its technical state says “available,” even after its reasoning value has expired.

This can hurt quality as well as cost. Nelson F. Liu and colleagues first released [Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172) as a 2023 preprint, and the work was published in the peer-reviewed journal *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics* in 2024. Across long-context retrieval and question-answering tests, models performed worse when relevant information sat in the middle than when it appeared near the beginning or end. The study was not a coding-agent cost experiment, but it demonstrates the mechanism that matters here: fitting information inside a window does not guarantee that the model will use it well.

Anthropic made the same constraint explicit in a 2025 engineering article: [“Context is a critical but finite resource for AI agents.”](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents) My take is that teams should treat context like production state, with an admission rule and an exit condition, rather than like an ever-growing transcript.

A context record needs a source, a stated purpose, the task it belongs to, a freshness rule, and the condition that makes it safe to remove. A failing test can remain active until the fix passes. A design decision can remain active until a later approved decision replaces it. A raw search result should usually expire once its verified finding has been transferred into the work packet.

A context item can move from candidate to active or rejected, and from active to retired; rejected and retired items cannot return to the request unless a new item is created with fresh provenance. Here, provenance simply means where the item came from and why the current task is allowed to trust it. The archive may still keep every log and transcript for audit. Retirement only removes an item from the active request envelope, which prevents deletion anxiety from becoming permanent model input.

This lifecycle also creates ownership. The team can inspect why an item entered, what kept it active, and which event retired it. Without that record, “clean up the context” remains advice. With it, context hygiene becomes a policy that architecture and evaluation can test.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Context should move through explicit candidate, active, rejected, and retired states. The archive can preserve everything, while the live request carries only items with a source, a current purpose, and an exit condition.</p>
</div>

## Cut repetition without breaking the cache

The obvious response to context growth is aggressive trimming, but that can trade one cost for another. Prompt caches depend on a stable matching prefix. If a system repeatedly rearranges its early instructions or tool definitions, later requests may lose the cheap cache retrieval that made repetition tolerable. Manus's engineering team recommends keeping the prompt prefix stable and treating cache hit rate, the share that finds reusable cached material, as a production metric. I think the practical answer is to separate stable operating policy from changing task evidence.

The stable prefix should contain the system rules and tool contracts that truly apply across the session. After that comes a compact work packet: the current objective, acceptance criteria, constraints, approved decisions, and the smallest set of evidence needed for the next action. Large logs, exploratory transcripts, broad search results, and superseded plans stay in an external artifact store. The agent receives a short finding plus a retrievable reference, and it can fetch the full artifact when a later step actually requires it.

Consider a dependency upgrade in a large codebase. The active packet may need the package manifest, the lockfile change, the failing compatibility test, the public API constraint, and the last reviewer decision. It probably does not need every package-manager progress line, the first three abandoned repair attempts, or a repository-wide search result copied in full. Carrying the verified test failure forward preserves evidence; carrying the entire argument that produced it adds volume and competing instructions.

A few habits follow from that separation. Start unrelated work in a clean thread. Search before the agent begins its main loop, then provide the relevant files instead of making it rediscover the repository on every turn. Put limits on tool output and store oversized results outside the request. After a milestone, carry the artifact that governs the next step, such as an accepted plan, patch, or test result, while retiring the debate that led there.

Automatic compaction remains useful as a safety net. It is still a lossy summary created near the model's limit, on the tool's schedule, so it should not be the primary lifecycle policy. A team that waits for compaction has already paid to move the accumulated history through earlier turns and has delegated the choice of what to forget.

There is a failure boundary. A one-shot analysis may genuinely need a broad source set, and hard reasoning tasks that require the strongest available model may lose important connections when an engineer prunes too early. Context hygiene should therefore remove material because its purpose has ended, not because a universal token threshold fired. The policy must also watch cache retrieval, quality, and latency, because the cheapest-looking envelope can still produce more retries.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Keep shared policy stable for cache reuse, assemble a small active work packet for the current task, and archive bulky artifacts outside the request. Retire material when its purpose ends, while measuring whether the change damages cache efficiency, quality, or latency.</p>
</div>

## Put the meter at the request boundary

The request boundary is the last point inside an application before the final payload is sent to a model provider. Metering there captures what the model actually receives, after retrieval, prompt assembly, tool selection, and compaction have done their work. A dashboard above that boundary may know what documents were selected, while provider billing may know token totals; neither view alone explains which context policy produced the request.

Each request record should carry a task ID and request ID, the provider and exact model version, a timestamp, latency, the IDs of active context records, and the provider's raw usage fields. For Anthropic those fields distinguish ordinary input, cache-creation input, cache-read input, and output. Preserve those categories rather than translating them immediately into a generic “reused tokens” field, because providers define caching and billing differently.

The record should be append-only, which means an observed request is never silently rewritten; a correction arrives as a new linked record. That protects attribution when a price sheet changes or a task outcome is revised later. It also lets the team reconstruct the envelope that produced a result without pretending that a public-list estimate was cash.

Keep four meters separate. Actual API charges belong in the cash ledger. A flat subscription needs a predeclared allocation rule, such as cost per active engineer-day, because there may be no marginal per-request charge to observe. Public API-list-price exposure can remain as a separate scenario for capacity planning and repricing risk. Token traffic remains the physical shape of the loop. Mixing those measures produces dramatic numbers that cannot guide an engineering decision.

A useful provider-specific diagnostic is cache-read share: cache-read input divided by ordinary input plus cache-creation input plus cache-read input. It shows how much of the input side arrived through retrieval. It does not show whether the material was relevant or removable, so the request record must also join to the context lifecycle and the eventual task outcome.

I think this join is where observability becomes management. It can reveal that a retired repository scan kept returning, that a tool emitted the same schema on every turn, or that a smaller envelope caused extra failed attempts. The team can then change one policy and see both the traffic effect and the work effect, rather than celebrating a token reduction in isolation.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Meter the final assembled request and preserve the provider's raw usage categories. Keep cash cost, allocated subscription cost, public-list exposure, and token traffic separate, then join each request to its active context records and eventual outcome.</p>
</div>

## Price the result that survives review

A lower token count is an intermediate result. The product of a coding agent is a change that can be accepted, which requires the team to define acceptance before the run begins. For a code task, that may include a fixed test suite, static checks, no unresolved high-severity review finding, and no rollback within a stated attribution window. Different work needs different criteria, but the rule is constant: the outcome cannot be invented after seeing the costs.

The full numerator should include every attempt, successful or not. Add actual model charges or the predeclared subscription allocation, reviewer time valued at salary plus employer costs, relevant platform cost, and the implementation cost of the context policy over the measurement period. Then divide by the number of accepted results. This is cost per accepted result. It prevents a cheap model with many failures, or an elegant context service with heavy review overhead, from looking efficient because one narrow meter improved.

Test the policy on matched work. Select a representative task set, record the baseline, and run the lifecycle policy with the same model version, tool access, repository state, and acceptance checks. Pairing the same task conditions makes the comparison easier to interpret. Reviewers should not know which policy produced a result when that is practical, and the team should choose its required cost reduction and quality guardrails before looking at the outcome.

The decision needs more than an average. Inspect acceptance rate, reviewer correction time, latency, cache creation and retrieval, and the failures that cluster around missing context. If a policy lowers input traffic but pushes relevant evidence out of the envelope, the rejected results will put that cost back into the numerator. If the policy keeps quality stable and reduces retries as well as traffic, the gain is structural.

Model choice comes after this test. A cheaper model may be the right answer for routine work, and a stronger model may earn its price on difficult reasoning. But switching prices while leaving the request loop untouched does not tell the team whether it is buying intelligence or repeatedly shipping history. My take is to use the least expensive model that clears the acceptance bar after the envelope has been made deliberate.

The $200 plan can hide this decision for a while. Anthropic's [subscription terms](https://claude.com/pricing) use rolling five-hour windows, weekly limits, and discretionary caps, so the flat price does not promise unlimited capacity. Other vendors can change quotas, cache discounts, or billing units as well. A team that manages only seat price discovers its architecture when the commercial terms move against it.

I think cost per accepted result is the meter that survives that change. It treats the $7,470 screenshot as a warning about traffic rather than a fantasy invoice, keeps Jones's almost-96% tracker report bounded to 143 Codex threads in one working day, and forces context policy to prove itself in finished work. Cheap access can be rented. Control over what the agent reads, and proof of what its work costs, must be owned.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Define acceptance before measuring, charge every failed attempt and review minute to the task, and compare context policies on matched work with quality guardrails. Subscription prices may move; the durable engineering advantage is knowing the full cost of results that survive review.</p>
