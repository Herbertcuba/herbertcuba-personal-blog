A $200 AI subscription came with a $7,470.35 meter. On August 1, 2026, Om Patel posted a screenshot headed “Claude Code usage — July 2026.” Claude Code is the coding agent from AI company Anthropic. The image reported 6,156,261,087 tokens across 36,881 responses and valued that traffic at public application programming interface rates. An API is the metered interface software uses to call a model, and a token is a small unit of text the model processes.

Divide $7,470.35 by $200 and the ratio is 37.4 to one. Yet the larger figure was not the subscriber’s bill. [Claude Code’s cost guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs) says its local dollar display applies standard API list rates to recorded tokens and can differ from the customer charge. The available evidence is one screenshot: it does not identify the original workspace operator, the exact `claude-cost` build, or the raw provider export, and it says nothing about Anthropic’s internal serving cost.

A second self-report points toward the mechanism. [Nate B. Jones, the author of a video about his own Codex workspace](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y8vAQ1FgNbM), said his tracker recorded 3.77 billion tokens across 143 threads in one working day. Codex is OpenAI’s coding agent. His tracker classified 3.59 billion tokens as reused input, meaning material already supplied was counted again in later work. The rounded figures yield 95.2 percent, which Jones called “almost 96%.”

That percentage belongs to one tracker, one workspace, and one day. Jones did not publish the event schema or raw trace needed to reproduce it. I think the honest shock is still useful: flat access can hide an agent loop dominated by repeated input, so context needs the same lifecycle, telemetry, and outcome test as any other production resource.

## Read the three meters separately

The July report contains three accounting views that must not be collapsed. Cash cost is what the subscriber actually paid. Public-list-price exposure applies a dated retail API schedule to recorded traffic as a comparison. Token traffic describes what crossed the model boundary, regardless of whether the plan charged for each token.

The evidence behind the hook is useful precisely because its limits are visible:

| Observation | Owner and instrument | Sample and raw-data status | What it permits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| July Claude Code report | Posted by Om Patel; original operator and `claude-cost` version unknown | One reported workspace-month; screenshot available, provider export unavailable | The displayed token counts, line items, and public-list estimate, not a subscriber bill or serving cost |
| Codex reuse report | Nate B. Jones; tracker version and schema unpublished | One working day, 143 threads; video statement available, raw events unavailable | A tracker-reported repeated-input signal, not an industry rate |
| Manus production note | The team behind Manus, a general-purpose AI agent; internal instrumentation | Self-reported July 2025 average; raw events unavailable | Input-to-output asymmetry in that system, not cross-provider equivalence |
| Anthropic documentation | Vendor usage schema and dated price schedule | Published category definitions and rates | How to interpret documented fields and price a scenario, not how the screenshot’s requests were composed |

The report’s line items separate two cache operations. Prompt caching is Anthropic’s feature for reusing the processing of an unchanged prefix, such as stable instructions at the start of a request. Cache creation writes or refreshes that prefix in a cache entry, while cache retrieval reads an existing entry again.

Cache retrieval accounted for $3,595.52 of the public-list estimate. The one-hour and five-minute cache-write rows add up to $2,844.72, generated output was $1,015.97, and ordinary fresh input was $14.14. The retrieval and creation rows total $6,440.24, or 86.2 percent of the displayed $7,470.35.

That 86.2 percent is cache activity, not a reuse rate and certainly not a waste rate. A cache write is not retrieved input merely because it creates reusable state. The distinction matters because stable instructions may be both repeated and necessary, while caching can make their processing much cheaper.

The cache-read row supports a narrower plausibility check. Dividing $3,595.52 by 5.878 billion retrieved tokens gives about $0.61 per million. In [Anthropic’s public price table](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing) available in August 2026, cache hits were listed at $0.50 per million tokens for Opus 5 and $1 per million for Fable 5, two model tiers named in the screenshot. The report-level rate falls inside that range, although the screenshot does not split retrieval by model, so this is not a request-level reconstruction.

Jones’s 95.2 percent belongs to another measurement system. His tracker classified counts inside a Codex workspace. A July 2025 note from [the Manus engineering team](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus) reported an average input-to-output ratio around 100 to one. It supports the broader shape of input-heavy agent work, but it cannot verify Jones’s category or the Claude cache split.

I don’t know Anthropic’s cost to serve the month in the screenshot. The public evidence does not disclose it. I think these sources are strongest when they stay in their lanes: one shows the gap between a flat payment and a retail-rate meter, while the others give engineers a reason to inspect the composition of their own requests.

## Why old context keeps returning

For a stateless language-model interface, the client must supply or reference the prior context needed for the next call. Claude Code’s documented behavior is more specific: it sends the current conversation with every request, and each tool use leads to another request carrying that batch of tool results. The complete payload is the request envelope: instructions, conversation, tool definitions, selected source material, tool output, and the newest message.

Repetition is therefore structural. As a session continues, the envelope grows, and each additional tool step creates another occasion to send it. Prompt caching can make an unchanged prefix cheaper to process, but it does not make the envelope smaller or decide whether an old item still deserves to be there.

Repeated does not mean stale. A security policy, schema definition, or accepted constraint may need to appear throughout a task, and removing it can increase errors. The engineering question is narrower: which material can still change the next decision, which material must remain stable for safety or cache efficiency, and which material now belongs only in the durable record?

Consider a coding agent preparing a database migration that prevents duplicate payment records. The first request may need the current schema, the migration policy, and a failing test. By the twentieth request, the envelope may also contain rejected index designs, search output, tool errors, old explanations, and copies of material that no longer affect the decision. The task has not become twenty times more complex, but the request has accumulated its own history.

That history carries a quality cost as well as traffic. [*Lost in the Middle*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172), a study first released in 2023 by Nelson Liu and colleagues and later published in the peer-reviewed journal *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics*, tested multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval in long prompts. Models often performed better when relevant evidence appeared near the beginning or end and worse when it sat in the middle, including models designed to accept long contexts. The study was not a coding-agent cost experiment, but it shows why available capacity does not guarantee usable attention.

I call the carrying cost context debt. It returns on later requests and can bury the evidence that matters now. The practical limit should follow the job rather than a universal token ceiling: a broad investigation may need many sources, while a narrow correction may need one failing test and a small code region.

A cheaper model changes the price of processing each token. Request construction changes how many tokens enter, how often they return, and whether the model can find the decisive evidence. I think that is the control point worth testing first in multi-turn agent work, while one-shot tasks that genuinely require a broad body of evidence may benefit much less from aggressive removal.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A growing request envelope repeatedly carries old conversation, tool results, and source material across a stateless boundary. Some repeated material is necessary, and caching can lower its price. Context policy should retire material that no longer affects the task without removing evidence or destabilizing prefixes that still earn their place.</p></div>

## Give context an exit condition

Context hygiene means deciding what enters a request, why it remains, and when it leaves. An agent can use its transcript as both a durable task record and active working state, but those are different jobs. The durable record should preserve prompts, tool returns, failed attempts, decisions, and source trails. The active envelope should contain the current goal, constraints still in force, admitted evidence, and the newest working artifact.

My take is to make admission an immutable lifecycle event rather than a sentence in a prompt or a mutable dictionary field. This borrows from established software practice: event sourcing preserves changes as append-only events, while distributed tracing links related work with IDs, timestamps, and parent relationships. The agent layer does not need to invent a weaker version of either.

A candidate item should identify its source, content hash, purpose, version, exit condition, and expiry. It should also carry a data classification, meaning its sensitivity label; a trust zone, meaning the approved execution boundary; a processing region; and a retention rule. Activation should carry a lifecycle event that names the authorized principal, the approver, and the reason. The code must validate that event, rather than accept a non-empty approval ID as proof.

It permits `candidate → active`, `candidate → rejected`, and `active → retired`; terminal states cannot return to the envelope, and activation fails when an admission field is empty.

This synthetic Python example makes those rules explicit. State is derived by folding an append-only tuple of frozen events, rather than stored as a field someone can set directly. The envelope builder revalidates the full chain and refuses to serialize an active item after its expiry until a retirement event has been recorded.

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass
from datetime import datetime

ALLOWED = {
    "candidate": {"active", "rejected"},
    "active": {"retired"},
    "retired": set(),
    "rejected": set(),
}
REQUIRED = (
    "item_sha256", "source_ref", "purpose", "version", "exit_condition",
    "classification", "trust_zone", "region", "retention_rule",
)

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class ContextItem:
    item_id: str
    item_sha256: str
    source_ref: str
    purpose: str
    version: str
    exit_condition: str
    expires_at: datetime
    classification: str
    trust_zone: str
    region: str
    retention_rule: str

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class LifecycleEvent:
    event_id: str
    item_id: str
    from_state: str
    to_state: str
    occurred_at: datetime
    authorized_principal: str
    approver: str
    reason: str

def fold_state(item, events, principals, approvers):
    state, seen = "candidate", set()
    for event in (event for event in events if event.item_id == item.item_id):
        if not event.event_id or event.event_id in seen:
            raise ValueError("missing or duplicate lifecycle event")
        seen.add(event.event_id)
        if event.from_state != state or event.to_state not in ALLOWED[state]:
            raise ValueError(f"illegal transition: {state} -> {event.to_state}")
        if event.authorized_principal not in principals or event.approver not in approvers:
            raise ValueError("unauthorized lifecycle event")
        missing = [field for field in REQUIRED if not getattr(item, field)]
        if event.to_state == "active" and (missing or not event.reason):
            raise ValueError(f"cannot activate; missing {missing}")
        state = event.to_state
    return state

def append_event(item, event, events, principals, approvers):
    updated = (*events, event)
    fold_state(item, updated, principals, approvers)
    return updated

def build_envelope(items, events, now, principals, approvers):
    states = {item.item_id: fold_state(item, events, principals, approvers)
              for item in items}
    expired = [item.item_id for item in items
               if states[item.item_id] == "active" and item.expires_at <= now]
    if expired:
        raise ValueError(f"retirement event required for {expired}")
    return tuple(item for item in items if states[item.item_id] == "active")
```

The minimum test set is as important as the happy path. A complete, authorized activation must succeed. Missing admission data, an unauthorized principal, reactivation of a retired item, and duplicate event IDs must fail. Manually splicing an invalid event into the tuple must still fail when the builder folds the chain. An expired active item must block envelope construction until retirement is logged, and a mixed collection must serialize only active items. Preserve those failing traces with the policy version.

There is a real cache trade-off here. The Manus team argues for append-only context and masking unavailable tools because changing or reordering material near the front can break a stable cache prefix. Removing stale material near the front can therefore turn a cheap cache hit into more expensive fresh processing. The practical design is to keep a stable policy and tool prefix, put admitted task evidence in a controlled tail, mask tools that are unavailable in the current state, and measure cache hits, latency, quality, and cost together.

The familiar failure modes now become lifecycle bugs. Forever threads lack an exit condition. Whole files and raw attachments bypass selective admission. Retry loops keep adding errors without retiring failed paths. Bloated tool definitions enter requests even when the current state cannot call them. Automatic compaction remains useful near a model’s context limit, but a late summary is a recovery mechanism, not the application’s state model.

## Meter the request boundary

A context policy becomes enforceable where the application sends a request to the provider. At that boundary, the trace must connect context decisions to requests, retries, artifacts, reviews, and acceptance. Record time, parent links, policy and provider versions, hashes, outcome references, retention rules, and costs. For a cache entry, also record its time to live, the period before the entry expires. Otherwise a dashboard can show a lower token count without proving which prompt changed or whether the resulting work survived review.

The synthetic JSON below is a materialized attribution record assembled from immutable events. It is a test shape, not Anthropic or OpenAI production data. The raw request, lifecycle, artifact, review, and acceptance events remain append-only; the joined view gives an auditor one end-to-end record.

The event log should reject duplicate terminal usage for the same provider request and terminal event ID. Review and acceptance events point back to the artifact, attempt, and final request; they do not rewrite the earlier request event. This gives the seven-day attribution join a stable lineage even when review happens later.

Anthropic’s documented response usage separates ordinary `input_tokens`, `cache_creation_input_tokens`, `cache_read_input_tokens`, and `output_tokens`. A provider-specific cache-read share divides retrieved input by total input, where total input is the sum of all three input fields. Cache creation belongs in the denominator because those tokens were processed to create state, but only retrieval belongs in the numerator.

A provider adapter maps one vendor’s dated response fields into the stable record while preserving the raw payload. Save these two synthetic JSON Lines fixtures as `requests.jsonl`. JSON Lines is a text format with one valid JSON value per line; these particular values are objects.

```json
{"usage":{"input_tokens":100,"cache_creation_input_tokens":50,"cache_read_input_tokens":150,"output_tokens":20}}
{"usage":{"input_tokens":50,"cache_creation_input_tokens":0,"cache_read_input_tokens":50,"output_tokens":10}}
```

Then run this `jq` query. `jq` is a command-line tool for reading and transforming JSON.

```bash
jq -s '
  map(.usage) |
  {
    ordinary:  (map(.input_tokens // 0) | add // 0),
    created:   (map(.cache_creation_input_tokens // 0) | add // 0),
    retrieved: (map(.cache_read_input_tokens // 0) | add // 0)
  } |
  (.ordinary + .created + .retrieved) as $total |
  {
    metric_version: "anthropic-cache-read-share/v1",
    total_input: $total,
    cache_read_share: (if $total == 0 then null else .retrieved / $total end)
  }
' requests.jsonl
```

The fixtures return 400 total input tokens and a cache-read share of 0.5. An empty file returns zero and `null`, so missing data does not masquerade as zero reuse. Test cache-creation time-to-live values separately, and retain unsupported provider categories as `null` rather than inventing a zero.

An OpenAI adapter needs its own dated response fixtures, schema version, category mapping, and terminal-event deduplication test. Jones’s video does not provide that evidence, so his 95.2 percent cannot be poured into this normalized record as if it were an audited provider field. Cross-provider comparison begins only after both adapters pass the same fixture and lineage tests.

The request record must also keep traffic separate from money. Actual cash cost comes from the contracted bill or a subscription allocation chosen before analysis. Public-list-price exposure applies a dated retail schedule to the recorded traffic. A high cache-read share can coexist with low spend, and repeated policy or source material may be necessary rather than wasteful.

The useful alert is a change against a task-class baseline, not a universal red line. Compare migrations with migrations and narrow fixes with narrow fixes, then inspect envelope size, cache hits, latency, retries, review time, quality, and cost together. If a traffic metric stops predicting the full cost of checked and reviewed work, demote the metric instead of defending the dashboard.

Test each provider adapter against dated fixtures, reject duplicate terminal usage, and keep traffic, cash, and public-list exposure separate. A request metric matters only while it predicts the cost and quality of accepted work.</p></div>

## Start with one task, then earn the replay

The meter should pass a smoke test before it produces a dashboard. Take one closed task with a known source revision and checks, then replay it three times under the current context policy and three times under the proposed policy. Six runs cannot establish superiority. They can establish whether requests, context events, retries, artifacts, review time, acceptance, and cost join correctly from start to finish.

Hold the model revision, tools, retry limit, source revision, automated checks, and reviewer guidance constant. Randomize run order and hide the policy label from the reviewer where possible. Define acceptance before the first request: for a closed coding task, a candidate must pass every required automated check and receive an `approved_for_merge` event from a reviewer within seven days.

Before expanding, write a decision charter and put a named person beside each field:

| Charter field | Required commitment |
| --- | --- |
| Accountable sponsor | One person who owns the scale, hold, or stop decision and the decision date |
| Platform owner | Policy implementation, adapters, evidence package, and rollback |
| Finance | Loaded labor rates, contracted model cost, subscription-allocation rule, and payback test |
| Procurement | Price schedule, vendor-schema notice, retention, region, and export rights |
| Security and data protection | Data classification and trust-zone gate; a data protection officer joins when personal or regulated data requires one |
| All-in cap | Provider and platform cash, engineering hours, reviewer hours, and the cost of building the policy |
| Material benefit | The minimum reduction in full cost per accepted result, the cash or capacity mechanism that realizes it, and the maximum payback period |

A lower allocated cost for a fixed subscription is not cash saved. The charter must say whether the policy will reduce metered charges, release reviewer or engineer capacity, avoid an added seat, or merely improve an internal comparison. No realization mechanism means no return claim.

Once the measurement chain works, expand to a bounded exploratory replay. Twelve representative closed tasks, four narrow fixes, four migrations, and four investigations, run three times under both policies, produce 72 isolated runs. Reserve the review capacity and cash cap before the first run. I think twelve pairs are enough to expose broken joins and estimate variance, but not enough to call the method decision-grade without a predeclared precision argument.

Use the smoke-test variance to choose the next cohort so the confidence interval around the agreed material benefit is narrow enough for the decision. A paired-bootstrap confidence interval estimates uncertainty by repeatedly resampling matched task pairs, recalculating cohort cost per accepted result under both policies, and taking the middle 95 percent of the resulting differences. Keep the task as the unit of comparison so three repeated runs do not pretend to be three independent tasks.

Every zero-acceptance branch needs a rule. If the proposed policy has no accepted result while the current policy does, its outcome cost is unbounded and it fails. If the current policy has none while the proposal does, report the recovery but do not invent a finite ratio for the baseline. If both have none, the difference is undefined; mark the task class or bootstrap resample as joint failure, report its frequency, and do not drop it or coerce it to zero. A material share of joint failures makes the adoption decision inconclusive.

Apply uncertainty to quality guardrails too. Require the upper confidence bound for proposed-minus-current cost to clear the predeclared material-benefit threshold. Require the lower bound for the approval-rate difference to remain above the allowed loss, and the upper bound for the review-time ratio to remain below its limit. Any critical correctness or security failure stops adoption. An exhausted budget, missing event, or undefined outcome path stops the comparison and sends the evidence pipeline back for repair.

Preserve the cohort manifest, policy files, model and tool versions, raw provider events, context lifecycle events, adapter fixtures, deduplication tests, artifacts, review events, allocation rule, and analysis code. Include traces that show the effect on cache hits, latency, quality, and cost. Only a reproducible package can turn an exploratory replay into evidence another engineer can challenge.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Prove the complete attribution chain on one task before comparing policies. Predeclare the owner, all-in cap, material benefit, cash or capacity mechanism, uncertainty rules, zero-acceptance branches, quality limits, and rollback. A 72-run replay is exploratory until its cohort size is justified by the precision the decision requires.</p></div>

## Price the result that survives review

Token counts are operational signals, but they are the wrong final denominator. An accepted result is a dated event tied to a specific artifact, not a model saying it finished. In the replay, acceptance means every required automated check passed and a reviewer emitted `approved_for_merge` within seven days of the first request. In live reporting, the approved commit must reach the default branch inside the same attribution window.

A failed test, reviewer rejection, abandoned retry, or late candidate remains a cost without becoming an accepted result. Store the event type, acceptance time, artifact reference, review minutes, and failed-check identifiers with the task outcome. This keeps rejection visible instead of allowing a cheap failed run to improve the average.

The final metric is full cost per accepted result:

**actual model cash or predeclared subscription allocation + loaded review cost + platform expense + policy implementation cost, divided by accepted outputs**

Loaded review cost values reviewer time at a labor rate that includes compensation and employer costs. Platform expense covers the proxy, evaluation runner, storage, and other infrastructure used by the measured workflow. Implementation cost belongs in the payback calculation even when the code later becomes shared infrastructure.

The meters have different financial meanings:

| Meter | What it can support | What it cannot support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contracted model charges | Cash avoided when the bill actually falls | Savings inferred from token share alone |
| Flat-subscription allocation | Consistent internal unit cost or capacity comparison | Cash savings unless a seat, plan, or spend commitment changes |
| Public-list-price exposure | Scenario analysis under a dated retail schedule | Customer charge, provider cost, or booked savings |
| Reviewer and engineering time | Capacity released when measured work falls | Cash savings unless the organization can redeploy or remove the cost |

For a flat seat, freeze the allocation rule before seeing the result. Allocating by task-attributed agent-active minutes can compare policies on the same clock, but it cannot make a $200 subscription cheaper. Adoption still needs a material outcome improvement and a named way to realize cash or capacity within the charter’s payback period.

My take is that context earns its place when it improves acceptance enough to pay for itself. Less context is a loss when it creates more failures, longer reviews, unstable cache prefixes, or slower requests. More context is justified when the checked and reviewed result becomes cheaper despite the extra traffic.

I think ownership belongs with the platform team that controls the request boundary and can join requests to outcomes. Finance validates labor rates and allocation. Procurement, Security, and data-protection staff control the vendor and data gates. The accountable sponsor decides whether evidence clears the material-benefit and payback thresholds; a prettier traffic chart does not.

The $200 plan muted the cash signal, while the $7,470.35 retail-rate meter made hidden traffic visible. Jones’s 95.2 percent gives engineers a reason to inspect their own loops, not a percentage to copy into a forecast. The decisive number begins after the tokens have been spent: what it cost to produce work the organization was willing to accept.

A context policy earns adoption only when it lowers the full cost of accepted work without crossing its quality limits and produces a benefit the organization can actually realize.</p></div>
