[Om Patel shared](https://x.com/om_patel5/status/2083401185172541449) a screenshot of one Claude subscriber's July 2026 usage. The plan cost $200. A tracking tool priced the same usage at $7,470.35 using API list rates, creating a 37.4x gap.

The subscriber still paid $200, and the larger figure is a counterfactual estimate. I don't know Anthropic's serving cost because the screenshot cannot reveal it. I think the useful story begins once that boundary is clear.

The image counted 6.16 billion tokens across 36,881 responses. Cache reads represented 48.1 percent of displayed value, one-hour cache writes 38.0 percent, and output 13.6 percent. A write stores a prefix; a read retrieves one. Their combined 86.2 percent describes cache operations, while the read line identifies retrieval.

A separate trace sharpens the mechanism. Nate B. Jones [reported](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y8vAQ1FgNbM) 3.77 billion tokens across 143 Codex threads in one day, with 3.59 billion classified as reused input. That is 95.2 percent for one instrumented day. The list-price view exposes scale, provider usage fields describe delivery, and reused-input classification estimates repetition. Usefulness still requires an outcome test.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The $7,470 figure is an API-list-price estimate behind a $200 plan. Cache reads, cache writes, and one provider-specific reuse measure reveal different parts of the repeated-context problem.</p></div>

## Cheap reuse can still be expensive context

Anthropic's [Messages API](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/working-with-messages) keeps no conversation state between calls, so the client sends history again. [Prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching) makes a matching prefix cheaper, with cache reads priced at one tenth of base input. That discount measures delivery efficiency. Relevance has to show up in acceptance rate, review time, or accepted-result cost.

I think context debt is retained material that fails that test. If a paired replay can remove old turns without reducing acceptance or increasing review time, keeping them adds cost without improving the result. Long context can also bury useful evidence, as the [Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172) study showed.

*Edge of Tomorrow* gives us the right model. Cage carries the lesson into the next loop while the battlefield resets. An agent should carry evidence that changed the outcome and let the sprawling transcript expire.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A cache hit proves that a prefix was delivered cheaply. Context hygiene asks whether retaining that prefix improves an accepted outcome.</p></div>

## Give carry-forward an admission rule

A practical policy admits two classes. An accepted artifact needs a passing check and human acceptance; it may cross into future tasks until a newer version supersedes it. A reviewed failure note needs cited tool evidence and a reviewer decision of `accepted_as_failure_evidence`; it may enter the next attempt of the same task and expires when that task closes. Raw transcripts and unreviewed patches receive `carry_forward:false`.

In a renewal-defect trace, attempt one ends `ci:fail`, `review:unreviewed`, and `carry_forward:false`. Attempt two ends `ci:pass`, `review:accepted`, `class:accepted_artifact`, and `carry_forward:true`, with expiry set to supersession. Both records retain model cost and reviewer minutes.

I think this boundary should be a state transition, recorded rather than inferred. Keep the provider, region, rate-card version, raw cache read and write fields, model cost, task and artifact versions, check result, reviewer identity and minutes, retention class, and expiry. For Anthropic, calculate retrieved-input share as `cache_read_input_tokens / (input_tokens + cache_creation_input_tokens + cache_read_input_tokens)`. For another provider, preserve the formula and label the result as a proxy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Accepted artifacts can survive until superseded. Reviewed failure evidence gets one same-task retry. Everything else expires, and the trace preserves enough detail to audit cost and admission.</p></div>

## Test the policy on paired work

Take 30 closed defects from one product domain, grouped in advance by complexity and acceptance-test breadth. Replay every task under the growing-thread policy and the admission policy in clean environments, alternating which policy runs first. Pin the model, prompt, tools, retry cap, and rate card. Give two blinded reviewers a fixed four-hour envelope and one frozen acceptance rubric.

The denominator is `(model spend + reviewer hours × fully loaded hourly rate) / accepted results`, with the loaded rate including employment overhead. Report model money and reviewer minutes separately when Finance lacks that rate. A policy with zero accepted results fails. Adopt after at least a 10 percent cost reduction, an unchanged accepted count, stable median review time, and no material regression. A broad miss preserves the default; a miss isolated to one task class goes back for redesign.

I think any saving that sends debugging work to reviewers is accounting fiction. The paired evaluation, rather than either public trace, earns the policy decision.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Compare both policies on every matched task under fixed conditions. Lower accepted-result cost counts only when acceptance, quality, and review effort hold.</p></div>

## Price the result that survives review

My take: token price belongs in the cost model, while cost per accepted result belongs on the operating scorecard. The platform owner should report accepted changes, failed attempts, model spend, reviewer minutes, and retained-artifact class for each engineering domain at the monthly FinOps review.

Supplier exposure means dependency on one provider's cache pricing, retention terms, region support, or usage fields. Reversibility means the team can export its traces and admitted artifacts, then rerun the comparison with another supplier. Once those dependencies sit beside accepted-result cost, Procurement can compare terms without losing the engineering outcome.

A context budget becomes capacity planning when every rejected attempt and reviewer minute is attached to the change that survives. The screenshots give teams a reason to install the meter. The matched evaluation tells them which policy deserves the budget.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Govern model spend, review effort, retained evidence, and supplier dependency per accepted change. The unit worth funding is the result that survives review.</p></div>
