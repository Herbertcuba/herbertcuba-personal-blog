On August 1, 2026, [Om Patel, founder of the research database BigIdeasDB](https://bigideasdb.com/authors/om-patel), [posted a screenshot on the social platform X](https://x.com/om_patel5/status/2083401185172541449) with two arresting figures: $200 paid for Claude Max and $7,470.35 for the same July activity at public API prices. Claude Max is a high-usage subscription from the AI company Anthropic. The post and image do not identify the subscriber, so this is a story about the posted meter, not Patel's personal account.

The image came from [`claude-cost`, an independently published command-line dashboard](https://registry.npmjs.org/claude-cost/latest) for Claude Code, Anthropic's coding agent for reading repositories, editing files, and running commands. It showed 36,881 responses and 6,156,261,087 tokens, the small units of text that model providers count.

The two dollar figures are 37.4 times apart, but they belong to different ledgers. The $200 was reported subscription cash. The $7,470.35 was API-list-price exposure, a what-if estimate using the public pay-per-token rates for Anthropic's application programming interface, or API. Anthropic's internal cost of serving those requests remains undisclosed. I don't know that number, and the screenshot cannot reveal it.

I think the careful version of this story is more useful than the sensational one. A flat seat can make an enormous request loop feel free at the moment an engineer chooses what to send. A separate measurement, in which almost 96% of one working day's tokens were prior input sent again, helps explain how such loops become so large.

The governing idea is complete request evidence: enough information to reconstruct what crossed the provider boundary, what authorized it, how each attempt was priced under separate ledgers, and whether the output was accepted. Platform Engineering owns that technical boundary and its emergency rollback. One named business sponsor owns the bounded evaluation and the final production decision. The unit of value is the full cost per accepted result, with every failed attempt and reviewer minute still attached.

## What $7,470 of usage actually means

The posted dashboard separated the estimate into $14.14 of fresh input, $1,015.97 of model output, $2,844.72 of cache creation, and $3,595.52 of cache retrieval. Prompt caching is a provider feature that stores a stable block of input so later requests can reuse it at a lower rate. Cache creation writes that block; cache retrieval reads it again.

Together, cache creation and retrieval represented $6,440.24, or 86.2% of the public-rate estimate. The dashboard also reported 5.878 billion cache-read tokens. Dividing their $3,595.52 estimated value by that volume gives about $0.61 per million tokens, which falls inside [Anthropic's published cache-retrieval price range](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing) for the models shown. This does not prove the dashboard captured every request perfectly, but it shows that the displayed cache arithmetic is internally coherent.

Repeated does not mean removable. A cached security policy may be essential on every request, while yesterday's test log may be stale. Provider billing fields can prove that material was repeated and show how it was priced; only the application can explain why each piece remained relevant.

The clean operating view keeps three columns separate:

| Economic view | What it answers | What the screenshot supplies |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription cash | What did the post report as paid for access? | $200 for the month |
| API-list-price exposure | What would the recorded activity cost at public per-token rates? | $7,470.35 |
| Provider serving cost | What did Anthropic spend to run it? | No disclosed figure |

This separation matters because [Claude's paid plans use rolling five-hour windows, weekly limits, and discretionary additional caps](https://claude.com/pricing). A flat fee is a commercial access rule, not a promise of unlimited volume at fixed economics. I think public-rate exposure still earns a place in engineering telemetry, provided nobody quietly converts it into cash paid or verified savings. It gives teams a stable rate card for comparing request designs while Finance keeps the actual ledger intact.

A reconstructable event should therefore retain the provider's native usage object, the model response identifier, the applicable price-sheet version, and the predeclared method used to allocate subscription cash. If a model price or usage field changes later, the team can reproduce the old comparison instead of rewriting history with a new rate.

Keep subscription cash, public-rate exposure, and provider serving cost separate, and version the rate card behind every comparison.</p></div>

## Three calculations, three different claims

The first calculation is direct: $7,470.35 divided by $200 equals 37.35175, or 37.4 times when rounded. That is a ratio between reported cash and estimated public-rate value. Calling it a verified provider loss would change the claim.

The second calculation holds the July activity constant for a quarter. Three months would mean $600 in reported subscription cash and $22,411.05 in public-rate exposure, a difference of $21,811.05. That is an implied discount from public API rates under a flat-activity assumption, not cash that appeared in the subscriber's bank account.

The third calculation is a ceiling test. One million subscribers producing the same activity for six months would generate $1.2 billion in subscription cash and $44.8 billion in public-rate exposure, leaving a $43.6 billion gap. Its equal-usage assumption becomes implausible at scale: most users are lighter, Anthropic can tighten limits, public API prices include commercial margin, and list price is not serving cost. It is a boundary case, not a forecast.

How much lower is the true cost? I don't know. Nobody outside Anthropic can derive it from this image or the public rate card. The honest conclusion is narrower: a $200 access price can hide request volume that looks enormous when translated through a different commercial schedule.

That narrow claim is enough. Once the choice to resend context has no visible marginal price, engineers need a meter closer to the request boundary than the monthly seat invoice.

## Context is a consumable resource

[Anthropic's Claude Code cost guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs) describes the mechanism plainly: “Claude Code sends your full conversation with every request, and each time Claude uses tools it sends another request carrying that batch of tool results.” The agent answers, searches, reads, runs a command, and asks the model to reason again over a larger package. History is counted on every trip, not only when it first entered the thread.

Caching lowers the price of an unchanged prefix, the stable opening block of a request. Automatic compaction can summarize a conversation as it approaches the model's context limit, which is the maximum text the model can consider at once. Both are useful defaults. Neither can decide what remains relevant to a team's task, and a summary created near a limit follows the coding tool's schedule rather than the task's acceptance criteria.

There is a serious countercase to aggressive cleanup. Removing or reordering a stable prefix can reduce cache retrieval and trigger new cache writes, so a smaller request may cost more or respond more slowly. Dropping a tool definition while retaining an old call to that tool can leave the model with an event it can no longer interpret. Context hygiene is selective retention, not blanket deletion.

A second public measurement shows how large the repeated share can become. In a video published on July 29, 2026, [AI practitioner Nate B. Jones](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y8vAQ1FgNbM), who created and promotes a Token Saver Skill for context management, reported 3.77 billion tokens across 143 threads in Codex, OpenAI's coding agent, during one working day. His tracker classified 3.59 billion tokens as reused input, meaning prior material sent again. Using the rounded figures, that is 95.2%, or almost 96%.

That percentage belongs to one person, one workspace, and one day. It also differs from Anthropic's cache-read field because a tracker-defined category and a provider billing category measure different things. The wider pattern has independent support: [Manus, a company building general-purpose AI agents, reported in 2025](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus) an average input-to-output ratio of about 100 to 1. The dashboard Patel posted showed roughly 186 total tokens consumed for each output token. These measurements share a direction, not a denominator.

Volume can become a quality problem as well. *[Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)*, a study first released in 2023 and published in the peer-reviewed journal *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics* in 2024, tested retrieval and question answering over long inputs. Computer scientist Nelson F. Liu and his co-authors found weaker performance when relevant information sat in the middle. Its direct evidence concerns those tasks, so a coding team still needs its own evaluation, but the paper removes any reason to treat a larger context as automatically safer.

I think the stale part deserves a useful name: context debt. It charges interest on every later request, in volume and sometimes in attention. The name matters only if it changes the engineering response. Teams need to know which material belongs in a stable prefix, which belongs in the current working set, and which should remain in a durable record outside the request.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Coding agents repeatedly resend conversations and tool results. One instrumented Codex workspace measured 95.2% reused input in a day, while other observations confirm input-heavy loops without creating a universal percentage. Control relevance without ignoring cache-prefix stability, tool dependencies, or the durable history needed to explain a result.</p></div>

## Five ways agents burn the budget

The failure modes are ordinary, which is why they survive. Each one allows material to cross the provider boundary because it was already nearby, not because the current task still needs it.

The fifth failure is easy to underestimate. MCP, the Model Context Protocol, is a standard way to connect tools to language models. [Software developer Geoffrey Huntley's estimate, reported by independent analyst Simon Willison in 2025](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Aug/22/too-many-mcps/), found that one popular GitHub MCP configuration defined 93 tools and consumed about 55,000 tokens before the first user turn. The exact number belongs to that configuration, but the mechanism is general: tool descriptions and schemas are request content too.

The retry problem needs its own record. Retry lineage means every new attempt points back to the same piece of work, so failed, abandoned, and repaired calls remain attached to the eventual outcome. Otherwise a dashboard can celebrate the one request that passed and silently discard the twelve that made it possible.

My take is that operator habits are necessary but insufficient. A clean-thread norm can reduce accidental carryover, and pre-searching can keep a whole file out of the request. Dependable control begins when the application admits every piece of provider-bound content under the same policy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Forever threads, oversized sources, uncurated artifacts, invisible retries, and tools admitted by name all create the same control failure: provider-bound content crosses without a current reason, exact authorization, or complete cost attribution.</p></div>

## Put one builder at the request boundary

The highest-value habits remain simple, but they need a system behind them. The matrix below separates the operator move from the enforcement that makes it survive long sessions, parallel work, and automated retries.

Habits get us only so far. The durable control belongs at the request boundary, the final application-owned step that assembles material before it reaches the provider. A context item is one discrete piece of provider-bound material, such as system policy, a user or assistant message, a source passage, or a tool definition. A request envelope is the complete assembled payload, including model choice, output limit, system text, messages, and tool schemas.

That wider boundary closes a common hole. Governing only the system prompt still lets retired text enter through conversation messages. Allowing a tool name still lets a changed description or input schema cross under old approval. One trusted builder must own every channel, reject the whole request when any item fails, and cap the serialized bytes of the final payload rather than the convenient subset an application happens to label “context.”

To make the boundary auditable, separate admission from traceability. Before activation, an item needs a source, task, owner, reason, future expiry, kind-specific content, and content hash. A content hash is a fixed-length fingerprint of the exact content; the kernel below uses SHA-256, a widely used cryptographic hash function. Approval binds an item identifier to its version and hash. A tool receives an additional binding to its versioned schema hash. The final request receives its own digest so a later comparison can detect any change between the approved envelope and the recorded provider call.

I think a design this specific should carry executable evidence. The proof is easy to scan before reading the code: malformed records cannot activate; changed or unauthorized records cannot enter; altered or oversized payloads cannot be sent. The dependency-free Node.js program below, using the server-side JavaScript runtime, tests those lifecycle, authority, and final-payload boundaries by building the complete provider request from approved identifiers rather than forwarding raw messages or tool objects. Save it as `boundary.mjs` and run `node boundary.mjs`; the fixture prints `14 complete-boundary assertions passed`.

```js
import assert from "node:assert/strict";
import { createHash } from "node:crypto";

const digest = (value) => createHash("sha256")
  .update(typeof value === "string" ? value : JSON.stringify(value), "utf8")
  .digest("hex");

const EDGES = new Set([
  "candidate>active",
  "candidate>rejected",
  "active>retired",
]);

function bodyOf(item) {
  return item.kind === "tool"
    ? { kind: item.kind, tool: item.tool }
    : { kind: item.kind, role: item.role ?? null, text: item.text };
}

function validateForActivation(item, now) {
  for (const field of ["id", "taskId", "source", "owner", "reason", "expiresAt"]) {
    if ((typeof item[field] === "string") === false || item[field].trim().length === 0) {
      throw new Error(`${item.id}: invalid ${field}`);
    }
  }
  const textItem = item.kind === "system" || item.kind === "message";
  const validText = typeof item.text === "string" && item.text.length > 0;
  if (textItem && validText === false) throw new Error(`${item.id}: invalid text`);
  if (item.kind === "message" && ["user", "assistant"].includes(item.role) === false) {
    throw new Error(`${item.id}: invalid role`);
  }
  if (item.kind === "tool") {
    const tool = item.tool;
    const validTool = tool && typeof tool.name === "string" &&
      typeof tool.version === "string" && tool.input_schema &&
      typeof tool.description === "string";
    if (validTool === false) throw new Error(`${item.id}: invalid tool`);
  }
  if (Number.isInteger(item.version) === false || item.version < 1) {
    throw new Error(`${item.id}: invalid version`);
  }
  const expiry = Date.parse(item.expiresAt);
  if (Number.isFinite(expiry) === false || expiry <= now) {
    throw new Error(`${item.id}: invalid expiry`);
  }
  if ((digest(bodyOf(item)) === item.contentHash) === false) {
    throw new Error(`${item.id}: hash mismatch`);
  }
}

function transition(store, id, next, now) {
  const current = store.get(id);
  if (current == null || EDGES.has(`${current.state}>${next}`) === false) {
    throw new Error(`${id}: illegal transition`);
  }
  if (next === "active") validateForActivation(current, now);
  const updated = Object.freeze({ ...current, state: next });
  store.set(id, updated);
  return updated;
}

function buildRequest(store, plan, approval, policy, beforeFinalize = () => {}) {
  const approvalExpiry = Date.parse(approval.expiresAt);
  if ((approval.taskId === policy.taskId) === false ||
      Number.isFinite(approvalExpiry) === false || approvalExpiry <= policy.now) {
    throw new Error("stale approval");
  }
  if (policy.allowedModels.has(plan.model) === false ||
      Number.isInteger(plan.maxTokens) === false ||
      plan.maxTokens > policy.maxOutputTokens) {
    throw new Error("request policy rejected");
  }
  if ((approval.planDigest === digest(plan)) === false) {
    throw new Error("approval plan mismatch");
  }

  const ids = [...plan.systemIds, ...plan.messageIds, ...plan.toolIds];
  if (ids.length > policy.maxItems || (new Set(ids).size === ids.length) === false) {
    throw new Error("item budget rejected");
  }

  const snapshot = ids.map((id) => {
    const item = store.get(id);
    const binding = approval.items[id];
    if (item == null || (item.state === "active") === false) {
      throw new Error(`${id}: not active`);
    }
    validateForActivation(item, policy.now);
    if ((item.taskId === policy.taskId) === false ||
        policy.authorizedIds.has(id) === false) {
      throw new Error(`${id}: unauthorized`);
    }
    if (binding == null || (binding.version === item.version) === false ||
        (binding.contentHash === item.contentHash) === false) {
      throw new Error(`${id}: approval mismatch`);
    }
    return item;
  });

  const byId = new Map(snapshot.map((item) => [item.id, item]));
  const systems = plan.systemIds.map((id) => byId.get(id));
  const messages = plan.messageIds.map((id) => byId.get(id));
  const tools = plan.toolIds.map((id) => byId.get(id));
  if (systems.some((item) => (item.kind === "system") === false) ||
      messages.some((item) => (item.kind === "message") === false) ||
      tools.some((item) => (item.kind === "tool") === false)) {
    throw new Error("channel mismatch");
  }
  for (const item of tools) {
    const binding = approval.tools[item.tool.name];
    const schemaHash = digest(item.tool.input_schema);
    if (binding == null || (binding.itemId === item.id) === false ||
        (binding.version === item.tool.version) === false ||
        (binding.schemaHash === schemaHash) === false) {
      throw new Error(`${item.tool.name}: tool binding mismatch`);
    }
  }

  const payload = Object.freeze({
    model: plan.model,
    max_tokens: plan.maxTokens,
    system: systems.map((item) => ({ type: "text", text: item.text })),
    messages: messages.map((item) => ({ role: item.role, content: item.text })),
    tools: tools.map((item) => item.tool),
  });
  const payloadBytes = Buffer.byteLength(JSON.stringify(payload), "utf8");
  if (payloadBytes > policy.maxPayloadBytes) throw new Error("payload byte budget rejected");

  beforeFinalize();
  for (const item of snapshot) {
    const current = store.get(item.id);
    const unchanged = (current == null) === false && current.state === "active" &&
      current.version === item.version && current.contentHash === item.contentHash;
    if (unchanged === false) {
      throw new Error(`${item.id}: state changed during build`);
    }
  }

  return Object.freeze({
    payload,
    evidence: Object.freeze({
      approvalId: approval.id,
      approvalDigest: digest(approval),
      policyId: policy.id,
      payloadBytes,
      requestDigest: digest(payload),
      itemVersions: ids.map((id) => ({ id, version: byId.get(id).version })),
    }),
  });
}

function verifyRecordedRequest(payload, evidence) {
  if ((digest(payload) === evidence.requestDigest) === false) {
    throw new Error("request digest mismatch");
  }
}

const NOW = Date.parse("2026-08-16T12:00:00Z");
function candidate(id, kind, value, changes = {}) {
  const base = {
    id,
    taskId: "task-17",
    version: 1,
    state: "candidate",
    kind,
    source: "policy-store",
    owner: "platform",
    reason: "required for fixture 17",
    expiresAt: new Date(NOW + 60_000).toISOString(),
    ...(kind === "tool" ? { tool: value } : value),
    ...changes,
  };
  return { ...base, contentHash: changes.contentHash ?? digest(bodyOf(base)) };
}
function activate(store, ...items) {
  for (const item of items) {
    store.set(item.id, item);
    transition(store, item.id, "active", NOW);
  }
}

const system = candidate("sys-1", "system", { text: "Follow policy 17" });
const message = candidate("msg-1", "message", { role: "user", text: "Fix fixture 17" });
const tool = candidate("tool-1", "tool", {
  name: "read_file",
  version: "2.1.0",
  description: "Read one approved fixture",
  input_schema: { type: "object", properties: { path: { type: "string" } }, required: ["path"] },
});
const store = new Map();
activate(store, system, message, tool);
const plan = {
  model: "model-from-manifest",
  maxTokens: 1024,
  systemIds: [system.id],
  messageIds: [message.id],
  toolIds: [tool.id],
};
const approval = {
  id: "approval-7",
  taskId: "task-17",
  expiresAt: new Date(NOW + 30_000).toISOString(),
  planDigest: digest(plan),
  items: Object.fromEntries([system, message, tool].map((item) => [
    item.id, { version: item.version, contentHash: item.contentHash },
  ])),
  tools: {
    read_file: {
      itemId: tool.id,
      version: tool.tool.version,
      schemaHash: digest(tool.tool.input_schema),
    },
  },
};
const policy = {
  id: "policy-3",
  taskId: "task-17",
  now: NOW,
  authorizedIds: new Set([system.id, message.id, tool.id]),
  allowedModels: new Set([plan.model]),
  maxOutputTokens: 1024,
  maxItems: 3,
  maxPayloadBytes: 1024,
};

let passed = 0;
const check = (assertion) => { assertion(); passed += 1; };
const built = buildRequest(store, plan, approval, policy);
check(() => assert.deepEqual(built.payload, {
  model: "model-from-manifest",
  max_tokens: 1024,
  system: [{ type: "text", text: "Follow policy 17" }],
  messages: [{ role: "user", content: "Fix fixture 17" }],
  tools: [tool.tool],
}));
check(() => verifyRecordedRequest(built.payload, built.evidence));

const malformed = candidate("bad-owner", "system", { text: "x" }, { owner: "" });
check(() => assert.throws(() => {
  const local = new Map([[malformed.id, malformed]]);
  transition(local, malformed.id, "active", NOW);
}, /invalid owner/));

check(() => assert.throws(() => {
  const local = new Map(store);
  transition(local, message.id, "retired", NOW);
  buildRequest(local, plan, approval, policy);
}, /not active/));

check(() => assert.throws(() => {
  const local = new Map(store);
  const changed = candidate("tool-1", "tool", {
    ...tool.tool,
    input_schema: { type: "object", properties: { command: { type: "string" } } },
  });
  local.set(changed.id, { ...changed, state: "active" });
  const changedApproval = {
    ...approval,
    items: {
      ...approval.items,
      [changed.id]: { version: changed.version, contentHash: changed.contentHash },
    },
  };
  buildRequest(local, plan, changedApproval, policy);
}, /tool binding mismatch/));

check(() => assert.throws(() => buildRequest(store, plan, {
  ...approval,
  expiresAt: new Date(NOW - 1).toISOString(),
}, policy), /stale approval/));

check(() => {
  const local = new Map(store);
  assert.throws(() => buildRequest(
    local,
    plan,
    approval,
    policy,
    () => transition(local, message.id, "retired", NOW),
  ), /state changed during build/);
});

check(() => assert.throws(() => buildRequest(store, {
  ...plan,
  maxTokens: 512,
}, approval, policy), /approval plan mismatch/));

check(() => assert.throws(() => verifyRecordedRequest(
  { ...built.payload, max_tokens: 2048 },
  built.evidence,
), /request digest mismatch/));

check(() => assert.throws(() => buildRequest(store, plan, approval, {
  ...policy,
  authorizedIds: new Set([system.id, message.id]),
}), /unauthorized/));

check(() => assert.throws(() => {
  const local = new Map(store);
  local.set(message.id, Object.freeze({ ...message, state: "active", taskId: "task-99" }));
  buildRequest(local, plan, approval, policy);
}, /hash mismatch|unauthorized/));

const expired = candidate("msg-1", "message", {
  role: "user",
  text: "Fix fixture 17",
}, { expiresAt: new Date(NOW - 1).toISOString() });
check(() => assert.throws(() => {
  const local = new Map(store);
  local.set(expired.id, { ...expired, state: "active" });
  buildRequest(local, plan, approval, policy);
}, /invalid expiry/));

check(() => assert.throws(() => buildRequest(store, plan, approval, {
  ...policy,
  maxPayloadBytes: 20,
}), /payload byte budget/));

const duplicatePlan = {
  ...plan,
  messageIds: [message.id, message.id],
};
check(() => assert.throws(() => buildRequest(
  store,
  duplicatePlan,
  { ...approval, planDigest: digest(duplicatePlan) },
  { ...policy, maxItems: 4 },
), /item budget/));

assert.equal(passed, 14);
console.log(`${passed} complete-boundary assertions passed`);
```

The run passes fourteen assertions. Malformed or expired records cannot activate, while retired, unauthorized, cross-task, stale, duplicated, or altered material cannot cross. A same-name tool with a changed schema is rejected, as are a state race, an oversized payload, and any final request that no longer matches its recorded digest. The successful case shows that messages come from the same governed store as system text and tools.

Treat this kernel as evidence of a boundary contract, not a production library. Its hashing uses JavaScript's known construction order; a multi-language system should specify a canonical serialization, one agreed byte-for-byte representation, before calculating hashes. The raw `Map`, approval, and policy are trusted fixtures. A production builder must fetch those records from authenticated authoritative stores, make direct state replacement impossible, and expose only validated transitions. The provider adapter also needs an integration test after the vendor payload is assembled, plus transactional or revision-aware reads that fail when a stored version changes during construction.

None of these primitives is new. Runtime schema validation checks values after types have disappeared. Capability-style authorization limits what a task may use. Content-addressed records identify data by a hash, append-only event logs preserve prior events, and durable workflow engines resume work from persisted state. The proposed contribution is their synthesis at the model request boundary, tied to provider-native usage and an accepted-result evaluation. It should not be sold as a new security primitive.

At the provider layer, [Anthropic's Messages API, its programmatic interface for model requests](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/beta/messages/create), returns separate usage fields for ordinary input, cache creation, cache retrieval, and output. Preserve those raw names before adding common labels. Renaming every repeated category “reused input” would erase the provider-specific meaning needed to reconstruct the estimate.

That still leaves an evidence gap. The public material from Patel, Jones, and Manus supplies aggregates, not a real request-level trace or a controlled test of one context rule. No redacted provider event or completed task cohort, a comparable group of evaluation tasks, appears in the task evidence, and inventing one would defeat the argument. Production approval should wait for a version-locked packet containing the executable boundary, one real redacted raw event with its corresponding record in stable local fields, plus one improving and one regressing paired trace.

## Make accepted work the denominator

A smaller request can still be a worse system. It may omit evidence, trigger more retries, increase reviewer effort, destroy a useful cache prefix, or produce a fast answer that fails the task. Token reduction earns its value only when the resulting work survives the same acceptance standard.

Evaluation acceptance is the result inside a bounded comparison: an output meets criteria declared before the run and within a fixed attribution window, the period during which related attempts and repairs are charged to it. Production acceptance is a later governance decision that considers the evaluation packet, operating controls, rollback proof, and cash or capacity case. Mixing those states lets a successful fixture masquerade as approval to run the policy in production.

The attribution rule needs a tail. If work is reopened or a regression is found inside the window, the original result loses evaluation acceptance and the repair attempts return to its numerator. A defect found after the window remains visible as a production incident linked to the original result, but it does not silently rewrite the predeclared experiment. Teams should publish both views.

Test one context change, the treatment, against the current policy, the baseline, on the same tasks. This is a paired comparison because each task runs under both policies. Freeze the repository state, model and API revision, prompt, tools, context policy, output and retry budgets, cache starting condition, and review method. Randomize run order, hide the policy label from the reviewer where practical, and report the within-task difference with uncertainty rather than comparing two unrelated averages.

The following worksheet is deliberately copyable. It turns the protocol into a decision someone can fund, stop, and revisit without inventing an estimate or result. FinOps, the practice of linking technology use to financial accountability, owns the cash method rather than the public-rate headline.

| Field | Commitment required before the pilot | Accountable owner |
|---|---|---|
| Task and due date | Named task class, fixture hashes, sample count, start date, decision date | Evaluation lead |
| Effort cap | Maximum platform hours, reviewer hours, provider cash, and calendar duration | Business sponsor |
| Cash boundary | Subscription allocation, incremental credits or API cash, reviewer labor, and relevant platform cost | FinOps owner |
| Acceptance states | Evaluation criteria, attribution window, production approval criteria, and reopened-work rule | Evaluation lead and sponsor |
| Materiality | Minimum useful improvement and paired uncertainty rule | Business sponsor |
| Guardrails | Quality, security, latency, cache, data, and reviewer-effort limits | Platform and control owners |
| Stop rule | Conditions that end the pilot before the sample is complete | Business sponsor |
| Rollback | Baseline version, restore test, emergency authority, and recovery target | Platform Engineering |
| Realization | Named cash release or capacity gain, measurement owner, and payback horizon | Business sponsor and Finance |

This worksheet defines the evidence behind the production decision, so local governance has to use the same record rather than a parallel compliance story. For a Nordic or wider European deployment, add a control map covering organizational risk classification, human oversight, data classes, processing region, retained evidence, accountable owner, approval point, review cadence, provider-change notice, exit rights, and rollback authority. That map is not a legal conclusion. Privacy, Security, Legal, and the data protection officer responsible for privacy oversight must connect the evidence to the organization's actual obligations and supplier terms.

The realization row matters because fewer tokens under a flat seat do not automatically release cash. Value may come from avoiding incremental credits, retiring seats at renewal, reducing reviewer labor or platform cost, or producing more accepted work with the same team. Choose one mechanism and a payback horizon before the run. If the pilot cannot show that mechanism without moving its assumptions afterward, it has not made a production investment case.

Then calculate two results and keep them separate:

```text
actual cost per accepted result =
  (allocated subscription cash + incremental provider cash
   + reviewer labor + relevant platform cost across every attempt)
  / evaluation-accepted results

API-list-price exposure per accepted result =
  public-rate value of every provider request in the cohort
  / evaluation-accepted results
```

A cohort with zero accepted results fails the evaluation; it cannot manufacture a flattering denominator by dropping failed calls. Reviewer minutes should remain visible beside their monetary conversion, and every retry stays attached through retry lineage. The public-rate equation compares request designs and supplier terms. The actual-cost equation describes the team's economics.

My take is that this metric also improves procurement. A provider export becomes useful when it preserves stable usage fields, historical rate cards, model and API revisions, retention changes, and enough identifiers to join attempts to outcomes. Exit rights and rollback stop being legal decoration because the team can show which evidence would disappear after a supplier change.

There is precedent for the commercial terms changing. [In July 2026, technology critic Ed Zitron reported](https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-more-you-buy-the-more-you-lose/) that heavy users of GitHub Copilot, GitHub's AI coding assistant, consumed more than 25 times their monthly subscription fee before token-based billing was introduced and users revolted. That case does not predict when or whether Anthropic will reprice Claude Max. It shows why a team should own its meter before a discount changes.

I think that is the defensible close to the $200 and $7,470 story. The screenshot makes hidden volume visible, while the 95.2% observation explains one way repeated input can dominate a coding day. Neither number proves waste or savings. Once every provider-bound item needs a current reason and every attempt stays attached to an outcome, the team can test the policy instead of arguing from a dramatic meter.

The cheapest agent is not the one with the lowest seat price or the smallest prompt. It is the one that produces accepted work at the lowest full cost without borrowing against quality, control, or tomorrow's repair queue.
