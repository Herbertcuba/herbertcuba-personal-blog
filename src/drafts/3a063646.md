One month of AI coding carried two prices: $200 paid and $7,470.35 on the shadow meter. In August 2026, [Om Patel](https://x.com/om_patel5/status/2083401185172541449), an engineer who measures his own coding-agent usage, published a July report from the `claude-cost` tool on X, the social network formerly called Twitter. It counted 36,881 responses and 6.16 billion tokens in Claude Code, the terminal-based coding agent from Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude. A token is a small piece of text that a language model reads or writes.

Patel used Claude Max, Anthropic’s $200 flat monthly plan. The larger figure applied Anthropic’s public pay-per-token rates to his recorded traffic. It was a counterfactual, meaning a what-if calculation, rather than an invoice or a view into Anthropic’s private serving cost. The comparison still matters because $7,470.35 is 37.4 times the price Patel saw.

A separate measurement suggests where much of the traffic may sit. In one self-measured day, another engineer’s tracker classified 95.2 percent of the tokens in his coding workspace as reused input. That percentage belongs to one workspace, not the industry. I think its value is the engineering question it creates: which old material deserved another trip through the model, and which material returned only because the software kept the transcript alive?

This is a context problem with an economic consequence. Context is the complete working bundle sent to the model, including instructions, messages, tool descriptions, retrieved documents, intermediate work, and the current request. Context engineering is the deliberate choice of what enters that bundle and what may survive into the next one. My take is that teams should use acceptance as the boundary for memory, then judge the whole system by cost per accepted result: the model and human cost required to produce work that passes a defined technical check.

## The bill is a trace of state

The composition of Patel’s report tells us more than its total. Cache reads accounted for $3,595.52, or 48.1 percent of the list-price estimate. One-hour cache writes added $2,841.89, or 38.0 percent, while generated output represented $1,015.97, or 13.6 percent.

Prompt caching lets a provider store an unchanged opening block of a request, known as a prompt prefix. A cache write creates or refreshes that stored block. A cache read retrieves a matching block during a later call at a lower price than ordinary input. Those are different events, so their combined dollar share cannot be treated as a reused-input percentage.

The narrower claim is strong enough. Cache activity carried $6,437.41 of counterfactual value, 86.2 percent of the report, while newly generated output carried a much smaller share. The public price sheet provides a useful cross-check: Patel’s report included Claude Fable 5 and two Claude Opus versions, whose cache-read prices were $1 and $0.50 per million tokens respectively. Dividing $3,595.52 by 5.878 billion reported cache-read tokens produces a blended price of roughly $0.61 per million, inside that published range.

That arithmetic shows internal consistency, not independent reproduction. We still do not have Patel’s raw request log. I don’t know what Anthropic spent to serve the workload, and the retail API price cannot answer that question. Three identical months would carry $22,411.05 of public API value against $600 in subscription fees, but that extension assumes behavior remains unchanged even though Anthropic applies rolling five-hour limits and weekly caps.

A second measurement points toward the repeated input behind that value. In a July 2026 video, [Nate B. Jones](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y8vAQ1FgNbM), an AI practitioner who publishes analysis of coding agents, said his tracker recorded 3.77 billion tokens across 143 threads during one day in Codex, OpenAI’s coding agent. It labeled 3.59 billion tokens as reused input, which is 95.2 percent for one self-measured day in one workspace.

Jones sells a context-management product, and he has not published the raw export, collection code, model versions, or full labeling rule required to reproduce the measurement independently. Anyone can check the division; only Jones can currently check the collection. I think the figure is a reason to meter more disclosed workloads, not permission to call 95.2 percent an industry average.

The architecture explains why input can grow so large. A model API is the metered connection software uses to call a model. Anthropic’s Messages API is stateless, which means the service keeps no conversation memory between calls; the client has to send the working history again. The context window sets the maximum amount the model can receive in one request, but a larger window does not decide what belongs inside it.

Imagine an agent repairing a failed database migration. The first call may contain the migration file, the error, and a test command. By the twentieth call, the transcript may also include an obsolete runbook, an unsafe rollback attempt, repeated terminal output, several rejected patches, and one passing test. The latest question could be a sentence long while the surrounding envelope carries the whole investigation back to the model.

Caching makes a matching prefix cheaper, yet it does not make stale evidence useful. [“Lost in the Middle,”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172) a 2023 study by researchers affiliated with Stanford University and the University of California, Berkeley that appeared in the journal *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics* in 2024, found that models often performed worse when relevant information appeared in the middle of long inputs. The model can have room for the evidence and still struggle to use it.

Automatic compaction helps when the envelope approaches its limit. Claude Code documents a feature that summarizes earlier conversation at that point. I think of it as a safety valve rather than a retention policy: it arrives after the thread has accumulated, and a general summary must guess which details deserve to survive. The better moment to decide is when an artifact passes or fails its check, while the evidence is still attached to it.

A shadow meter can restore part of the signal that flat access removes. It is an internal report that applies a dated price sheet to observed usage without presenting the result as an invoice. Show that counterfactual beside actual contracted spend, then connect both numbers to task outcomes. Otherwise a team can see traffic without learning whether the traffic did useful work.

## Make acceptance the memory boundary

The database-migration transcript contains two kinds of material that should not be confused. Evidence includes source passages, test results, and reviewer decisions that support a claim. An artifact is the output under judgment, such as a patch, configuration file, or migration plan. An accepted artifact has passed the check chosen for that task, including any required automated tests and human approval.

That same acceptance event can control what the agent remembers. New evidence or an artifact begins in `candidate` state while it awaits a task-specific check. A passing candidate becomes `carried` and may enter later model requests. Failed material becomes `rejected`. Material that was once valid becomes `expired` when its task closes or a newer decision replaces it. Rejected and expired items remain in the audit record without remaining active in the prompt.

The admission rule has to be specific enough for a test. The policy permits `candidate` to move to `carried` only when its `approval_event_id` points to a passing check or reviewer approval for the same artifact and task. The approval event records the check, its outcome, and the exact content reviewed.

The forbidden paths matter because they preserve an honest history. A `rejected` item never moves to `carried`: corrected work enters as a new candidate with a new artifact identity. When the task closes, every remaining carried item expires, and when a requirement supersedes an artifact, the system expires the old one. An `expired` item cannot return to `carried` either.

A minimal record needs a task identifier, artifact identifier, state, approval-event identifier, policy version, content hash, and expiration time. The content hash is a compact digital fingerprint of the exact bytes under review. It prevents a passing decision for one version from admitting a modified version under the same name.

The runtime gate can stay small:

```python
def may_carry(item, approval):
    return (
        item["state"] == "candidate"
        and approval["outcome"] == "pass"
        and approval["task_id"] == item["task_id"]
        and approval["artifact_id"] == item["artifact_id"]
        and approval["content_hash"] == item["content_hash"]
        and approval["policy_version"] == item["policy_version"]
    )
```

The important test is the refusal. A trace should show a rejected artifact attempting to use an earlier passing event and being denied. Tests should also cover approval from another task, an outdated content hash, and a different policy version. A gate that records only successful transitions proves very little about the boundary it claims to enforce.

Now return to the migration. The unsafe rollback remains linked to its failed test. The corrected migration receives a new artifact identity, becomes carried only after its hash matches the passing check, and expires from active context when the incident closes. Its final patch can live in the repository and the durable runbook. The model does not need the entire debate to inherit the verified result.

I think this is the difference between cleaning a prompt and governing state. A reminder to start a fresh thread depends on attention. A runtime policy fails closed, explains every admission, and gives old context a defined route out.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Use the same acceptance event that validates an artifact to decide whether it may survive. Candidate, carried, rejected, and expired states keep the audit trail intact while preventing failed or obsolete material from returning as active guidance.</p>

## Measure the policy against a baseline

A stricter memory policy is only useful if it improves the system. It could shrink context yet trigger more retrieval, more retries, or more human correction. The measurement record therefore has to join context state, provider usage, task outcome, and review effort.

Anthropic exposes three fields that describe input traffic. `input_tokens` covers ordinary input after the last cache breakpoint, the boundary where the provider checks for a matching stored prefix. `cache_creation_input_tokens` covers input stored or refreshed on the request. `cache_read_input_tokens` covers material retrieved from an existing cache entry. For this provider, a retrieved-input share can be calculated as:

```text
retrieved-input share = cache_read_input_tokens
                        / (input_tokens
                           + cache_creation_input_tokens
                           + cache_read_input_tokens)
```

Cache creation belongs in the denominator because it is input first seen and stored on that request. It stays out of the numerator because the request did not retrieve it from an existing entry. If the provider changes what a field means, the series may stop being comparable, so every report should preserve the provider, model, field schema, cache settings, and price-sheet date.

Reproducibility matters more than a dramatic percentage. Keep a redacted JSONL export, a text file containing one complete JSON record per line, along with request and task identifiers, collection-code version, context-policy version, and the rule used to mark a result accepted. Anyone publishing a reuse figure should also state the models, time period, cache configuration, and missing data. A screenshot can start the investigation; an export lets another engineer repeat the calculation.

The practical controls fit around three boundaries. Start each task with a compact brief and an acceptance check instead of inheriting an unrelated thread. Retrieve the file regions and source passages needed for the current decision, and expire that working bundle when the task ends.

For each request, load large tool descriptions only when the next action requires them. Keep stable instructions at the front so prompt caching can match, place changing evidence later, and cap both retries and output length. Verbose output costs once when generated and again when a later request carries it forward.

Only then choose the model. Route simple extraction or classification to the least expensive model that still passes, and escalate when the check demands stronger reasoning. Switching to a cheaper model while preserving a swollen envelope changes the unit rate without correcting the volume.

An enterprise pilot should be narrow enough to inspect. Give one platform engineer one provider export and one task class, record the actual setup hours rather than promising a universal estimate, and review the result monthly beside the supplier invoice. The platform team owns usage fields and policy code, Finance supplies the contracted and loaded labor costs, and Procurement watches cache pricing, retention, storage region, export access, and schema changes.

Then use paired tasks. Replay the same closed work once under the current context policy and once under the new one while holding the model, tools, retry limit, base instructions, and acceptance criteria fixed. Reviewers should not know which condition produced an output. Decide the acceptable cost, quality, and review-time limits before looking at the results, or the team will move the standard toward whichever answer it prefers.

<p>Join provider fields to task outcomes, preserve enough metadata to reproduce every ratio, and test the context policy against matched work. Smaller prompts count as progress only when acceptance and review effort hold.</p>

## Meanwhile in sci-fi

A retention rule is easy to underestimate because stale instructions often look legitimate. They may have been correct when written, signed by the right authority, and faithfully preserved. The failure begins when the world changes but the instruction does not.

## Price the work that survives

The state rule protects what the model sees. The final metric has to protect the organization from optimizing traffic while ignoring value. Token prices are useful for debugging, but a low-cost run that produces an unusable patch still creates review and rework. A more expensive run that passes the same check with less correction may be the better system.

Use cost per accepted result as the denominator:

```text
cost per accepted result =
    (all model charges
     + loaded human-review cost
     + material task-level tracing and storage cost)
    / accepted results
```

Loaded human-review cost means the reviewer’s time with the salary, benefits, and planning overhead used by Finance. Failed calls and retries remain in the numerator because they contributed to the surviving result. Tracing and storage belong there when they are material and can be assigned to the task; otherwise model spend can look better merely because work moved into an uncounted service.

Report the metric by task class. One-shot analysis may need a broad source packet, while a looping repair agent usually benefits from retiring failed attempts quickly. A confidence interval, the range that shows how much an estimate might move with another sample, helps distinguish a stable improvement from noise. When the range is too wide to support the adoption rule, call the result exploratory and collect more cases.

The commercial unit may change before the architecture does. Anthropic’s current price sheet already distinguishes cache creation from cache retrieval, while enterprise buyers negotiate retention, storage region, and usage commitments. Here is a falsifiable bet: before September 2027, at least one major model provider will either sell retained-context duration or volume as a separate enterprise control, or materially tighten the reuse allowance inside a flat plan. Enterprise contracts are the likely first venue because they already contain the terms needed to define that product. If neither move happens, the bet fails.

There is a recent warning about habits formed under flat access. Technology critic [Ed Zitron](https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-more-you-buy-the-more-you-lose/) reported that the heaviest users of GitHub Copilot, Microsoft-owned GitHub’s coding assistant, consumed more than 25 times their monthly subscription fee before a move toward token billing triggered a revolt. That precedent does not predict Anthropic’s decision, but it shows what happens when behavior trained under one price unit meets another.

I don’t know whether the first constraint will look like retention pricing, a lower plan limit, or a revised cache rate. The operating response is the same. Give the metric a named platform owner, let domain engineers define acceptance, have Finance provide the labor basis, and ask Procurement to watch every contract field that can change the calculation.

Patel’s month shows how easily a flat price can remove traffic from engineering judgment. The 95.2 percent day shows one place that hidden traffic can accumulate. Neither measurement tells your team what to cut. Your own request records and accepted outcomes do.

The useful discipline is simple to state and demanding to run: every carried item must explain its admission, and every agent bill must end at work someone was willing to accept.
