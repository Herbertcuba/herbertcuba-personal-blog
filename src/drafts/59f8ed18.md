Imagine you build a small tool for one old house. It gathers service dates, manuals, contractor notes, and the odd recurring problems that never make sense to anyone seeing the house for the first time. After a year, it can prepare the next maintenance list without forcing you to reconstruct the past.

Then the company behind the AI builder shuts down. You have some exported records, but the links between them, the rules you corrected, and the reasons behind those rules are gone. The tool was made for you, yet the useful knowledge inside it never truly belonged to you.

That failure reveals the real question. AI can make software creation cheaper. It becomes personally empowering only when the knowledge added by the person remains theirs.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A personal tool creates agency only when its owner can keep the knowledge that makes it useful.</p></div>

## Start with the day the provider disappears

On August 19, 2026, Nate B. Jones, host of the AI News & Strategy Daily YouTube channel, published [a video about five kinds of software people can make with AI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=joRXo6x7Pgk). His most useful point for this argument is not the list. It is his definition of freedom: “The freedom comes when you own the project files and the data and the decisions.”

Project files are the editable materials and settings needed to run and change a tool. The data is its accumulated history. The decisions are the important rules: what the tool should do, which exceptions matter, and why earlier choices were made.

Now test the old-house tool at the moment its provider disappears. Could another builder reproduce its important job using the materials you kept? Could that happen within limits on time, cost, and outside help that you chose in advance?

An export is not enough if it strips away the relationships that made the records useful. Editable files are not enough if an unexplained outside service still controls the result. Perfect independence is not the standard. Continuity is. You own the useful capability when a provider can be replaced without erasing the learning that made the tool personal.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Ownership becomes visible when a tool’s useful history can survive the loss of its original provider.</p></div>

## One person is enough of a market

Jones describes tools built for a household and for a ferry commuter. They matter because their value comes from fitting details that a mass-market application has little reason to understand. A large product usually has to serve needs shared by many customers. Personal software can begin with the exception that bothers one person every Tuesday.

That changes what counts as worthwhile software. The old-house tool needs no customers, sales plan, or path to growth. If it saves its owner from searching through the same documents and reconsidering the same questions, it has done its job.

This is why personal software is not a smaller software-as-a-service product. A software business normally has to generalize across customers. A personal tool can become more useful as it becomes more particular. Its narrowness is not a failure to reach the market. It is the source of its value.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Software can now justify its existence by fitting one life extremely well, even when nobody plans to sell it.</p></div>

## The valuable part arrives after the first version

At first, the house tool may be little more than a collection of records and reminders. Its distinctive value appears as the owner adds service history, corrects false assumptions, records exceptions, and explains why certain choices worked. The first version may be easy to replace. This accumulated judgment is not.

In [*The 3 Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my 2026 book about technological and economic change, I argue that skills, content, and workflows become stronger assets when their useful effects are no longer tied to every new hour of work. I also describe a person’s perspective and private context as intangible capital: non-physical resources that are valuable because they improve how future work gets done.

Personal software gives that idea a practical container. A choice made once can improve the next maintenance plan. A correction made today can prevent the same mistake next winter. This reusable ability to apply stored judgment is what I mean here by productive intelligence.

When a person controls that history and it keeps saving effort, it begins to behave like productive capital: something they own that continues helping to produce a useful result. I mean this in a practical sense, not as an automatic legal or accounting classification. It does not need revenue or customers. It needs repeated usefulness and an owner who can keep it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Personal software becomes an asset when a person’s past judgment can keep improving future results.</p></div>

## Ownership brings chores

Calling something an asset does not make maintenance disappear. Later in the same video, Jones warns that AI-assisted creation can introduce unexplained paid services, expose sensitive data, or accidentally make an application public. Those risks matter more as a tool learns private details.

An important personal tool therefore needs a recoverable copy, reasonable protection, a short explanation of how it works, and someone willing to look after it. A trivial one-off tool can remain disposable. Trying to preserve every experiment would turn freedom into a pile of chores.

The point is to notice when a convenience has become something you rely on, then care for it accordingly. You may still rent computing power or use a large AI model. You do not need to control every layer. You do need a credible way to keep your own layer working when one supplier changes or disappears.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Durable ownership requires care, while low-stakes personal software can still be allowed to disappear.</p></div>

## The democratic question is who keeps the learning

Citizen-scale ownership means practical control at the level of a person or household. Most individuals will never own a data center or build a large AI model. The personal layer may be the first realistic part of the AI system they can own: their records, rules, corrections, and history of choices.

In the epilogue to *The 3 Crucibles*, I argue that distributed ownership of productive AI assets can counter the concentration of intelligence. Personal software shows how that could work in ordinary life.

If a provider supplies the AI and also traps everything the tool learns about its user, leaving means surrendering the value created through years of use. The provider’s technology improves the tool, but the person’s own experience also strengthens the provider’s hold.

If the personal history can travel, the relationship changes. The provider still supplies important infrastructure, but the person can switch services without buying back their own experience or starting again. Every correction strengthens something the person keeps.

Allowing millions of people to generate apps is therefore not the same as allowing millions of people to own useful intelligence. For a builder, the practical question is where the value goes each time a user improves a tool. If it stays only with the platform, abundance can deepen dependence. If the records, rules, and working materials can travel with the person, personal software becomes productive capital even when nobody else ever uses it.

That is the democratic promise worth pursuing: software for one life, owned at the scale of that life.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI becomes more democratic when people can carry the useful history of their personal tools from one provider to another.</p></div>
