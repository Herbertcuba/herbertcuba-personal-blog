When people say AI is democratizing software, they usually mean that more people can make apps. That is true. It is also the least interesting part of the change.

A tool generated in an afternoon can still make its owner dependent on the company that generated it. The interface may feel personal while the data sits in a closed system, the important rules remain hidden, and leaving means starting again. Creation became easier. Ownership did not.

The more important change is that software no longer needs a market to be worth making. Imagine a parent coordinating school timetables, travel times, pickup arrangements, and the weekly exceptions no standard calendar understands. A small tool could learn those details and prepare a workable plan every Sunday. No company needs to sell it. Its value comes from remembering how this one life works.

If the parent can keep and move that accumulated knowledge, the tool becomes more than a convenience. It becomes an asset.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Making software is easier. The real gain begins when a person can keep the knowledge that makes their software useful.</p></div>

## A useful tool no longer needs a market

On August 19, 2026, Nate B. Jones, host of the AI News & Strategy Daily YouTube channel, published [a video about five forms of software people can now make](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=joRXo6x7Pgk). They range from local tools and web apps to mobile apps, background services, and hardware projects. What matters here is not the list itself, but the small and highly specific problems in his examples.

Jones describes software for a household and for a ferry commuter. These tools are useful precisely because they can fit one person’s life in a way a mass-market application cannot. A large product has to serve common needs. Personal software can begin with the odd exception that matters to one person every week.

Jones also draws the crucial line between access and ownership: “The freedom comes when you own the project files and the data and the decisions.” Project files are the editable materials and settings needed to understand, run, and change the tool.

AI lowers the cost of building around private details. That means a recurring problem no longer has to attract thousands of customers before it deserves software.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Personal software is valuable because it can serve details that are too narrow for a mass-market product.</p></div>

## The code is not the scarce part

Return to the weekly planning tool. Its first version may only combine several calendars. Its value grows when the parent corrects bad assumptions, records recurring exceptions, and explains which constraints matter most. Over time, the tool contains a working account of how the family actually operates.

A plain name for this is productive intelligence: knowledge stored so that a tool can do useful work with it again. The intelligence is not mysterious. It is the accumulated judgment that prevents the owner from solving the same problem from scratch every week.

In [*The 3 Crucibles*](https://www.cubagarcia.com/books/the-three-crucibles/), my 2026 book about technological and economic change, I argue that skills, content, and workflows should become assets that are no longer tied to every physical hour of work. The book describes context, perspective, taste, and ways of solving problems as intangible capital: valuable resources that do not take a physical form.

Personal software connects those ideas. When a person’s judgment is stored in a tool, remains under that person’s control, and keeps producing a useful result, it starts to function as productive capital. I mean that in a practical sense, not as an automatic legal or accounting classification.

Not every personal app qualifies. A tool earns the label only through repeated usefulness and retained control. It does not need revenue, customers, or a plan to become a company. That is why personal software is not miniature SaaS. The asset is the reusable capability, not the market around it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The lasting value is not the generated code. It is the owner’s judgment, stored in a form that can keep doing useful work.</p></div>

## Use the handoff test

Ownership is easy to claim while the original service is working. A better question is what you could hand to someone else if that service disappeared.

Choose one important job the tool performs. Could another builder, human or AI, reproduce that job using your data, the editable project materials, and a short maintenance note? That note should explain why the important rules exist, which outside services the tool depends on, and how to check that the result is correct. The handoff should also fit within an amount of time, money, and outside help you chose before anything went wrong.

An export button is not enough if the exported records lose the relationships that make them useful. Editable files are not enough if an undocumented paid service still controls the result. Possessing fragments of a tool is different from preserving its working ability.

This does not require total independence. Personal software can rely on cloud hosting or large AI systems. Practical ownership means those providers can change without erasing the history and judgment accumulated around the tool.

There is also a burden of care. Jones warns that AI-assisted creation can introduce unexplained paid services, expose sensitive data, or accidentally make an application public. Important personal tools need backups, access controls, and someone responsible for maintaining them. A low-stakes tool may be better treated as disposable. A lasting tool should be called an asset only after it has proved useful more than once, remained manageable to care for, and survived a handoff.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>You own the useful capability only when its data, rules, and essential result can survive a realistic handoff.</p></div>

## The democratic gain is the right to leave

I use citizen-scale ownership to mean practical control available to a person or household. It does not mean owning the data centers, advanced chips, or large AI models underneath a tool. Those parts of the system may remain highly concentrated.

The smaller personal layer can still belong to the individual. It contains the records, corrections, preferences, and decisions that make generic technology useful in one particular life.

In the epilogue to *The 3 Crucibles*, I argue that distributed ownership of productive AI assets can counter concentrated intelligence. The mechanism is straightforward. A provider has less hold over people who can leave without abandoning everything their tools have learned about them.

If every correction makes a tool more useful but harder to leave, improvement becomes lock-in. If the person can take the useful history elsewhere, the same correction strengthens the person’s own asset instead.

This is why AI’s most democratic effect may not be the number of apps people can generate. It may be the ability to turn private context into useful capital without first turning it into a product or a company.

The question for builders is no longer only, “What can this person create?” It is also, “After the tool has learned how their life works, can they take that learning with them?”

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Citizen-scale agency grows when people can leave a provider without leaving behind the knowledge their lives added to the tool.</p></div>
