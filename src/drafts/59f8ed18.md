Imagine someone tending an allotment, a small garden plot with six beds. During one season, they use an AI-assisted tool to record which bed stays damp, which tomato variety failed twice, when the shared tap opens, and who can water during a July trip.

Then their work schedule changes and a friend offers to help. Suppose the gardener discovers that the notes can be viewed inside the service but not carried out, while its rules cannot be changed without starting again. The tool still works, but it can no longer adapt to the life it was built around.

The gardener supplied the observations and corrections. The service supplied a convenient place to use them. If those contributions cannot be separated, the gardener has access to a personalized tool but little control over the value they added.

This is why I think AI’s promise is not simply that more people can make software. The harder question comes after months of use: who controls the records, rules, and corrections that now make it useful?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Personal software becomes empowering when the person controls the knowledge that makes it personal.</p></div>

## The part worth owning is what the person adds

At the beginning, the garden tool knows almost nothing about this plot. Each time the gardener uses it, they add an exception: the north bed can wait after rain, the seedlings by the fence cannot, and certain July dates require help from a neighbor. The screen may look much the same, but its advice changes.

The software becomes more useful because later decisions begin with what has already been noticed. It does not create the gardener’s judgment. It lets that judgment be used again.

In my 2026 book *The 3 Crucibles*, about technological and economic change, I argue that skills, content, and workflows become assets when their usefulness is detached from repeating the same physical hour. I describe perspective and proprietary context as intangible capital. In the garden, that simply means hard-won knowledge about this particular plot. When software carries that knowledge into the next decision, yesterday’s attention keeps helping.

The tool may never earn money or attract another user. It can still be an asset because its owner no longer has to solve the same problem from the beginning each time.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The durable value is not the first version of the app. It is the owner’s accumulated knowledge becoming useful again.</p></div>

## One user can be the whole point

Nate B. Jones, the creator of the August 19, 2026 video [Nobody Laid Out The Five Kinds Of Software You Can Make. So I Did](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=joRXo6x7Pgk), describes tools for a household and a ferry commuter. He argues that personal software can fit one person’s life in a way a mass-market app cannot. An audience of one is not a defect here. It is what allows the software to pay attention to exceptions that nobody else needs.

Jones also puts the ownership requirement plainly: “The freedom comes when you own the project files and the data and the decisions.” Project files are the editable materials used to build the tool. Data is the history it has collected. Decisions are the choices embedded in its behavior: what receives priority, which exceptions matter, and why.

A personal tool can still rely on outside services. Ownership does not require technical isolation. It means the person retains realistic choices: keeping the records, changing important behavior, and moving what matters without reconstructing years of context from memory.

Jones warns that AI-assisted building can introduce unexplained paid services, expose sensitive information, or accidentally make an application public. Those cautions matter because personal software can contain details that were never intended for an audience. Keeping control therefore includes understanding where the information goes and who can see it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Personal software can depend on outside services while still leaving its essential records, rules, and choices with the user.</p></div>

## Creation spreads only part of the power

When we call AI a democratizing force for software, creation is the visible change. But if the ability to create spreads while control over the files, records, and embedded choices stays elsewhere, people receive easier access without gaining much agency.

In the epilogue to *The 3 Crucibles*, I argue that distributed ownership of useful AI assets can counter concentrated intelligence. Personal software is where that large argument becomes ordinary. People do not need to own a frontier AI model or start a company. They can begin by keeping control of tools shaped by their own knowledge.

This is also why personal software should not be treated as an unfinished SaaS business. The gardener may be the only person the tool should ever serve. Its value comes from helping that person reuse lessons already paid for with attention, effort, and time.

For builders, the practical question is straightforward. After months of real use, who controls the corrections, records, and choices that made the tool fit? If the user can keep and reshape them, the software is turning lived knowledge into an owned asset. If not, the app may be personal while its lasting value accumulates somewhere else.

AI’s democratic effect may be quieter than a rush of new apps. Ordinary people can turn their own context into something that keeps helping, then keep that value under their control. Creation starts the process. Ownership determines who benefits from what comes next.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The deeper democratic gain is not merely making software available. It is letting people keep the value their own lives add to it.</p></div>
