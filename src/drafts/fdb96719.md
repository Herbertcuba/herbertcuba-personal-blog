The important enterprise AI question is changing. It began with model selection: Which artificial-intelligence model should we use? It is becoming a question of authority: Where may an AI agent act, and who controls the environment around it?

An AI agent is software that uses a model to pursue a goal, make intermediate decisions, and potentially call tools or change another system. In March 2025, Telenor, the Norwegian telecommunications group, and Ericsson, the Swedish telecommunications-equipment company, announced work on agentic AI for radio controls. A radio access network is the equipment and software connecting mobile devices to the wider telecommunications network. Introducing agents into that setting matters because they move closer to consequential operations than a chatbot drafting an answer.

The announcement does not prove that autonomous agents control Telenor’s production network. It came from the companies involved, described co-creation rather than broad deployment, and has not been supported by an independent technical assessment. Its value is narrower: it shows why the execution environment around an agent is becoming an infrastructure concern.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Once an AI system receives credentials and permission to act, controlling its execution environment matters as much as choosing its model.</p></div>

## The durable layer sits around the model

An agent runtime is the operational environment connecting an agent to models, organizational data, memory, credentials, software tools, and policies. It determines what the agent can access, which actions it may take, when a person must approve an action, what gets recorded, and how the agent can be stopped.

That runtime can outlast any individual model. A company might replace one model with another while retaining its agent identities, permissions, approval rules, audit records, and incident procedures. Those controls encode how the organization delegates authority. Replacing them for every pilot would be like rebuilding identity management for every new business application.

“Locally governed” does not necessarily mean hardware installed on company premises. It means that the organization can enforce its chosen rules and legal boundaries. The runtime might operate on premises, in a private cloud dedicated to one company, through a sovereign-cloud service designed around jurisdictional and operational-control requirements, or across a hybrid deployment combining several environments.

Data residency, meaning where information is stored or processed, is only one part of that control. A European data centre does not help much if an agent inherits excessive permissions, sends sensitive records to an unsuitable model, or can alter an operational system without approval. Conversely, an enterprise can call an external model through a tightly controlled gateway while keeping agent identity, data access, tool permissions, and activity records under its own governance.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The strategic asset is not necessarily a locally owned model; it is an enforceable runtime that governs how AI receives data and exercises authority.</p></div>

## The Nordic evidence is converging, not conclusive

No available survey directly measures spending on locally governed agent runtimes across Nordic enterprises. The case instead rests on three signals from different parts of the market.

The first is operational experimentation. Telenor and Ericsson’s radio-control work places agents near telecommunications infrastructure, where availability, authorization, monitoring, and recovery already matter. Nokia, the Finnish network-infrastructure supplier, separately promotes sovereign AI data-centre networks and agent-assisted network operations. These corporate sources demonstrate vendor direction, not proven customer demand or return on investment. Still, they show that major Nordic infrastructure companies expect AI agents to enter systems where errors carry operational consequences.

The second signal is investment in European-controlled computing capacity. LUMI AI Factory is a European Union initiative built around LUMI, a high-performance supercomputer operated by CSC – IT Center for Science, a Finnish state-owned nonprofit, in Kajaani. Its initial 13 services include computing resources, datasets, training, technical assistance, and compliance support. Around 3,500 research projects had used LUMI, and more than half incorporated AI.

Those figures do not measure enterprise agent adoption, and LUMI AI Factory is not itself an enterprise agent runtime. The relevant point is that European-controlled computing, expertise, and data services are becoming provisioned infrastructure rather than remaining a policy ambition. Shared regional capacity gives Nordic organizations more options than either buying every processor themselves or depending entirely on a global provider.

The third signal is enterprise demand running ahead of organizational readiness. The 2026 Nordic AI Navigator, produced by the digital-services company Vivicta with the strategy consultancy Kairos Future, drew on around 340 professionals and decision-makers in Finland, Sweden, and Norway, plus approximately ten executive interviews. More than 40% of respondents expressed caution about globally hosted AI models.

The study reported a majority spanning two distinct groups: organizations that had already piloted autonomous agents and organizations that expected agents to enter operations within the following twelve months. The public summary does not provide separate percentages for those groups, so it cannot establish how many had already moved beyond expectation. Only 31% reported a detailed AI strategy, and most assessed their organizations at levels one or two on a five-level maturity scale.

This is not evidence of a settled market. It is evidence of an emerging control problem: agent ambitions are advancing faster than the governance and operating models needed to manage them.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Telecom experiments, European computing investment, and enterprise attitudes point in the same direction, but low maturity and limited deployment evidence keep the conclusion provisional.</p></div>

## Regulation rewards evidence, not geography

The European Union’s AI Act, Regulation 2024/1689, entered into force on 1 August 2024 and follows a phased implementation schedule. It classifies AI systems by risk. For covered high-risk uses, the European Commission identifies obligations including risk management, activity logging, technical documentation, human oversight, cybersecurity, robustness, and accuracy.

The law does not require every organization to host AI locally. Most AI applications fall into minimal- or no-risk categories, and requirements for higher-risk systems concern controls and outcomes rather than one prescribed deployment model. Regulation therefore cannot support a blanket case for self-hosting.

It does make runtime capabilities more valuable when agents enter consequential workflows. An organization may need to establish which agent identity began a task, which model and software version ran, what data the agent received, which tools it called, what a person approved, and what changed. The runtime can turn those questions into operational records rather than after-the-fact reconstruction.

That requires least privilege, the security principle that an identity receives only the minimum access needed for its task. It also requires approval gates for consequential actions, audit logs recording relevant events, tested releases, and reliable ways to interrupt an agent, revoke its credentials, and reverse unwanted changes. This is where the [control-core approach to AI governance](/posts/build-the-control-core/) becomes concrete: policy has practical value when the execution environment can enforce it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The AI Act strengthens the case for traceable and controllable execution, but it neither mandates local hosting nor makes self-operated infrastructure automatically compliant.</p></div>

## The countercase favours selective control

Public-cloud providers can offer European regions, encryption, private networking, mature identity systems, audit records, and specialist security teams. For many organizations, a managed service will be safer, cheaper, and easier to maintain than a locally operated platform.

The economics also favour concentration. Capable AI systems depend on costly processors, energy, high-speed networking, software maintenance, and scarce engineering skills. Reproducing the entire stack inside every Nordic enterprise could increase expense and operational fragility without increasing meaningful control. Smaller companies may gain more practical authority from a carefully contracted European service than from hardware they cannot secure or maintain well.

Local operation can also concentrate risk. An agent runtime holding broad credentials becomes a high-value target when isolation, patching, secrets management, or incident response is weak. Prompt injection—malicious or unintended instructions hidden in content that attempt to redirect an AI system—does not disappear because the server is nearby. Neither do compromised software dependencies, unsafe tools, insider threats, or authorization mistakes.

The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity, known as ENISA, analysed 4,875 incidents from July 2024 through June 2025 in its 2025 threat landscape. Its public summary does not identify agent-runtime incidents separately, so that number describes the wider threat environment rather than an agent-specific crisis. It still reminds technology leaders that every new execution layer joins an already contested system.

There is also no verified comparative evidence showing that locally governed runtimes consistently deliver better cost, safety, reliability, or business returns than global managed services. Current sources demonstrate interest, experimentation, enabling capacity, and regulatory relevance. They do not establish a universal winner.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Managed services can outperform self-operated stacks, while local infrastructure can create new concentrations of risk; control must be tested in practice rather than inferred from location.</p></div>

## A board-defensible decision starts with authority

The evidence supports a measured conclusion. Locally governed agent runtimes are beginning to become strategic infrastructure for Nordic technology leaders, particularly where agents touch telecommunications, healthcare, finance, public services, or other sensitive operations. They are not yet a standardized or broadly deployed enterprise layer.

The likely near-term architecture is hybrid. Sensitive data and policy enforcement may remain within private or sovereign environments. Lower-risk work may use global services. European computing capacity may support selected workloads. Model routing, which selects different models according to task, capability, cost, sensitivity, or jurisdiction, can connect these environments without pretending that one provider fits every use case.

For a CIO, the practical test is not “cloud or on premises?” It is whether the organization can answer a more demanding set of questions:

- Does every agent have a distinct identity and narrowly limited credentials?
- Can access be restricted by dataset, tool, action, and environment?
- Are model requests, tool calls, approvals, policy decisions, and resulting changes recorded?
- Must a person approve actions with significant consequences?
- Can the agent be interrupted and its credentials revoked quickly?
- Can unwanted changes be reversed?
- Can workloads move when a provider’s price, jurisdiction, capability, or risk changes?
- Is a named human owner accountable when the agent behaves incorrectly?

If those answers remain consistent across several use cases, the organization is building infrastructure. If every pilot invents separate identities, permissions, logs, and emergency procedures, it still has a collection of experiments.

The Nordic signal is therefore not a rush to place every model in a company data centre. It is an early shift toward retaining meaningful control over agents that hold credentials, encounter sensitive data, and act inside operations. Whether that shift becomes standard infrastructure remains open. The decision worth making now is which forms of authority an enterprise is unwilling to outsource by accident.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The emerging Nordic pattern is a hybrid runtime layer that preserves enterprise control over identity, data, actions, evidence, recovery, and provider choice without requiring universal self-hosting.</p></div>
