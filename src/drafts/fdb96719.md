Choosing an artificial-intelligence model used to feel like the big enterprise decision. Then the model learned to call tools, retrieve private data, hold credentials, and change other systems. The harder question became: who controls the environment in which it acts?

That distinction matters in the Nordics because agents are moving closer to consequential operations while most organizations are still learning how to govern them. In March 2025, Telenor, the Norwegian telecommunications group, and Ericsson, the Swedish telecommunications-equipment company, [announced a proof of concept involving agentic AI for radio controls](https://www.ericsson.com/en/press-releases/3/2025/telenor-and-ericsson-successfully-co-create-agentic-ai-for-radio-controls-and-expand-innovation-partnership). A radio access network is the equipment and software connecting mobile devices to the wider telecommunications network. It is operational infrastructure, not an office chatbot.

The announcement does not establish broad production deployment or business results. It does show what changes when an agent approaches a live system: model quality is only one concern. Identity, authorization, monitoring, human approval, interruption, and recovery become part of the architecture.

This is the emerging Nordic signal. Locally governed agent runtimes are beginning to look like strategic enterprise infrastructure, but the evidence supports an early, uneven transition—not a settled market or a universal return to company-owned hardware.

## The runtime is where authority becomes real

An artificial-intelligence agent is software that uses a model to pursue a goal, make intermediate decisions, and potentially call tools or take actions. Its agent runtime is the operational environment connecting that agent to models, organizational data, memory, credentials, software tools, and policies.

The runtime decides which customer records an agent can read, which application programming interfaces it can call, whether it may approve a refund, and when a person must intervene. It can also record the model used, the instructions received, the tools called, the approval granted, and the resulting change. Those records matter when an organization must explain an outcome or investigate a failure.

This layer can be more durable than the model itself. Models will change as capability, price, jurisdiction, and risk change. An enterprise should be able to replace one without rebuilding every identity, permission, approval rule, audit record, and incident procedure around it.

“Locally governed” therefore means enforceable organizational control, not simply a server in a nearby building. The runtime might operate on premises, in a private cloud dedicated to one organization, in a sovereign cloud designed for particular jurisdictional and operational requirements, or across a hybrid deployment combining several environments.

Data residency—the location where information is stored or processed—is only one control. A Nordic data centre cannot compensate for excessive permissions or an agent allowed to alter a critical system without approval. Conversely, an organization can call an external model through a controlled gateway while retaining authority over data access, tool use, logging, and approval.

The strategic object is not necessarily the model. It is the system that decides how much organizational authority the model receives.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A governed runtime preserves control over identity, data, tools, approvals, and evidence even when the enterprise changes models or infrastructure providers.</p></div>

## Three Nordic signals point in the same direction

The available evidence does not directly measure a Nordic market for locally governed agent runtimes. It supports a more limited inference through three different signals: operational experimentation, regional infrastructure, and enterprise attitudes.

The first comes from telecommunications. Telenor and Ericsson’s radio-control proof of concept moves agents toward a system where errors can affect network operations. Nokia, the Finnish network-infrastructure company, also [markets sovereign AI data-centre networks](https://www.nokia.com/data-center-networks/sovereign-ai/), defining sovereign AI around control of data, computing resources, governance, networks, and jurisdiction. Nokia’s page is vendor positioning, not independent evidence of customer adoption. Together, these sources show where major Nordic infrastructure suppliers expect the architecture to move, not how widely customers have moved already.

The second signal is provisioned European computing capacity. [LUMI AI Factory](https://lumi-supercomputer.eu/lumi-ai-factory-first-services/) is a European Union initiative built around LUMI, a high-performance supercomputer at the Kajaani data centre of CSC – IT Center for Science, a Finnish state-owned nonprofit. LUMI is owned by the EuroHPC Joint Undertaking, the European partnership funding shared supercomputing infrastructure, and hosted by an 11-country consortium.

The factory’s initial catalogue included 13 services spanning computing resources, datasets, training, technical assistance, and compliance support. The same announcement said LUMI had supported around 3,500 research projects, more than half involving AI. Those project figures do not measure agent adoption, and the factory is not an enterprise agent runtime. Its relevance is practical: European-controlled computing, expertise, and compliance assistance are becoming available as shared infrastructure. A Nordic organization does not have to choose only between buying every processor and depending completely on a global platform.

The third signal is demand constrained by low organizational maturity. The [2026 Nordic AI Navigator](https://www.vivicta.com/en/newsroom/all-news-and-releases/press-releases/2026/nordic-organizations-are-guarded-optimists-about-ai---new-study-reveals-gap-between-ambition-and-execution/) was produced by Vivicta, a digital-services company that sells enterprise transformation services, with the Nordic strategy consultancy Kairos Future. It drew on around 340 professionals and decision-makers in Finland, Sweden, and Norway, plus approximately ten executive interviews.

More than 40% of respondents were cautious about globally hosted AI models. The public summary also said most respondents had either piloted autonomous agents or expected agents to participate in operations within twelve months, without separating the percentages for those two groups. At the same time, only 31% reported a detailed AI strategy, and most placed their organizations at levels one or two on a five-level maturity scale.

That tension is more informative than either number alone. Interest in agents is advancing faster than the operating models required to manage their authority. This is precisely when a shared runtime layer starts to become strategic: before every business unit builds a different answer.

## Regulation increases the value of enforceable controls

The European Union AI Act, Regulation 2024/1689, entered into force on 1 August 2024 and is being applied through a phased schedule. It uses a risk-based framework rather than treating every AI application alike. For covered high-risk systems, the [European Commission identifies requirements](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai) including risk management, activity logging, technical documentation, human oversight, cybersecurity, robustness, and accuracy.

The Act does not impose a general requirement to host agents locally. Most AI applications are categorized as minimal or no risk, and even higher-risk obligations concern controls and outcomes rather than one prescribed infrastructure model. Regulation is therefore not a credible blanket argument for self-hosting.

It does make the runtime more important when an agent enters recruitment, healthcare, credit, public services, critical infrastructure, or another consequential workflow. The organization may need to show which agent initiated an action, which model and software version ran, what data entered the process, which tool was called, and what a person approved. A runtime can capture that evidence as the work happens.

The required mechanisms are familiar from enterprise security, but agents combine them in a new place. Least privilege means giving each agent only the minimum access needed for its task. Approval gates prevent consequential actions from proceeding without a qualified person. Audit logs preserve a time-ordered record of relevant events. Tested interruption and rollback procedures let operators stop an agent, revoke its credentials, and reverse an unwanted change where reversal is possible.

This is what governance centred on enforceable controls looks like in practice, and it is the operational idea behind [building an AI control core](/posts/build-the-control-core/). A policy document can state the rule. The runtime must apply it consistently and produce evidence that it did.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The AI Act does not mandate local hosting, but consequential AI increases the need for runtime controls that can enforce policy and preserve evidence.</p></div>

## Local control can still be the worse choice

The case for governed runtimes is not the same as a case for operating the entire stack internally. Major cloud providers offer European regions, private networking, encryption, identity controls, audit logs, and security teams that many enterprises could not reproduce economically. A mature managed platform may be safer and more reliable than a poorly maintained local deployment.

Computing economics also favour shared infrastructure. Capable models require costly processors, energy, high-speed networks, software maintenance, and specialist talent. Duplicating those resources inside every Nordic company could raise cost and operational fragility without improving meaningful control. A carefully contracted European or global service may give a smaller organization more practical control than hardware it lacks the people to secure.

Local operation can also concentrate risk. A runtime holding powerful credentials becomes a valuable target if its isolation, patching, secrets management, or incident response is weak. Prompt injection—malicious or unintended instructions embedded in content to redirect an AI system—does not disappear when the server is on premises. Neither do compromised dependencies, unsafe tools, insider threats, or authorization errors.

The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity, known as ENISA, [analysed 4,875 incidents](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/publications/enisa-threat-landscape-2025) occurring from July 2024 through June 2025 in its 2025 threat landscape. Its published summary does not present those incidents as agent-runtime failures. The number is useful only as context: enterprises are adding agent execution to an already contested technology environment.

Nor do the cited sources establish that locally governed runtimes produce better cost, safety, reliability, or return on investment than managed global services. They show experimentation, concern, enabling capacity, and regulatory relevance. Vendor material is strongest as evidence of product direction, while corporate proofs of concept show exploration rather than durable results.

The countercase narrows the conclusion. Physical locality is not the strategic advantage. Verifiable control is—and an enterprise should be willing to obtain it from a capable provider when that produces the stronger system.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Self-operation can increase cost and security risk; the right architecture is the one that delivers verifiable control, not the one with the shortest physical distance.</p></div>

## The board decision is about authority, not hardware

The defensible conclusion is conditional. Locally governed agent runtimes are beginning to become strategic infrastructure for Nordic technology leaders, particularly where agents touch telecommunications, healthcare, finance, public services, or other sensitive operations. The transition remains early, and current evidence does not support calling this a standard enterprise layer.

The most plausible near-term design is hybrid. An enterprise may keep sensitive data and policy enforcement in private or sovereign environments while using global services for lower-risk work. Model routing—the selection of different models according to task, capability, price, sensitivity, or jurisdiction—can connect those environments. This preserves choice without pretending that one model or provider should serve every workflow.

A CIO can test whether this is becoming infrastructure by asking:

- Does every agent have a distinct identity and narrowly limited credentials?
- Can permissions be restricted by dataset, tool, action, and environment?
- Are model requests, tool calls, approvals, policy decisions, and resulting changes recorded?
- Must a qualified person approve actions with significant consequences?
- Can operators interrupt the agent and revoke its credentials quickly?
- Can unwanted changes be reversed where the underlying system permits it?
- Can workloads move if a provider’s price, jurisdiction, capability, or risk changes?
- Is one named human owner accountable when the agent behaves incorrectly?

If the answers remain consistent across several use cases, the organization is building durable infrastructure. If each pilot invents its own identities, permissions, evidence, and emergency procedures, the organization still has a collection of experiments.

The Nordic shift is not a rush to place every model in a company data centre. It is a recognition that agents can carry organizational authority across data, software, and operations. Technology leaders do not need to predict which deployment model will win before acting. They need to decide which forms of authority they are unwilling to outsource without enforceable limits, evidence, and an exit.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The emerging Nordic architecture is likely to combine multiple providers with enterprise control over identity, permissions, evidence, recovery, and portability.</p></div>
