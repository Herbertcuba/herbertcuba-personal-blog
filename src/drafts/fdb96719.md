An AI model can suggest a network change. An AI agent can receive credentials, inspect operational data, call a software tool, and make the change. That difference turns an artificial-intelligence purchasing decision into an infrastructure decision.

In March 2025, Telenor, the Norwegian telecommunications group, and Ericsson, the Swedish telecommunications-equipment company, announced that they had co-created agentic AI for radio controls. A radio access network is the equipment and software connecting mobile devices to the wider telecommunications network. Bringing an agent into that environment matters because the system is moving closer to operational authority, not merely producing text for an employee.

The announcement does not establish that autonomous agents control Telenor’s production network. It came from the participating companies rather than an independent technical assessment, and co-creation may include limited experiments. Yet it exposes the question Nordic technology leaders now need to answer: if an agent can act inside the enterprise, who governs the environment in which it does so?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>When AI receives credentials and permission to act, its execution environment becomes an infrastructure concern rather than a model-selection detail.</p></div>

## The runtime is the new unit of control

An agent runtime is the operational environment that connects an AI agent to models, data, memory, credentials, software tools, and policies. It determines what the agent can see, which actions it can take, when a person must approve an action, what activity is recorded, and how the agent can be stopped.

This is different from the model itself. A company can replace one language model with another while keeping the surrounding identities, permissions, approval gates, and audit records intact. That surrounding layer is likely to be more durable than any individual model because it contains the organization’s rules about authority.

“Locally governed” should therefore describe enforceable control, not physical proximity. A locally governed runtime might operate on company premises, in a private cloud dedicated to one organization, through a sovereign-cloud service designed around jurisdictional and operational-control requirements, or across a hybrid deployment combining several environments. Data residency—where information is stored—is one control, but it is not the whole governance model.

The reverse is equally important. Running an open-weight model, whose trained parameters can be downloaded and operated independently, does not make an agent secure. If that agent has excessive permissions, unverified tools, or incomplete records, the organization has moved the risk without governing it.

The strategic layer is where intelligence acquires authority.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An agent runtime governs the connection between AI capability and enterprise authority; local governance can span on-premises, private-cloud, sovereign-cloud, and hybrid environments.</p></div>

## Three signals point toward infrastructure status

There is no direct measurement showing that locally governed agent runtimes are already standard across Nordic enterprises. The better case comes from three different signals that converge on the same architectural direction.

The first is operational experimentation. The Telenor–Ericsson work brings agents near network control, while Nokia, the Finnish network-infrastructure company, and Ericsson are developing agent-based capabilities for telecommunications operations. These vendors benefit from promoting the category, so their announcements show product direction rather than proven customer demand. Still, telecommunications is a meaningful leading edge: network operators already work under demanding requirements for availability, authorization, monitoring, and incident response.

The second signal is regional computing capacity. LUMI AI Factory is a European Union initiative built around LUMI, a high-performance supercomputer operated by Finland’s state-owned CSC – IT Center for Science in Kajaani. Its first 13 services combine computing resources, datasets, training, technical assistance, and compliance support. Approximately 3,500 research projects had run on LUMI, with more than half incorporating AI.

Those projects do not measure agent adoption, and LUMI AI Factory is not itself an enterprise agent runtime. Its relevance is that European-controlled computing, expertise, and data services are becoming available as shared infrastructure. Nordic organizations do not necessarily have to choose between buying their own hardware and depending entirely on a global provider.

The third signal is demand arriving ahead of readiness. The 2026 Nordic AI Navigator, a study produced by the digital-services company Vivicta with the strategy consultancy Kairos Future, drew on around 340 decision-makers and professionals in Finland, Sweden, and Norway, plus approximately ten executive interviews. More than 40% of respondents were cautious about globally hosted AI models. Most had piloted autonomous agents or expected agents to participate in operations within twelve months.

Yet only 31% reported having a detailed AI strategy, and most placed their organizations at levels one or two on a five-level maturity scale. That execution gap weakens any claim of widespread adoption. It also explains why runtime governance is becoming strategic: operational ambition is advancing faster than the management systems required to control it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Telecom experimentation, European computing capacity, and Nordic enterprise demand support an emerging infrastructure shift, while low organizational maturity shows that the shift is incomplete.</p></div>

## Regulation makes controls valuable, not local hosting mandatory

The European Union’s AI Act, Regulation 2024/1689, entered into force on 1 August 2024 and follows a phased implementation schedule. It classifies AI systems according to risk. For covered high-risk uses, the European Commission identifies obligations including risk management, activity logging, technical documentation, human oversight, cybersecurity, robustness, and accuracy.

The Act does not require every company to operate AI locally. Most AI uses are considered minimal or no risk, and even high-risk requirements concern controls and outcomes rather than prescribing one hosting architecture. Using the law as a blanket argument for self-hosting would be inaccurate.

The law does, however, make the runtime more consequential. Consider an agent involved in recruitment, healthcare, credit, public services, or critical infrastructure. An organization may need to establish which identity started a task, which model and agent version ran, what data it received, which tools it called, what a person approved, and what changed. The runtime is where those policies can become operational evidence.

That requires distinct agent identities and least privilege, the security principle that each identity receives only the access needed for its task. It also requires approval gates, appropriate audit logs, tested releases, and the ability to interrupt an agent, revoke its credentials, or reverse its changes. These are concrete versions of the [control-core approach to AI governance](/posts/build-the-control-core/): policy becomes useful when software can enforce it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>European regulation strengthens the case for traceable and controllable execution, but it does not prescribe local infrastructure or make self-hosting inherently compliant.</p></div>

## The countercase rules out an easy sovereignty story

A mature public-cloud service can provide European regions, encryption, private networking, identity controls, audit records, and specialist security operations. For many organizations, that service will be safer and less expensive than an internally operated stack.

The economics also favour shared infrastructure. Capable AI systems require costly processors, energy, networking, software maintenance, and scarce engineering skills. Reproducing all of that inside every Nordic enterprise would create expense without necessarily creating control. Smaller organizations may gain more sovereignty in practice from a well-specified European managed service than from hardware they struggle to maintain.

Local operation can also concentrate risk. A runtime with broad credentials becomes a high-value target, particularly if patching, secrets management, isolation, or incident response is weak. Prompt injection—malicious or unintended instructions hidden in content that try to redirect an AI system—still works across physical boundaries. So do compromised software dependencies and unsafe tools.

The wider cyber environment is already hostile. The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity, known as ENISA, analysed 4,875 incidents occurring between July 2024 and June 2025 in its 2025 threat landscape. Its public summary does not isolate incidents involving agent runtimes, so the figure is context rather than evidence of a distinct agent crisis. It nevertheless underlines the operational environment into which agents are being introduced.

There is also no verified causal evidence that locally governed runtimes produce better business results than global managed services. Current sources demonstrate interest, experimentation, investment, and regulatory relevance. They do not offer controlled comparisons of cost, reliability, risk reduction, or return on investment.

These objections do not remove the need for governance. They remove the shortcut that equates governance with geography.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Managed services may outperform self-operated systems on cost and security, while local infrastructure can concentrate risk; the relevant test is enforceable control, not ownership or location.</p></div>

## The likely Nordic answer is governed hybridity

The evidence supports a qualified verdict: locally governed agent runtimes are beginning to become strategic infrastructure for Nordic technology leaders, especially where agents touch telecommunications, healthcare, finance, public services, or other sensitive operations. They are not yet a mature or widely standardized enterprise layer.

The likely architecture is hybrid. Sensitive records and policy enforcement may remain within a private or sovereign environment. Low-risk work may use global services. European computing capacity may support selected workloads. Model routing—the selection of different models according to task, sensitivity, cost, or jurisdiction—can connect these environments without treating one provider as the answer to every problem.

For a CIO, the board-defensible decision begins with authority rather than hardware. Every proposed runtime should answer a short set of questions:

- Does each agent have a distinct identity and narrowly scoped credentials?
- Can access be restricted by dataset, tool, action, and environment?
- Are model requests, tool calls, approvals, policy decisions, and changes recorded?
- Must a person approve consequential actions?
- Can the agent be interrupted and its credentials revoked quickly?
- Can unwanted changes be reversed?
- Can workloads move if a provider’s price, jurisdiction, capability, or risk changes?
- Is a named human owner accountable when the agent behaves incorrectly?

If those answers are durable across several use cases, the organization is building infrastructure. If every pilot invents its own identities, logs, permissions, and emergency controls, the organization still has a collection of experiments.

Nordic enterprises do not need to own every model or processor. They do need meaningful authority over agents that hold credentials, encounter sensitive data, and act inside operations. The measured market evidence remains early. The infrastructure decision does not.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The emerging Nordic pattern is a hybrid runtime layer that preserves enterprise control over identity, data, actions, evidence, and exit options while using several sources of models and computing capacity.</p></div>
