A business can automate a meaningful share of work without eliminating an occupation. It can also keep every employee and still leave those people worse off.

That is the tension inside the jobs debate. A job bundles routine production, coordination, judgement, learning and responsibility. AI deployments usually alter individual activities first. Management may then redesign a position, leave vacancies unfilled or, eventually, reduce employment across an occupation. Treating all of that as one event hides both the economic opportunity and the human risk.

Will AI take our jobs? It will take parts of many jobs, and some positions may disappear. That could be good if less necessary labour produces more value and a better life. It is not good merely because a productivity chart rises.

## What the evidence says now

A [2025 field study](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658) found that AI-assisted customer-support agents resolved about 15% more issues per hour. Gains were largest among less-experienced staff. That is an observed result in one company and occupation, not a labour-market forecast.

Reliability is uneven. In a [2025 experiment](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700), consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to solve a problem correctly when it fell outside the tool’s tested capabilities. Speed can disappear once checking, errors and rework are counted.

The [International Labour Organization](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure), the United Nations body focused on labour standards and working life, estimated in 2025 that one in four workers was in an occupation with some generative-AI exposure. Exposure means the technology could affect parts of the work; it does not mean the tool is in use or the job will vanish. The ILO judged transformation more likely than wholesale replacement. This is modelled possibility, not a redundancy count.

The strongest Nordic evidence is more grounded and more limited. A [study revised in March 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777) by economists Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard connected surveys across 11 highly exposed occupations with Danish administrative records. During the first two years after ChatGPT’s public release, it found no measurable average change in earnings or recorded hours, ruling out average changes above 2%. Among users who reported saving time, 85% devoted it to other work.

Two years cannot answer a ten-year question. It does show that early workplace change did not automatically become higher pay, shorter days or widespread redundancy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Today’s data show altered tasks and uneven productivity; the eventual effect on occupation-level employment remains unknown.</p></div>

## A productivity gain is not a social outcome

Suppose a claims team no longer spends each morning assembling routine case summaries. The released capacity might shorten customer waits, deepen work on difficult cases, reduce prices, increase returns to owners or support a smaller workforce. The model changes the labour requirement; contracts, competition, ownership, taxation and bargaining shape who benefits.

The attractive version is not a world in which every old role is preserved. It is one in which fewer unwanted tasks are needed to meet more needs. People can spend more time on judgement, care, relationships and craft. Organisations can make services affordable that were previously too costly. A shorter paid week could also turn productivity into autonomy, provided income does not fall with the hours.

The darker version uses the same capability to raise quotas, remove junior work that teaches the profession, weaken worker leverage and channel most returns to a small group of model suppliers, cloud platforms and capital owners. Efficiency grows while security and agency shrink.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Automation becomes progress only when lower labour requirements improve human life instead of merely shifting income and risk.</p></div>

## History offers possibilities, not reassurance

A [2024 study of US Census records](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389) found that most US employment in 2018 was in job specialties absent from the 1940 classification. New work clearly can emerge, but that comparison spans 78 years. It does not tell us whether displaced people found equally good work when they needed it.

Earlier automation also produced losses. [Industrial-robot exposure](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285) reduced employment and wages in affected US local labour markets. [Computerisation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15150) contributed to occupational polarisation: routine middle-income work contracted while employment grew toward higher- and lower-paid work. These are older US cases, not predictions for Nordic generative AI.

Their warning is narrower and harder to dismiss. National productivity can improve while particular occupations and regions carry prolonged damage. Skill mismatch and geography slow adjustment; new positions may pay less or arrive after household savings are gone. History supplies possible mechanisms, not a promise that this transition will be kind.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>New work may emerge over time, but aggregate gains can coexist with lasting losses for particular workers and regions.</p></div>

## Design the Nordic bargain before deployment scales

Nordic economies already have useful tools: [collective bargaining, social dialogue and support for people changing jobs](https://nordics.info/themes/nordic-labour-markets). They help only when AI adoption is negotiated before headcount decisions harden.

Every significant deployment should answer four questions:

- Which tasks disappear, and which human judgement, accountability and learning must remain?
- What is net productivity after review, rework, errors, integration and incidents?
- Who bears transition risk across occupation, age, gender, contract type and region?
- Where will the gain go: better service, lower prices, reinvestment, pay, leave, shorter hours, redeployment or headcount reduction?

Companies should involve employee representatives early, fund retraining and redeployment, and state how gains may reach staff. Governments and social partners need income protection, portable training and regional support. Competition, tax and ownership policy matter when returns gather around a few infrastructure providers.

The aim is not to preserve every task. It is to reduce necessary labour while preserving income, dignity, agency, learning and accountable human judgement—and to give workers and citizens a fair claim on the value created. Under those conditions, AI taking parts of our jobs could be the point. Without them, greater efficiency can simply mean a faster transfer of power and risk.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Gain sharing and transition design belong inside the AI investment case, not in the repair work after displacement.</p></div>
