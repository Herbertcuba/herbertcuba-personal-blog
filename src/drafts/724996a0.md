In a Danish study of generative AI—systems that create text, software, images, or other output from prompts—85% of chatbot users who reported saving time put it into other work tasks. Their jobs had become faster in places. Their working lives had not become shorter.

The finding comes from a [study revised in March 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777) by economists Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard. They linked surveys of about 25,000 workers at 7,000 workplaces to Danish administrative employment records. The research covered 11 occupations considered highly exposed to chatbots during the first two years after ChatGPT, OpenAI’s conversational AI product, was released publicly in November 2022. Average effects on earnings and recorded hours were indistinguishable from zero, and the researchers ruled out average effects larger than 2%.

That result complicates the argument in the [July 2026 Maxinomics video *AI Was Supposed To Take Your Job. Why Hasn’t It?*](https://youtu.be/zRv5kW5mAxM). Maxinomics, a YouTube economics channel, describes jobs as bundles of tasks, contrasts elevator operators with airline pilots, and shows how AI-assisted design made a one-off object economical to produce. Its closing forecast is sharper: a person using AI may displace an otherwise similar person who refuses it. The creator presents that as a leaning and invites viewers to check the argument. It is still a forecast, not an observed labour-market law.

AI is already taking over parts of jobs. It will transform many occupations and may remove some positions. But the good outcome is not unemployment itself. The opportunity is to reduce necessary labour while preserving people’s income, dignity, agency, learning, and claim on the value created.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI can save time long before it reduces working hours, raises pay, or eliminates an occupation. Those are separate outcomes.</p></div>

## AI meets tasks before it meets occupations

A task is one unit of work. A position is one person’s role at an employer, containing several tasks and responsibilities. An occupation is the wider labour-market category covering similar positions across many employers. One automated task may alter a position without making it disappear; many redesigned positions may eventually shrink or transform an occupation.

Automation means technology performs a task with less human labour. Augmentation means technology helps a person perform it. Productivity is the amount or quality of output produced from a given input, such as cases resolved per hour. Higher productivity does not, by definition, mean higher wages, fewer hours, or greater wellbeing.

Technical exposure is earlier still. It describes tasks an AI could affect, not whether an employer will adopt the system or use it to replace labour. A capability must be integrated into a workflow, checked for errors, assigned accountable owners, and supported by organizational change before it produces a reliable economic result.

Maxinomics uses elevator operators and pilots to show how similar technical changes can produce different occupational outcomes. Automatic controls largely removed the need for dedicated elevator operators. Cockpit automation took over portions of manual flying, while trained pilots retained regulated responsibility for safety, judgment, and exceptional conditions. Demand, regulation, workflow design, and human accountability shaped the difference.

The video’s prediction about AI users replacing non-users is therefore plausible in some settings but incomplete as a general rule. A highly capable user may become more valuable. The same productivity gain could also expand output, remove a vacancy, reduce staffing, lower prices, or be absorbed by checking and integration costs. The task changes first; the employment outcome follows from several further decisions.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Capability, adoption, task redesign, productivity, and employment form a conditional chain. No step automatically determines the next.</p></div>

## What the evidence says—and does not

The Danish study provides the most directly relevant current evidence for Nordic leaders. Workplaces introduced training and internal tools, and employees reported new AI-related tasks. Yet researchers found no adoption-linked change in employment, wage bills, job creation, employees leaving or being dismissed, or early-career hiring. Early-career employment did decline in several exposed occupations, but the researchers did not find that adopter firms caused those declines. One Danish study cannot establish a Nordic-wide result, and two years cannot settle a ten-year question.

Task-level research nevertheless shows that generative AI can produce meaningful gains. A [2025 peer-reviewed customer-support study](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658) found that AI assistance increased issues resolved per hour by about 15% on average, with larger gains for less-experienced workers. A [2023 randomized experiment involving 453 college-educated professionals](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586) found that ChatGPT reduced completion time on clearly defined writing tasks by 40% and raised assessed quality by 18%.

The boundary is unreliable. In a [peer-reviewed experiment involving 758 consultants](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700), AI users completed 12.2% more tasks and worked 25.1% faster when assignments fell within the system’s capabilities. On a task outside that boundary, users were 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer. The researchers call this the jagged technological frontier: an AI may handle one complex assignment well and fail on an apparently adjacent one. Checking time, rework, incidents, and accountable approval belong inside every productivity calculation.

Exposure models answer a different question. The International Labour Organization, the United Nations agency for work and labour standards, [estimated in 2025](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) that one in four workers globally held an occupation with some generative-AI exposure. Only 3.3% of global employment fell into its highest-exposure category, and the organization judged job transformation more likely than wholesale replacement. Exposure was uneven: in high-income economies, 9.6% of women’s employment and 3.5% of men’s employment fell into that highest category.

The OECD, an intergovernmental economic-policy organization, [estimated in 2023](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2023_08785bba-en.html) that 27% of employment was in occupations at the highest automation risk when all automation technologies—not generative AI alone—were included. The International Monetary Fund, a global financial institution, [modelled in 2024](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity) that AI could affect almost 40% of jobs globally and about 60% in advanced economies. In those scenarios, affected includes work that AI complements as well as work it substitutes.

**Observed evidence** currently shows adoption, changing workflows, uneven task-level gains, and no measurable average employment, earnings, or hours effect in the Danish study. **Modelled exposure** estimates which tasks technology could affect. **Forecasts** concern future occupational decline, new work, and whether AI users will replace non-users. Treating those three categories as interchangeable creates certainty the evidence does not provide.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Current evidence supports task change and uneven productivity gains. It does not turn exposure estimates into redundancy forecasts or establish a broad Nordic employment effect.</p></div>

## Why taking work away could be the point

A society should not preserve every inherited task merely to keep people busy. The economic opportunity is to produce what people value while consuming fewer scarce resources, including human time. Search, transcription, scheduling, routine reconciliation, boilerplate drafting, and repetitive document retrieval can all absorb hours that people would rather devote to judgment, care, relationships, craft, or life outside work.

Maxinomics makes its strongest positive case through a custom object. AI-assisted computer-aided design—software for specifying a manufacturable object—combined with 3D printing, which builds a physical object layer by layer, made a one-off pyramid economical. The example illustrates nonconsumption: a person goes without because the available solution is too expensive, inaccessible, or difficult to arrange. No designer had necessarily lost an order because the object otherwise would not have been produced.

Lower production costs can similarly make personalized education, localized services, accessibility features, research, and small-market products feasible. That creates output instead of merely dividing an existing market among fewer workers. Whether the effect is large enough to support new employment depends on demand, competition, investment, and how quickly organizations build useful services around the technology.

There is also an autonomy case. If AI removes tedious steps while leaving people more discretion over consequential work, job quality can improve. But apparently routine tasks are not always empty. Drafting, research, documentation, and manual checking often train junior employees, reveal unusual failures, and build the judgment needed for senior responsibility. Removing those tasks without replacing their learning function can improve today’s throughput while weakening tomorrow’s expertise.

Leaders therefore need to ask what a task contributes beyond its immediate output. Some tasks should disappear. Others should become supervised practice, simulation, or progressively harder review work so that the learning ladder survives.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Less necessary labour can create new output, autonomy, and leisure, provided automation removes burdens without quietly removing learning, judgment, or responsibility.</p></div>

## The saved hour has claimants

The productivity dividend is the additional value or time created from the same inputs. Once an AI system saves an hour, that capacity can move to several destinations:

- **Customers** may receive more output, better service, or lower prices.

- **Firms and owners** may receive higher margins, investment capacity, or capital returns.

- **Workers** may receive higher pay, profit sharing, additional leave, training, or fewer paid hours without a proportional income loss.

- **The labour market** may absorb the saving through redeployment, an unfilled vacancy, a redundancy, or newly created work.

- **The public** may receive tax revenue that funds services, transition income, and retraining.

- **Previously unserved demand** may become a new product, task, company, or occupation.

Several destinations can coexist. The deployment changes how much capacity exists; contracts, prices, ownership, bargaining, and public policy decide where that capacity goes. The Danish finding at the opening suggests that the immediate workplace default is often more output rather than more leisure or pay.

The distribution problem predates generative AI. A [2019 analysis across OECD economies](https://www.insee.fr/en/statistiques/fichier/4253019/510_511_512_Pak_Pionnier_Schwellnus_EN.pdf) found that real wage growth had separated from productivity growth in many countries as labour’s share—the portion of economic value paid to workers rather than owners—declined. The study is not evidence about generative AI. It shows why productivity cannot be treated as a synonym for shared prosperity.

Ownership matters for the same reason. If a small number of firms controls models, cloud infrastructure, valuable data, or intellectual property, a large share of the dividend can flow to capital. Bargaining power—the ability to influence pay, hours, and working conditions—then determines whether employees can claim part of the gain. Weak bargaining may instead produce higher quotas, tighter surveillance, less discretion, and responsibility for errors generated by systems workers do not control.

Job counts alone also miss the quality of the transition. New work can offer lower pay, less security, fewer learning opportunities, or less predictable hours. It can arrive too slowly for displaced workers, or in another region requiring a move they cannot make. National prosperity can rise while particular occupations, demographic groups, and communities carry concentrated losses.

Displacement is therefore not liberation by default. When an employer removes a salary but preserves the output, the affected worker has lost income rather than gained free time. The positive case begins when reduced labour requirements preserve purchasing power, dignity, agency, and a credible share of the resulting value.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI creates a productivity dividend, but ownership, bargaining, competition, management, and public policy determine who receives it and who carries the transition risk.</p></div>

## History is a risk register, not a promise

Maxinomics opens with the cash machine. On 27 June 1967, Barclays, a British bank, unveiled a cash dispenser at its Enfield branch in north London. The machine automated services previously performed by tellers, making it an apparently straightforward story of occupational replacement.

The mechanism proved more complicated. Economist and legal scholar James Bessen found that [average teller staffing in urban US branches fell from 20 to 13 between 1988 and 2004](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm), while the number of urban branches increased by 43%. Lower staffing costs made more branches viable, and teller work shifted toward customer relationships and sales.

That was not the lasting outcome. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics, the federal agency responsible for employment data, recorded about 347,400 teller positions in 2024 and [projects a 13% decline between 2024 and 2034](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/tellers.htm). The ATM story shows that expanding demand can offset labour savings during one period without protecting an occupation indefinitely.

New work is real, but it is easy to overstate. A [2024 study by MIT labour economist David Autor and colleagues](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389) found that roughly 60% of US employment in 2018 was in job titles absent from the 1940 Census occupational index. That is a 78-year comparison. A new title does not necessarily represent a wholly new occupation, a net new job, or work of equal quality for the people who were displaced.

Earlier computerization also contributed to occupational polarization: growth at the high- and low-wage ends alongside contraction in routine middle-income work. Autor and economist David Dorn documented that US pattern in a [2013 labour-market study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15150). An economy can create work while making the path between occupations less secure and the distribution of rewards less equal.

Some automation produced clear local harm. Economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo found in [peer-reviewed research on US industrial robots](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285) that one additional robot per 1,000 workers reduced the local employment-to-population ratio by about 0.2 percentage points and wages by roughly 0.42%. Industrial robots differ from generative AI, but the result shows how aggregate gains can coexist with regional losses.

The China shock—the concentrated rise in Chinese import competition after China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001—was a trade event, not automation. It is still relevant to transition dynamics. A [2021 longitudinal study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29401) found that adverse employment and income effects remained visible in exposed US regions through 2019. Skill mismatch, meaning a gap between displaced workers’ capabilities and available job requirements, can persist long after an economy has found new sources of growth.

Generative AI may spread faster than cash machines or industrial robots because software can be copied across thousands of cognitive workflows at low additional cost. Earlier waves reveal possible mechanisms—demand expansion, new work, polarization, and persistent local loss—but cannot tell us which combination AI will produce.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Past technologies created demand and new work, but they also polarized occupations and caused lasting local losses. History broadens the range of outcomes; it does not select one.</p></div>

## The Nordic test is governance before displacement

Nordic countries have institutions that can shape this transition, although they provide no immunity. Nordics.info, a research platform hosted by Denmark’s Aarhus University, reports that [more than 80% of Nordic employees are covered by collective agreements](https://nordics.info/themes/nordic-labour-markets). Collective bargaining allows employers and worker representatives to negotiate pay, hours, training, and working conditions. Denmark’s flexicurity model combines relatively flexible hiring and separation with income protection and active help finding new work, although national arrangements differ across the Nordic region.

Those institutions become valuable when they enter deployment decisions early. Every serious AI programme should pass five tests before its productivity claims support large-scale workforce changes:

- **State the intent and owner.** Say whether a project is intended to augment employees or substitute for positions. Name the accountable executive, map the role’s purpose and task bundle, and identify where human judgment and sign-off remain necessary.

- **Set complete stop-or-go criteria.** Establish a baseline for output, quality, checking time, rework, incidents, customer outcomes, workload, recorded hours, pay, vacancies, and internal mobility. Break results down by occupation, age, gender, contract type, and location. A pilot that saves drafting time while creating hidden review costs has not proved productivity.

- **Rebuild learning before removing junior work.** Replace automated research, drafting, documentation, and checking with supervised practice, simulations, rotations, and progressively harder assignments. Otherwise, a short-term saving can weaken the future talent pipeline.

- **Fund transition inside the business case.** Involve employees and unions before displacement decisions are fixed. Provide paid training, income security, and regional or mobility support, and use redeployment before redundancy where it is economically viable.

- **Allocate verified gains before scaling.** Decide how the dividend will be shared among customers, investment, owners, workers, and working time. Gain sharing can include pay, employee ownership, bonuses, training budgets, additional leave, or shorter hours. Publish the allocation internally and review it when real results replace pilot assumptions.

Governments and social partners—employer organizations, unions, and public authorities—must handle what individual companies cannot. That includes portable training rights, adequate transition income, regional support, effective competition, and tax capacity if income shifts from labour toward capital. Retraining without time and income support will exclude the people least able to absorb the risk.

Will AI take our jobs? It is already taking tasks. It will transform occupations and remove some positions, but the scale and timing remain uncertain. Capability, demand, organizational design, worker voice, and public institutions will shape the result.

Why could that be good? Producing valuable goods and services with less necessary labour can expand prosperity, autonomy, and time outside work. It becomes progress only when the people carrying the transition retain income, dignity, agency, learning, accountability, and a fair claim on the productivity dividend.

The goal is not to save every task. It is to make less work necessary without making people less secure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Nordic leaders can make AI-driven labour savings beneficial by governing task design, measurement, learning, transition support, and gain sharing before displacement occurs.</p></div>
