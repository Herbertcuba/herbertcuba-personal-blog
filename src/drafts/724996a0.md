The argument about AI and jobs usually begins at the wrong altitude. A chatbot can take over part of a report without eliminating the analyst’s position. A position can disappear at one company while the occupation grows elsewhere. Productivity can rise while pay and working time remain flat. Those are different economic events, yet they are routinely collapsed into one word: replacement.

Maxinomics, a YouTube economics channel, builds its [July 2026 video *AI Was Supposed To Take Your Job. Why Hasn’t It?*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRv5kW5mAxM) around a useful distinction. A job is a bundle of tasks organized toward an outcome, and technology can remove steps without removing the outcome or the person responsible for it. The video moves from cash machines to elevator operators and pilots, then to a custom object made economically feasible by AI-assisted design. It closes with a sharper prediction: a worker using AI may displace an otherwise similar worker who refuses it. That is a plausible competitive effect, but it is still a forecast rather than an observed general law.

The defensible answer is therefore conditional. AI is already taking parts of jobs, will transform many occupations, and may eliminate some positions. Current evidence does not justify saying that everyone will be replaced, or that little will change. Removing unwanted labour and lowering production costs could create more output, autonomy, and leisure. But a saved hour does not automatically become free time, and higher productivity does not automatically become higher pay.

That is the real leadership question: not only what AI can do, but where its productivity dividend goes.

## The cash machine did not settle the argument

On 27 June 1967, Barclays, the British bank, unveiled a cash dispenser at its Enfield branch in north London. British actor Reg Varney made the first £10 withdrawal, according to the [bank’s historical archive](https://home.barclays/news/2017/06/from-the-archives-the-atm-is-50/). The automated teller machine, or ATM, looked like an obvious job killer because it performed a visible task previously handled by a teller.

Maxinomics uses this as its opening parable, but the verified history is more interesting than either the fearful or optimistic version. Economist and legal scholar James Bessen found that the average number of tellers in an urban US branch fell from 20 to 13 between 1988 and 2004, while the number of urban branches increased by 43%. Lower branch costs enabled expansion, and tellers shifted toward customer relationships and sales. Automation reduced labour per branch without initially reducing total teller employment, as Bessen explained in his [2015 historical analysis](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm).

That was not the end of the story. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics, the federal agency responsible for employment data, recorded about 347,400 teller positions in 2024 and [projects a 13% decline between 2024 and 2034](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/tellers.htm). The projection is not an observed result, but it shows why one phase of an automation wave should not be treated as a permanent equilibrium.

The video’s comparison between elevator operators and airline pilots makes the same point through two different paths. Automatic controls largely removed the need for a person to operate an elevator. Cockpit automation, by contrast, removed portions of manual flying while regulated pilots retained responsibility for safety, judgment, and exceptional conditions. Technology affected tasks in both cases; demand, regulation, organizational design, and the need for human accountability shaped the occupational result.

## A task disappears before an occupation does

A task is a discrete unit of work. A position is one person’s role at an employer, containing several tasks and responsibilities. An occupation is the broader labour-market category covering similar positions across many employers. Automation means technology performs a task with less human labour; augmentation means it helps a person perform that task better. Productivity is output per unit of input. None of these terms, by itself, tells us what happens to wages, hours, vacancies, or employment.

The input-to-output model in the Maxinomics video is valuable because it separates the purpose of a job from its current procedure. Customers want a resolved insurance claim, a safe flight, a useful legal analysis, or a good meal. They rarely value every search, transcription, reconciliation, or formatting step required to produce it. Removing such steps can free people for judgment, care, relationships, craft, and decisions.

Yet repetitive does not mean worthless. Routine research can teach a junior employee how a field fits together. Manual checking can reveal rare failure modes. Documentation can preserve organizational memory. Leaders who delete these tasks without redesigning how people learn may improve this quarter’s throughput while dismantling next year’s talent pipeline.

The video’s custom pyramid illustrates a different mechanism: nonconsumption, which occurs when somebody goes without a product because creating it is too expensive or difficult. AI-assisted computer-aided design—software used to create a manufacturable model—combined with 3D printing, which builds physical objects layer by layer, made a one-off object feasible. No incumbent designer necessarily lost an order because the order would otherwise not have existed. Lower setup costs created new output.

That example does not prove that new demand will offset every displaced position. It demonstrates why substitution is only half the economic story. AI can compete with existing labour, but it can also compete with things that were never made.

## What the evidence says today

Current evidence belongs in three separate boxes: observed outcomes, modeled exposure, and forecasts.

The strongest Nordic observation comes from economists Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard. Their [Danish study, revised in March 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777), linked surveys of approximately 25,000 workers across 7,000 workplaces to administrative records for earnings, hours, occupations, and employers. It covers 11 occupations considered highly exposed to chatbots, after the public release of ChatGPT, the generative-AI chatbot launched by AI company OpenAI in November 2022.

Work changed. Adoption spread, firms introduced training and internal tools, and workers reported new AI-related tasks. Yet after two years, the researchers found no average effect on earnings or recorded hours distinguishable from zero; their design ruled out average effects larger than 2%. They also found no adoption-linked change in employment, wage bills, job creation, separations, or early-career hiring. Most revealingly, 85% of users who said they saved time redirected it to other work tasks. Workflow transformation arrived before measurable leisure or pay.

Task-level productivity gains are real, but uneven. A peer-reviewed [2025 customer-support field study](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658) found that AI assistance increased issues resolved per hour by about 15% on average, with larger gains among less-experienced workers. A randomized [2023 experiment published in *Science*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586), involving 453 educated professionals, found that ChatGPT reduced completion time on bounded writing tasks by 40% while raising assessed quality by 18%.

A [consultant experiment peer-reviewed in 2025](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700) supplies the necessary warning. Among 758 consultants, AI users completed 12.2% more tasks and worked 25.1% faster when tasks fell within the system’s capabilities. On a task just outside those capabilities, AI users were 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer. The researchers call this irregular boundary the jagged technological frontier: an AI may handle one complex task well and fail on an apparently adjacent one. Verification, rework, and accountable sign-off therefore belong inside any productivity calculation.

Modeled exposure is different again. The International Labour Organization, the UN agency for work and labour standards, estimated in its [2025 task-based index](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) that one in four workers globally was in an occupation with some generative-AI exposure, while 3.3% of employment was in its highest-exposure category. Exposure means that AI could affect some tasks; it does not mean adoption, automation, or redundancy. The distribution is uneven: in high-income economies, 9.6% of women’s employment and 3.5% of men’s employment fell into the highest category.

The OECD, an intergovernmental economic-policy organization, [estimated in 2023](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2023_08785bba-en.html) that 27% of employment was in occupations at the highest automation risk when considering all automation technologies, not generative AI alone. The International Monetary Fund, which analyzes global macroeconomic risks, [modeled in 2024](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity) that AI could affect almost 40% of jobs globally and about 60% in advanced economies. Again, *affect* includes assistance as well as substitution.

Forecasts—wholesale occupational elimination, AI users replacing non-users, or new work fully offsetting lost work—belong in the final box. They may become true. The first two years of widespread chatbot adoption cannot settle a ten-year question, but neither should scenarios be reported as current labour-market facts.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Observed evidence shows changing workflows and uneven task-level gains, not broad Nordic job loss or shorter hours. Exposure estimates describe technical potential, while claims about future redundancies remain forecasts.</p></div>

## The saved hour has an owner

The productivity dividend is the additional value or time produced from the same inputs. One hour saved by AI can become another unit of output, a lower customer price, a higher profit margin, a wage increase, a shorter paid week, a vacancy left unfilled, a redundancy, a new product, or tax revenue that supports public services. Several destinations can coexist. The technology enlarges the menu; it does not choose the allocation.

Denmark’s 85% result reveals the current managerial default: saved time becomes more work. That is not inherently bad. Additional output can support growth, lower prices, and stronger services. But if workers receive neither more income, more autonomy, more security, nor more time, the claim that automation has liberated them is difficult to sustain.

The distribution problem predates generative AI. A [2019 analysis across OECD economies](https://www.insee.fr/en/statistiques/fichier/4253019/510_511_512_Pak_Pionnier_Schwellnus_EN.pdf) found that real wage growth had separated from productivity growth in many countries as labour’s share—the portion of economic value paid to workers rather than owners—declined. The study is not evidence about AI itself. It establishes that productivity gains do not automatically flow into wages.

Concentrated ownership raises the same risk. When a small number of firms control models, cloud infrastructure, data, or intellectual property, more of the dividend may flow to capital unless competition, taxation, broad ownership, or worker bargaining power changes the result. Workers may instead face higher targets, tighter monitoring, weaker discretion, and greater responsibility for errors made by systems they do not control.

Leisure is also an institutional outcome. The International Labour Organization’s [2023 review of working time](https://www.ilo.org/publications/working-time-and-work-life-balance-around-world) treats shorter hours as something produced through policy and negotiation, not as the automatic consequence of faster tools. For a company, gain sharing might mean higher compensation, profit sharing, additional leave, reduced hours, funded training, or credible redeployment guarantees. For society, it also includes transition income and public services.

If AI removes a salary while preserving only the task’s output, it has not freed the displaced worker from unwanted labour. It has transferred income and risk. The positive case begins when lower labour requirements preserve dignity, agency, purchasing power, and a fair claim on the value created.

## History offers mechanisms, not a promise

The optimistic historical case is substantial. A [2024 study led by MIT labour economist David Autor](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389) found that roughly 60% of US employment in 2018 was in job titles absent from the 1940 Census index. Technologies that complemented human work were associated with new specialties and greater occupational demand, while direct task automation suppressed demand.

The qualification matters just as much as the headline. The comparison spans 78 years. A new title does not necessarily represent a wholly new occupation, net job creation, or a good job. It says little about whether the people displaced in one decade found comparable work quickly, in the same region, or at the same wage.

Earlier computerization also contributed to occupational polarization: growth concentrated in high-paid professional roles and lower-paid services while routine middle-income work contracted. US economists David Autor and David Dorn documented that pattern in a [2013 labour-market study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15150). It is US historical evidence, not a forecast for Nordic economies, but it warns against treating any new job as an adequate replacement.

Automation can also produce concentrated losses. Economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo found that one additional industrial robot per 1,000 workers reduced the employment-to-population ratio by about 0.2 percentage points and wages by about 0.42% in exposed US local labour markets, according to their [2020 peer-reviewed study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285). Industrial robots differ materially from generative AI, yet the study shows how national gains can coexist with local damage.

The China shock—the surge in Chinese import competition, especially after China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001—was a trade event rather than an automation wave. It is still useful evidence about adjustment. A [2021 longitudinal study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29401) found that adverse employment and income effects remained visible in exposed US regions long after the shock peaked. Workers do not instantly acquire new skills, relocate, or recover lost status because an economy eventually creates different work.

These histories identify the strongest countercase: transition pain can last, skill mismatch can strand experienced workers, regional losses can hide inside healthy national totals, bargaining power can weaken, and new jobs can be worse or arrive too slowly. Generative AI may diffuse even faster because software can be replicated cheaply across cognitive tasks. History gives leaders mechanisms to watch. It does not select the outcome in advance.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Past technologies created new work and expanded demand, but they also polarized occupations and imposed lasting local losses. History presents possible adjustment paths, not a guarantee of compensation.</p></div>

## The Nordic test is managerial before it is technological

Nordic economies begin with useful institutions, not immunity. Nordics.info, a research platform hosted by Denmark’s Aarhus University, reports that [more than 80% of Nordic employees are covered by collective agreements](https://nordics.info/themes/nordic-labour-markets). Collective bargaining lets employers and worker representatives negotiate pay, hours, training, and conditions. Denmark’s flexicurity model combines relatively flexible employment with income security and active support for re-employment, although Swedish, Finnish, Norwegian, Icelandic, and Danish arrangements are not identical.

These institutions can turn AI adaptation into shared gain, but only if leaders use them before displacement decisions are final. Every serious deployment should pass five tests:

- **Map the role before pricing the saving.** Identify its purpose, tasks, decisions, learning function, relationships, and accountable human. Then distinguish an augmentation pilot from a project explicitly intended to remove positions.

- **Measure the complete result.** Track throughput alongside quality, rework, incidents, customer outcomes, recorded hours, wages, vacancies, internal mobility, and workload. Break results down by occupation, age, gender, contract type, and region so averages do not conceal concentrated harm.

- **Protect the learning ladder.** If AI removes junior drafting, research, or documentation, replace that practice deliberately through supervised review, simulations, rotations, and progressively harder assignments. Cheap output today should not consume tomorrow’s expertise.

- **Design the transition with the technology.** Fund retraining with income security, use redeployment before redundancy where viable, and involve employees and unions early. Training without time, money, or a realistic destination merely transfers responsibility to the worker.

- **Decide where the dividend goes.** Precommit how verified gains will be divided among customers, investment, owners, employees, and working time. Make the allocation visible rather than allowing higher quotas or vendor costs to absorb it by default.

Public institutions and social partners must handle what individual firms cannot: portable training rights, adequate transition income, regional support, competition policy, and tax systems that remain effective when returns move from labour toward capital. Redistribution matters after losses occur, while bargaining, profit sharing, competition, and ownership rules shape who receives the gain in the first place.

This is not a brake on adoption. It is infrastructure for durable adoption. People have a stronger reason to improve the technology when they have agency over its use and a credible claim on its benefits.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Nordic institutions create channels for gain sharing and transition support, but leaders must activate them through task mapping, complete measurement, protected learning, negotiated change, and an explicit dividend allocation.</p></div>

## The point is less necessary labour, not fewer people

Will AI take our jobs? It is already taking tasks. It will transform many occupations and may remove positions, but current evidence cannot supply a credible universal redundancy count. The direction depends on technical capability, adoption, demand, organizational redesign, and the institutions governing distribution.

Why could that be good? Society does not owe every inherited task eternal life. Producing what people value with less necessary labour is the foundation of prosperity. It can create better services, previously uneconomic products, safer work, more autonomy, and more time beyond work.

But taking labour out of production is not the same as taking insecurity out of life. If the gain appears only as fewer salaries, weaker bargaining, degraded entry paths, or returns to concentrated owners, displacement is not liberation. It is a transfer.

Maxinomics is right to reject the simple story that a machine performs a task and a whole occupation vanishes. It is also right that learning to use AI will matter. Individual adaptation, however, is only half the bargain. Leaders decide how work is redesigned, owners decide how returns are claimed, and societies decide how transitions are supported.

AI taking parts of our jobs can be the point—if it reduces necessary labour while preserving income, dignity, agency, learning, accountability, and a fair share of the productivity dividend. The final test is not whether the machine can do more. It is whether people gain more control over what the saved hour becomes.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI displacement becomes progress only when less necessary labour produces wider prosperity and autonomy without stripping people of income, dignity, learning, accountability, or voice.</p></div>
