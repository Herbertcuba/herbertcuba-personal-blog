A customer-support team receives an AI assistant and begins resolving about 15% more issues per hour. That is what a [2025 field study](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658) in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* observed, with the largest gains among less-experienced agents. So how many jobs did AI take?

The evidence cannot answer that. The employer might serve more customers, cut queues, raise targets, slow hiring, increase pay or shorten working time. One technical result opens several economic paths.

This is why asking whether AI will take “our jobs” leads to such poor answers. AI will absorb parts of many roles and may remove some positions. Whether that becomes prosperity or insecurity depends less on the demonstration than on the organisation around it—and on who receives the gain.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A productivity gain does not determine a labour-market outcome. It creates choices about output, employment, income and time.</p></div>

## A job comes apart before it disappears

A job is a bundle of tasks: searching, drafting, checking, persuading, deciding and accepting responsibility. AI may perform one task while leaving the rest intact. If enough tasks disappear, an employer can redesign a role or remove a position. Only when that pattern spreads across employers does an occupation contract. These events are related, but they are not interchangeable.

The [International Labour Organization’s 2025 model](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) estimated that one in four workers globally had some generative-AI exposure, with 3.3% in its highest category. Exposure means that tasks could be affected, not that the workers will be dismissed. The ILO judged transformation more likely than wholesale replacement because occupations still contain work requiring human input.

Performance is uneven too. In an [experiment involving 758 consultants](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700), AI improved speed and output on suitable tasks. On a nearby task beyond the system’s capability, users were 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer. The researchers call this irregular boundary the “jagged technological frontier.” Current evidence supports task-level gains and workflow change. Net employment five or ten years from now remains a scenario, not an observation.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI encounters jobs task by task, and technical exposure is neither reliable automation nor a redundancy forecast.</p></div>

## Productivity does not choose its destination

A [Danish study revised in March 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777) connected surveys from about 25,000 employees in 11 exposed occupations with administrative records. During the first two years after ChatGPT’s launch, researchers found no measurable average change in earnings or recorded hours. They ruled out average effects larger than 2% for either earnings or recorded hours. Among chatbot users who did save time, 85% put it into other work.

The AI system makes a capability available; an organisation turns it into task change by adopting it, redesigning the workflow and accepting the new risks. From there, any productivity gain can become more output, lower prices, higher profits, better pay, shorter hours—or fewer positions. None of those destinations is coded into the model.

Lower labour requirements can be good. A clinic might spend less time on paperwork, a small manufacturer might quote custom work that was previously uneconomic, and a municipality might answer routine questions faster. People can recover attention for judgement, care and difficult decisions. But when saved capacity produces only tighter quotas or layoffs without income protection, the benefit has not disappeared. It has been captured elsewhere.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI can reduce the effort required to produce useful things. Prosperity or leisure follows only when the resulting value is deliberately shared.</p></div>

## Put the labour bargain in the deployment plan

Earlier automation offers warnings, not a script. [Computerisation contributed to occupational polarisation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15150): routine middle-income work contracted while growth shifted toward higher- and lower-paid roles. [Industrial-robot exposure](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285) lowered employment and wages in affected US local labour markets. These findings are not forecasts for Nordic generative AI. They reveal adjustment risks that national averages can hide: new jobs may arrive too slowly or be worse, skills may not transfer, losses can cluster by region, and concentrated ownership combined with weak worker bargaining can pull gains away from labour.

Nordic leaders can act without pretending to know their 2035 headcount. Before scaling a tool, map which tasks change, who may override it and who remains accountable. Protect routine work that forms the learning ladder for junior staff, or replace it with deliberate supervised practice. Measure quality-adjusted output, including errors and rework, alongside pay, hours, vacancies and internal moves. Break out the results by gender, age, contract type and region; an average can conceal who carries the transition.

Decide how gains will be shared while the options remain open: better pay, paid training, additional leave, shorter hours, lower prices, redeployment or transition support. [Collective agreements cover more than 80% of Nordic employees](https://nordics.info/themes/nordic-labour-markets), although national arrangements differ. That negotiating infrastructure can make adoption faster and more legitimate because workers know productivity is not simply a prelude to redundancy.

Will AI take our jobs? It will take tasks. Some positions and occupations will shrink, while others change or emerge. The scale and timing remain uncertain. Why could that be good? Because producing what people need with less unwanted human effort is genuine progress—if income, dignity, agency, learning and a fair share of the gain survive the transition. AI can reduce necessary labour. Leaders and societies must decide what that reduction is for.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Nordic opportunity is to treat AI productivity as a negotiated transition, preserving accountability, learning, dignity and a fair claim on the gains.</p></div>
