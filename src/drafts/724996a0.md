For most technologies, producing the same value with less human labour is called progress. With AI, the same prospect is often described as a threat. Both descriptions can be true. Productivity can release people from work they would rather not do. It can also release an employer from needing the people who currently do it.

AI will take over parts of many jobs, and it can eliminate positions. A whole occupation is more likely to change through many smaller decisions about workflow, hiring, investment and demand than vanish at one technological stroke. But that measured answer says nothing about who wins.

The useful question is therefore not whether AI takes work. It is whether lower labour requirements give people more prosperity and control over time, or simply move income and risk from workers to owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI can remove tasks and positions; whether that becomes progress depends on where the gains and risks go.</p></div>

## The work worth removing

There is no virtue in preserving avoidable toil. Search, transcription, routine reconciliation and first-pass drafting can consume time without being the reason someone chose a profession. A task is one piece of work. A job combines many tasks with judgment, relationships and responsibility; an occupation is the wider labour-market category containing similar jobs.

Removing a task can improve a job without removing its purpose. Lower production costs can also make a service or product economical when it previously cost more than customers could justify. Demand may then expand, creating work around an output that did not exist before.

Still, routine does not mean worthless. Drafting, checking and basic analysis often teach junior employees how expert judgment is formed. A useful deployment removes drudgery while deliberately preserving practice, feedback and a path to mastery.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The best automation removes avoidable effort without discarding responsibility, human value or the work through which expertise develops.</p></div>

## What the evidence shows—and does not

In a [2025 field study](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658), AI-assisted customer-support agents resolved about 15% more issues per hour. That is a productivity result from one workplace, not evidence that 15% of its workforce had become unnecessary.

Capability is also uneven. In a [consultant experiment](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700), participants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to solve a business problem correctly when it fell outside the system's demonstrated strengths. Fluent output can create rework when verification is weak.

At labour-market scale, the [International Labour Organization](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure)—the UN agency for work—estimated in 2025 that one in four workers worldwide held an occupation with some generative-AI exposure. Exposure means that AI could affect some tasks; it is not a redundancy forecast. The ILO judged job transformation more likely than wholesale replacement.

The closest Nordic evidence points the same way. A [Danish study revised in March 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777) linked surveys from 11 highly exposed occupations with employment records. During the first two years after ChatGPT's public release, it found no measurable average effect on earnings or recorded hours and ruled out effects larger than 2%. That early record cannot settle the next decade.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Current evidence shows real but uneven task-level gains, not broad occupational disappearance or a reliable long-term forecast.</p></div>

## The dividend does not distribute itself

Productivity means producing more or better output from the same input. Once a process needs less human time, that capacity can become more output, lower prices, higher profits, better pay, shorter hours, investment in new products, or fewer positions. Technology changes the amount of labour required. Demand, ownership, bargaining power and public policy shape the result.

The Danish study offers an early clue about the default. Among users who reported saving time, 85% redirected it to other work. More output may be commercially sensible, but it is not the same as workers receiving more pay or more control over their time.

History supports optimism and caution. A [US Census study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389) found that most American employment in 2018 was in job specialties absent from the 1940 classification. That is a 78-year adjustment, not a promise of a painless transition. Other research found that [industrial-robot exposure](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285) reduced employment and wages in affected US local labour markets, while [earlier computerisation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15150) was associated with occupational polarisation: routine middle-income work contracted as employment grew toward higher- and lower-paid ends.

These histories do not predict the Nordic outcome. They reveal how aggregate gains can coexist with skill mismatch, regional losses, weaker worker bargaining and concentrated ownership. New jobs may arrive elsewhere, later or with worse conditions. Automating junior work can also weaken the learning ladder before a replacement exists.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Productivity can increase prosperity while still harming particular workers, occupations and regions unless its distribution is deliberately governed.</p></div>

## Put the bargain inside the deployment plan

Nordic bargaining and social dialogue offer established ways to negotiate change before it becomes a staffing decision. Use them while the deployment is still designable.

Before scaling, put four commitments in the business case:

- **Purpose.** Name the task, desired outcome and baseline for time, quality, rework and customer impact.
- **Distribution.** Decide how verified gains will be split among investment, customers, owners, workers and working time.
- **Transition.** Budget redeployment, retraining and income support; monitor effects by role, seniority, gender, contract and region.
- **Responsibility.** Keep a named human accountable for consequential decisions and replace any junior work that previously served as training.

Public institutions have parallel responsibilities through competition policy, taxation, social insurance and support for affected regions.

The aim is not to freeze every current role. Some tasks should disappear. But society has not gained merely because a spreadsheet shows fewer labour hours. It has gained when less necessary work supports income, dignity, agency, reliable services and greater control over time. AI taking parts of our jobs can be good. Making it good is the work leadership cannot automate.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Treat AI deployment as a negotiated economic choice: remove unwanted labour while protecting income, learning, responsibility and a fair share of the gain.</p></div>
