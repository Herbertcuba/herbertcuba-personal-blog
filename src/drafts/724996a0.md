Denmark has already produced the kind of AI labour-market result that should make both optimists and pessimists uncomfortable. In 11 exposed occupations, chatbots spread quickly and reorganised work. Yet during the first two years after ChatGPT’s release, researchers found no measurable average effect on earnings or recorded hours and no corresponding employment change at adopting workplaces. Work moved before the labour-market outcome did.

That interval is where most executive decisions now sit. AI will perform parts of many jobs, transform occupations, and probably eliminate some positions. This could be an achievement if less human labour is needed to create useful things. It becomes a social failure when the same gain leaves workers with less income, weaker agency, or all the transition risk. A successful deployment can support either outcome.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI is changing work faster than headline employment. The eventual result remains open because technical capability and human welfare are separated by organisational choices.</p></div>

## Start with the piece of work that moves

The argument in Maxinomics’ [1 July 2026 economics explainer, *AI Was Supposed To Take Your Job. Why Hasn’t It?*](https://youtu.be/zRv5kW5mAxM), begins with a machine built to take over routine cash withdrawals. Barclays unveiled that cash dispenser in Enfield on [27 June 1967](https://home.barclays/news/2017/06/from-the-archives-the-atm-is-50/). The expected conclusion was fewer bank tellers. What followed was more complicated: between 1988 and 2004, average teller staffing at US urban branches fell from 20 to 13, while the number of branches rose by 43%. Economist James Bessen [attributes that coexistence](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm) partly to cheaper branch operations, which enabled network expansion and shifted tellers towards customer relationships.

This is easier to understand when we stop treating a job as one indivisible object. A task is a piece of work inside a role. A position is one person’s employment with an organisation. An occupation is the wider labour-market category containing similar positions. AI can remove a task while the position survives, just as one employer can remove positions while the occupation grows elsewhere.

Maxinomics develops this distinction through elevator operators and airline pilots. The former became a rare example of an occupation largely removed by automation; cockpit systems instead automated portions of flight while leaving skilled people responsible for the journey. The creator presents augmentation as the likelier path, acknowledges that AI reaches unreliable boundaries, and asks viewers to scrutinise his argument. His closing prediction—that an AI user will displace an otherwise similar non-user—is therefore a forecast, not a finding established by the historical examples.

Software output is not a redundancy notice. The system must work reliably on the relevant task, survive checking and rework, fit the wider process, and prompt an employer to change staffing. Demand may also respond when costs fall. Each step can alter the one before it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI encounters tasks first. Whether that changes a position or an occupation depends on reliability, workflow redesign, employer choices, and demand.</p></div>

## Evidence arrives at three different levels

At the task level, the productivity effect is real but uneven. AI assistance increased customer-support output by [about 15% on average](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658), with larger gains among less-experienced workers. In a randomised experiment with 453 professionals, generative AI [cut writing-task time by 40% and raised assessed quality by 18%](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586).

An experiment involving 758 consultants reveals why those results cannot simply be multiplied across an organisation. On tasks within the model’s capabilities, AI users completed 12.2% more work and finished 25.1% faster. On an adjacent task outside that boundary, they were [19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700). The researchers call this irregular boundary the jagged technological frontier: apparently similar tasks can sit on opposite sides of what a system can do reliably.

The labour-market evidence is earlier and quieter. A [Danish study revised in March 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777) linked surveys of about 25,000 employees at 7,000 workplaces to administrative records. Average effects on earnings and recorded hours were statistically indistinguishable from zero, and the researchers ruled out effects larger than 2%. Adopting workplaces showed no differential change in employment, wage bills, job creation, separations, or early-career hiring. Early-career employment did decline in several exposed occupations, but the researchers did not attribute those declines to adopting firms. Two years can reject some immediate claims; it cannot settle the next decade.

The large percentages in public debate belong to a third category: modelled exposure. The [International Labour Organization estimated in 2025](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) that one in four workers held an occupation with some generative-AI exposure, while 3.3% were in its highest category. It judged transformation more likely than wholesale replacement. An [IMF scenario](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity) says AI could affect almost 40% of jobs globally and about 60% in advanced economies; affect includes assistance as well as substitution. The OECD’s often-cited 27% figure concerns occupations at high risk from [all automation technologies](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2023_08785bba-en.html), not generative AI alone.

These layers answer different questions. Experiments measure performance on selected tasks, administrative records observe early workplace outcomes, and exposure models estimate where technology might reach. None is a redundancy forecast.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI has demonstrated bounded productivity gains, but those gains vary with task fit and verification. Current observations, exposure models, and forecasts must remain separate.</p></div>

## The optimistic case begins with something that did not exist

Maxinomics illustrates the positive case with an AI-assisted 3D design for a one-off physical pyramid. Without the cheaper design process, the creator would not have commissioned the object. No incumbent designer lost that particular order because there was no order to lose. Economists call this nonconsumption: a person goes without because the available option costs too much or is too difficult to obtain.

Lower costs can compete with doing without, not only with an existing worker. At organisational scale, that could make low-volume products, specialised services, and previously uneconomic customer needs worth serving. Output expands because the feasible market expands. This mechanism is more promising than a narrow plan to produce exactly the same things with fewer people.

There is also a direct human benefit. Search, transcription, reconciliation, scheduling, and first drafts can consume less attention, leaving more room for judgment, care, relationships, and craft. If paid working time eventually falls without a matching loss of income, the gain can become leisure and autonomy rather than merely higher throughput.

Yet repetitive does not always mean disposable. Routine analysis may train a junior employee to recognise unusual cases. Documentation may preserve organisational memory. A seemingly inefficient conversation may build trust. Removing these steps without rebuilding their learning or social function can make this quarter faster and the next generation of workers less capable.

History confirms that economies can create unfamiliar work, but it does not promise a gentle handover. David Autor and his co-authors found that roughly [60% of US employment in 2018 was in job titles absent from the 1940 index](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389). That 78-year horizon demonstrates adaptation; it says little about whether displaced people found equally good work quickly.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI can create value by making new output affordable and removing unwanted effort. The case weakens when automation also removes learning, trust, or income.</p></div>

## A productivity gain has to be allocated

Call the extra output or released capacity created by AI the productivity dividend. It can become more production, lower customer prices, higher profits, better pay, shorter working time, an unfilled vacancy, a new service, or tax revenue supporting public goods. The technology increases what is available. It supplies no allocation rule.

Denmark offers an early indication of the default. Among chatbot users who reported saving time, [85% redirected it to other work tasks](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777). Average earnings and recorded hours did not move. More output may be commercially sensible, but it is not the same welfare outcome as higher pay or more control over time.

Ownership and bargaining power shape the difference. Model providers, cloud platforms, data owners, shareholders, and companies with valuable complementary assets may capture much of the return. This is not a new concern: well before generative AI, research across OECD economies found that [real wage growth had separated from productivity growth in many countries as labour shares declined](https://www.insee.fr/en/statistiques/fichier/4253019/510_511_512_Pak_Pionnier_Schwellnus_EN.pdf). Concentrated AI ownership could reinforce that pattern, especially where workers negotiate individually after systems and staffing decisions are already fixed.

The consequences can also arrive unevenly. A tool may expand an experienced employee’s discretion while encoding that person’s knowledge into a system used to narrow everybody else’s role. It may reduce drudgery or intensify targets and surveillance. The ILO’s highest exposure category covers [9.6% of women’s employment in high-income economies, compared with 3.5% of men’s](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure), making occupational and gender effects impossible to treat as footnotes.

Earlier shocks show why national averages are insufficient. One additional industrial robot per 1,000 workers in exposed US local labour markets was associated with an estimated [0.2 percentage-point reduction in the employment-to-population ratio and a 0.42% wage decline](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285). The China shock—the surge in Chinese import competition after 2001, not an automation event—showed that regional employment and income damage can [persist long after a national economy adjusts](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29401). Computerisation also contributed to occupational polarisation, meaning growth in high- and low-paid work alongside contraction in routine middle-income roles.

Generative AI is not an industrial robot or a trade shock. These histories are warnings about adjustment, not forecasts. They show how new work can arrive too slowly, in another region, with different skill requirements, or on worse terms for the people who lost the old work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The same productivity gain can raise wages and autonomy or weaken jobs and bargaining power. Ownership, timing, location, and institutions determine who experiences progress.</p></div>

## Put three ledgers beside the AI business case

Nordic leaders do not need to predict the final number of jobs to govern the next deployment responsibly. They need a fuller account of what success means.

The first ledger is operational. Record throughput, quality, rework, incidents, customer outcomes, and the human review still required. A pilot that produces faster first drafts but shifts more effort into checking has not delivered the gain shown in its demo.

The second ledger concerns work. Track which tasks disappeared, which were created, what happened to hours, vacancies, internal mobility, workload, and entry-level learning. Break the results down by occupation, age, gender, contract type, and region. If routine work supplied the apprenticeship path, replace it with supervised practice and progressively harder assignments before removing it.

The third ledger follows the dividend. State how much value went to customers, capital, employee compensation, additional leave, shorter hours, training, redeployment, and public revenue. Make those choices before the benefit is absorbed into a new baseline. Where substitution is planned, provide paid time for retraining or moving into another role, credible income protection, and a named person accountable for consequential automated decisions.

Nordic institutions make this possible, though not automatic. More than [80% of employees across the region are covered by collective agreements](https://nordics.info/themes/nordic-labour-markets), with important differences between countries. Early negotiation with employees and unions can turn social dialogue into deployment infrastructure. Policymakers and social partners still need portable training rights, transition income, regional support, competition policy, and tax systems able to respond when returns shift from labour towards capital.

So, will AI take our jobs? It is taking tasks now. It will reshape occupations and is likely to remove some positions, but current evidence cannot responsibly supply the eventual scale or speed. Why could that be good? Because producing more with less necessary human effort can create prosperity, autonomy, and leisure. That promise survives only when income, dignity, agency, learning, accountability, and a fair share of the gain survive with it.

The ambition should not be to preserve every task. It should be to make less necessary labour produce more human choice—and to ensure the people carrying the transition can share in what it creates.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Measure operational value, workforce consequences, and distribution together. AI taking parts of jobs becomes progress only when lower labour requirements expand human choice without transferring all the risk to workers.</p></div>
