The hardest labour question begins after an AI system works.

Suppose a proposal team can prepare the same-quality work in less time. That is a real productivity gain: fewer human hours are needed for each proposal. Yet it says nothing about what follows. The company might pursue more clients, improve quality, lower prices, raise targets, increase profit, lift pay, shorten the week, or remove a position. One technical result opens several futures.

[Maxinomics’ YouTube economics explainer, published on 1 July 2026](https://youtu.be/zRv5kW5mAxM), builds its argument around this gap. It moves from the 1967 cash dispenser to jobs as bundles of tasks, contrasts the disappearance of elevator operators with the continuing responsibility of airline pilots, and uses an AI-assisted one-off object to show how lower costs can create demand that did not exist. Its closing bet is sharper: workers using AI may displace otherwise similar workers who do not. The creator also calls augmentation a lean, points to AI’s capability boundary, and asks viewers to scrutinise the case.

That caution is warranted. AI is already taking over tasks. Some positions may go, and some occupations may contract. But the path from less work to a better life contains decisions about organisation, ownership, bargaining, and public policy. Capability does not settle consequence.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI can remove time from tasks without deciding whether the result becomes growth, leisure, higher pay, or fewer jobs.</p></div>

## The word “job” hides three decisions

The video’s most useful move is to break work apart. A nurse, writer, programmer, or chef produces an outcome through many smaller activities. A task is one such activity. A position combines tasks with relationships, judgment, and responsibility. An occupation is the broader category across many employers.

AI can therefore automate a task without eliminating a position. An employer still has to redesign the workflow and decide what to do with the capacity released. Only when similar decisions spread across employers does an occupation shrink. Augmentation and substitution can also coexist: a system may draft the document while a person gathers context, checks the claims, makes the decision, and remains accountable.

Reliability complicates the picture. In a [peer-reviewed experiment involving 758 consultants](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700), AI users completed 12.2% more tasks and worked 25.1% faster when the assignments fell within the system’s capabilities. On a task outside that boundary, they were 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer. Researchers call this uneven boundary the jagged technological frontier: AI can handle one task impressively and fail on an apparently similar one.

Three decisions therefore sit between a model and the labour market. Can the system perform the task reliably? Will the organisation redesign work around it? Who receives the resulting value? A benchmark can reveal capability. It cannot forecast payroll.

## The first signal is rearrangement

The strongest Nordic evidence so far comes from a [Danish study revised in March 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777). Researchers linked surveys of about 25,000 workers at 7,000 workplaces to administrative records, covering 11 occupations highly exposed to chatbots during the first two years after ChatGPT’s public release.

Among users who reported saving time, 85% redirected it to other work tasks. Average effects on earnings and recorded hours were indistinguishable from zero, and the researchers ruled out average effects larger than 2%. Workplaces adopting chatbots showed no corresponding change in employment, wage bills, job creation, separations, or early-career hiring. The null result does not mean nothing happened. Work was being rearranged inside jobs before conventional labour statistics moved.

Bounded workplace studies show why adoption continues. AI assistance increased customer-support output by [about 15% on average](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658), with larger gains for less-experienced workers. A [randomised writing experiment with 453 professionals](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586) cut completion time by 40% and raised assessed quality by 18%. These are observed task results, not proof of economy-wide productivity, higher wages, or permanent employment effects.

Exposure estimates answer a different question. The [International Labour Organization, the UN labour agency, estimated in 2025](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) that one in four workers held an occupation with some generative-AI exposure, while 3.3% were in its highest-exposure category. It judged transformation more likely than wholesale replacement. The [OECD’s 27% estimate](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2023_08785bba-en.html) covers occupations at high risk from all automation technologies, not generative AI alone. The [IMF’s scenarios](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity) say AI could affect almost 40% of jobs globally and about 60% in advanced economies; “affect” includes assistance as well as replacement.

What we observe today is changing work and uneven productivity gains. What models estimate is technical exposure. Mass layoffs, AI users displacing non-users, and new jobs fully offsetting old ones remain forecasts.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Current evidence shows meaningful task change and bounded productivity gains, while exposure figures describe possibility rather than predicted redundancies.</p></div>

## The optimistic case is not job preservation

An economy does not become richer by protecting every manual step forever. The serious positive case for AI is that people can meet more needs with less unavoidable labour.

Maxinomics illustrates this with a small but revealing example. The creator wanted a one-off physical pyramid that was too expensive to commission through the usual design process. AI-assisted 3D design made it economical. In that example, no designer lost an order because the object would otherwise not have been made. This is competition with nonconsumption: technology serves demand that price, setup costs, or complexity previously suppressed.

The video’s elevator operator and airline pilot illustrate two other paths. One task bundle was largely erased by automation. In the other, automation changed the work while demand, regulation, judgment, and responsibility kept people in the cockpit. These examples do not predict AI’s path, but they show why “jobs lost” and “jobs saved” are too crude. Lower costs can remove labour per unit while expanding total demand.

That opens a worthwhile possibility. AI could reduce transcription, search, scheduling, reconciliation, boilerplate drafting, and other repetitive work. It could make specialised services affordable, create products that were previously uneconomic, and leave more human attention for judgment, care, relationships, and craft. But not every routine task is waste. Some provide practice, detect anomalies, build trust, or teach junior workers how the whole system behaves.

Productivity creates capacity, not welfare. It becomes prosperity only when the capacity produces something people value—and when those affected retain the means and freedom to benefit from it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The humane case for AI is not that every job survives unchanged, but that less necessary labour can support more useful output, autonomy, and time.</p></div>

## Growth can leave a trail

History supplies mechanisms and warnings, not an outcome forecast.

When Barclays unveiled its cash dispenser in Enfield, north London, in 1967, the machine automated transactions previously handled by tellers. A [2015 analysis by economist James Bessen](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm) found that tellers per urban US branch fell from 20 to 13 between 1988 and 2004, while the number of branches rose 43%. Expansion initially offset lower staffing per branch. That mechanism did not last forever: the US Bureau of Labor Statistics now [projects teller employment to fall 13% from 2024 to 2034](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/tellers.htm). Demand can delay or soften substitution without abolishing it.

The evidence on new work is equally double-edged. A [2024 study of US Census job titles](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389) found that roughly 60% of employment in 2018 was in titles absent from the 1940 index. That covers 78 years. It does not show that displaced workers moved quickly into comparable jobs. Research on [occupational polarisation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15150) also found growth at the high-paid professional and lower-paid service ends while routine middle-income work contracted. A new title can still bring lower pay, weaker security, or less autonomy.

Other shocks show how national gains can coexist with local damage. [Industrial robots reduced employment and wages](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285) in exposed US labour markets. The [China shock](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29401)—a trade shock, not automation—left regional employment and income effects that persisted long after import competition peaked. The lesson is about adjustment: new opportunities may arrive too slowly, in another region, or behind a skill barrier that displaced workers cannot cross without income and training support.

Generative AI may intensify these risks because software spreads quickly and reaches cognitive and entry-level tasks. Exposure is also uneven. In high-income economies, the ILO placed 9.6% of women’s employment and 3.5% of men’s in its highest-exposure category. If ownership of models, cloud infrastructure, and data remains concentrated while worker bargaining weakens, productivity can flow mainly to capital. [OECD-country research](https://www.insee.fr/en/statistiques/fichier/4253019/510_511_512_Pak_Pionnier_Schwellnus_EN.pdf) documented productivity and wage growth pulling apart before generative AI arrived.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Earlier technologies created new work and lasting prosperity, but they also produced polarisation, regional loss, skill mismatch, and gains that did not reliably reach workers.</p></div>

## The Nordic test is what the investment case counts

Nordic economies are not immune to displacement. Their advantage is that they have institutions capable of negotiating it. [More than 80% of Nordic employees are covered by collective agreements](https://nordics.info/themes/nordic-labour-markets), although national systems differ. Collective bargaining, social dialogue, income protection, and active support for re-employment can turn a technical transition into a negotiated one. They cannot make every loss painless, but they can stop efficiency from becoming abandonment.

Every AI investment case should carry three accounts:

- **Performance:** Measure the complete workflow after review, rework, errors, incidents, integration costs, and customer outcomes—not just the impressive step in the demo.

- **Work:** Show the expected effects on tasks, vacancies, positions, hours, pay, workload, autonomy, learning, and accountability. Break them down by occupation, age, gender, contract type, and region.

- **Distribution:** State how verified gains will be divided among additional output, lower prices, profit, compensation, training, additional leave, shorter working time, and transition support.

These accounts force decisions into the open. If a programme is intended to substitute for positions, say so before deployment. Involve employees and their representatives while the workflow is still negotiable. Preserve learning paths when AI removes junior tasks, use redeployment before redundancy where viable, and keep named human accountability for consequential decisions. Policymakers have a parallel responsibility to protect competition, tax capacity, portable training, income security, and regions where losses concentrate.

So, will AI take our jobs? It is already taking work out of them. It will reshape occupations and may eliminate positions, but current evidence cannot tell us how many or how quickly. Why could that be good? Because meeting more human needs with less unavoidable labour is a genuine form of progress.

The condition is demanding. People must retain income, dignity, agency, opportunities to learn, and a fair claim on the value created. Otherwise, automation does not liberate labour; it transfers income and risk away from workers. A machine can make labour less necessary. Turning that into freedom rather than insecurity remains a human responsibility.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI taking parts of jobs becomes progress only when leaders measure the whole outcome, negotiate transitions, preserve human capability, and share the gains.</p></div>
