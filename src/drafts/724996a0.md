The most useful early result about AI and work is not a layoff number. It is an answer to a simpler question: what happened to the time people saved?

A [Danish study revised in March 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777) linked surveys from roughly 25,000 employees at 7,000 workplaces with administrative employment records. It covered 11 occupations considered especially exposed to chatbots. Among the workers who reported saving time, 85% used it for other work tasks. During the first two years after ChatGPT became publicly available, the researchers found no measurable average change in earnings or recorded hours and could rule out effects larger than 2%.

That finding supports neither complacency nor panic. AI was already changing workflows, yet the usual measures of working life—pay, hours, hiring and employment—had barely responded. Productivity had created room to move. It had not decided where anyone would go.

Will AI take our jobs? It is already taking over parts of many jobs. That can remove positions and could shrink some occupations, while other roles acquire new tasks or expand as costs fall. Why could this be good? Because producing useful things with less unwanted labour is one foundation of prosperity. The unresolved question is whether the resulting gain becomes better services, higher income and more freedom—or simply higher targets, weaker bargaining power and concentrated profit.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI is already reorganising tasks. Whether that becomes better work, fewer jobs or more leisure depends on what organisations and institutions do with the time saved.</p></div>

## AI meets a task before it meets a job

A task is one unit of work: retrieving a document, reconciling an invoice or drafting a customer response. A position combines many such tasks with judgment, relationships and responsibility. An occupation is the broader labour-market category containing similar positions across many employers. These distinctions matter because software can perform a task without making the rest of the position disappear.

Consider what must happen after a task becomes faster. The employer has to redesign the workflow, decide how much checking remains, judge whether demand can grow and choose whether to use the capacity for better service, more volume or fewer people. An occupation contracts only when enough employers make similar choices and the labour saved is not absorbed by new demand or new work.

The early productivity evidence shows why this chain cannot be skipped. In a [customer-support deployment published in 2025](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658), AI assistance increased issues resolved per hour by about 15% on average, with the largest gains among less-experienced workers. That is a real task-level improvement. It is not, by itself, an employment forecast.

The limits are equally important. In an [experiment involving 758 consultants](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700), people using generative AI completed 12.2% more tasks and worked 25.1% faster when the assignments suited the system. On an adjacent task outside its capabilities, AI users were 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer. Researchers call this uneven boundary the jagged technological frontier: a system can excel at one task and fail at another that appears similar. Any productivity claim therefore has to include checking, errors and rework.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Task-level productivity is real, but job loss occurs only after decisions about workflow, demand, quality and staffing.</p></div>

## The big percentages are not layoff forecasts

In 2025, the International Labour Organization—the UN agency focused on work and labour standards—[estimated that one in four workers](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) held an occupation with some exposure to generative AI. Only 3.3% were in its highest-exposure category, and the ILO judged transformation more likely than complete replacement.

Exposure means that AI could affect some of an occupation’s tasks. It does not tell us whether employers will adopt the technology, whether it will assist or replace workers, or whether customer demand will expand. The [IMF estimate](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity) that AI could affect almost 40% of jobs globally and about 60% in advanced economies uses “affect” in this wider sense. Meanwhile, the [OECD’s often-quoted 27% figure](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2023_08785bba-en.html) concerns occupations at high risk from all automation technologies, not generative AI alone.

So far, the observed evidence shows rapid adoption, altered tasks and uneven productivity gains. The Danish records have not yet shown a clear adoption-linked shift in average employment or hiring. Forecasts of mass displacement, abundant new work or permanently higher wages remain scenarios.

That distinction should not become reassurance by default. Software can spread across thousands of workplaces without new machinery, and generative AI reaches cognitive tasks that earlier automation could not. Two years of limited aggregate effects cannot answer a ten-year question. But translating every exposure estimate into a redundancy estimate is just as misleading.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Exposure measures technical possibility. Current evidence shows task change; the long-run effect on employment remains open.</p></div>

## The point is less necessary work, not fewer livelihoods

An economy has not failed when it needs fewer human hours to produce something valuable. That is how societies become capable of making more, improving quality or freeing time for other purposes. The mistake is to confuse lower labour requirements with disposable people.

Imagine a small manufacturer that cannot justify the cost of designing a one-off fixture for a single customer. AI-assisted design could make a first version affordable, while a specialist still validates the material, tolerances and safety. If the customer would otherwise have gone without, the technology is competing with nonconsumption—the absence of a purchase—not simply taking an existing designer’s order. Lower costs can create demand as well as substitute for labour.

The same possibility exists inside jobs. Less time spent searching, transcribing, formatting and reconciling can leave more attention for judgment, care, relationships and craft. Yet not every routine task is waste. Some tasks teach junior employees how the work fits together; others provide the repetition needed to recognise when something is wrong. Removing them without rebuilding the learning path saves time today by borrowing competence from tomorrow.

Nor does a saved hour automatically become leisure. Working-time reduction means fewer paid hours without a proportional loss of income. It requires an explicit decision about staffing, compensation and workload. Otherwise, the likely result is more output in the same day. That may still be valuable, but it is a different benefit.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The upside is not unemployment. It is more useful output and more human freedom from each hour of necessary work.</p></div>

## An economy can adapt while people are left behind

Earlier automation shows that economies can invent work on an enormous scale. Research by David Autor and colleagues found that [roughly 60% of US employment in 2018](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389) was in job titles absent from the 1940 occupational index. But that comparison spans 78 years. It does not tell us whether displaced workers moved quickly, whether the new jobs paid as well or whether they appeared in the same regions.

Other evidence gives the less comfortable version of adjustment. Research on [industrial robots in US local labour markets](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285) found employment and wage losses in more exposed areas. Research on the [China import shock](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29401)—a trade shock rather than an automation wave—shows why geography matters: local employment and income damage can persist for years even when the national economy keeps growing.

New work can also be polarised, with growth concentrated in highly paid professional roles and lower-paid services while routine middle-income jobs contract. A displaced administrator cannot instantly become an AI engineer, and a new role in Stockholm does little for someone tied to a town whose largest employer is reducing staff. Skill mismatch, housing, family obligations and professional identity all slow the movement that looks effortless in an economic model.

The risks will not be evenly shared. The ILO estimates that in high-income economies, 9.6% of women’s employment and 3.5% of men’s employment sit in its highest generative-AI exposure category, largely because occupations remain gender-segregated. Exposure is not job loss, but it identifies where transition plans should begin. Similar scrutiny is needed for junior workers, contractors and regions dependent on a narrow group of employers.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>New work can coexist with painful losses for particular people and places, so transition speed and job quality matter as much as totals.</p></div>

## Decide where the saved hour goes

Suppose a team can deliver the same useful output in 800 hours instead of 1,000 after paying for models, integration, checking and rework. Those 200 hours are a productivity dividend. The company can use them to serve more customers, lower prices, increase profit, raise compensation, shorten working time, fund training or remove a vacancy. Usually it will choose a mixture.

The technology does not make that allocation. Managers, customers, owners, contracts and public institutions do. Earlier [OECD-country research](https://www.insee.fr/en/statistiques/fichier/4253019/510_511_512_Pak_Pionnier_Schwellnus_EN.pdf), conducted before generative AI, documented periods when real wages grew more slowly than productivity and labour’s share of income declined. That history does not predict the AI outcome, but it removes any basis for assuming that productivity will naturally reach workers.

For Nordic leaders, the practical questions should arrive before large-scale deployment:

- **Which work should disappear?** Map the tasks, but also record the purpose, accountable decisions and learning function of each role.
- **Is the gain real?** Measure output, quality, customer outcomes, model costs, checking, errors and rework—not only minutes saved in a demonstration.
- **Where will verified gains go?** Decide how much capacity supports growth, compensation, additional leave, shorter hours, training and redeployment before higher targets become the default.
- **Who carries the transition risk?** Track effects by occupation, gender, age, contract type and region, and involve employees and worker representatives while choices remain open.

The Nordic countries have useful infrastructure for this conversation. [More than 80% of Nordic employees are covered by collective agreements](https://nordics.info/themes/nordic-labour-markets), although the national systems differ. Collective bargaining, social dialogue and active labour-market support provide channels for negotiating gain sharing and transition. They do not guarantee a fair result; leaders still have to use them.

Some choices sit beyond any individual company. Concentrated ownership of models, cloud infrastructure and data can direct a growing share of the gains towards a small set of firms and investors. Competition policy, broad access to infrastructure, tax capacity, income protection, retraining and regional support are therefore part of AI policy, not repairs to be considered after displacement occurs.

Will AI take our jobs? It will take tasks, reshape occupations and remove some positions. It will probably also create work that current forecasts cannot name. The balance and pace remain uncertain.

Could that be good? Yes. Reducing the labour needed to produce a good life is one of technology’s oldest promises. It becomes progress only when people retain income, dignity and agency through the transition—and receive a fair share of the productivity they helped create. Otherwise, the saved hour is not freedom. It is risk transferred from the organisation to the worker.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI taking parts of jobs can be progress only when the saved value supports useful output while workers keep income, dignity, agency and a fair share of the gain.</p></div>
