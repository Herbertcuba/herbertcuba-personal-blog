In Denmark, generative AI—systems that create text, software, images, or other output from prompts—has already answered one narrow version of the jobs question. Among chatbot users who reported saving time, 85% put it into other work tasks. Parts of their jobs had become faster. Their paid working lives had not become shorter.

The finding comes from a [study revised in March 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777) by economists Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard. They linked surveys of about 25,000 workers at 7,000 workplaces to Danish administrative employment records. The research covered 11 occupations considered highly exposed to chatbots during the first two years after ChatGPT, OpenAI’s conversational AI product, was released publicly in November 2022. Average effects on earnings and recorded hours were indistinguishable from zero, and the researchers ruled out average effects larger than 2%.

That saved hour reveals what is missing from most arguments about AI and employment. The technology can change how work is done before it changes how many people work, what they earn, or how much time they get back.

The [July 2026 Maxinomics video *AI Was Supposed To Take Your Job. Why Hasn’t It?*](https://youtu.be/zRv5kW5mAxM) builds its case around that distinction. Maxinomics, a YouTube economics channel, begins with the cash machine, describes jobs as processes made of tasks, contrasts elevator operators with airline pilots, and uses an AI-assisted custom object to show how lower costs can create demand that did not exist before. It ends with a forecast: a person using AI may displace an otherwise similar person who refuses it. The creator presents augmentation as a tentative judgment, acknowledges the limits of current systems, and invites viewers to check the argument.

The video’s central distinction is sound. AI encounters tasks before it encounters occupations. Its optimistic conclusion needs one more variable: who receives the time and value released when those tasks require less labour.

Will AI take our jobs? It is already taking tasks, will probably remove some positions, and may shrink some occupations. The effect on total employment, wages, and working time remains uncertain. Why could that be good? Because producing valuable goods and services with less necessary labour could give us more output, more autonomy, or more time. Whether it does depends on decisions made by companies, workers, owners, and public institutions.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI can save time long before it changes employment, pay, or working hours. The destination of that saved time is the real economic question.</p></div>

## The first thing AI takes is a task

Maxinomics describes a job as a process that turns inputs into a desired result. A programmer turns requirements into working software. A nurse turns clinical information and care into better patient outcomes. Between the input and the result sit many distinct activities, some valuable in themselves and others merely necessary under the current way of working.

A **task** is one unit of that work. A **position** is one person’s role at an employer, containing several tasks and responsibilities. An **occupation** is the wider labour-market category covering similar positions across many employers. Automating document retrieval may change a lawyer’s position without eliminating it. Enough changes across enough employers may eventually transform or contract the occupation.

**Automation** means technology performs a task with less human labour. **Augmentation** means it helps a person perform the task. **Productivity** is the amount or quality of output produced from a given input, such as cases resolved per hour. None of those terms tells us, on its own, what happens to wages, working hours, headcount, or wellbeing.

Technical **exposure** comes earlier still. It describes work an AI could affect, not work that has already been automated. An employer must adopt the system, integrate it into a workflow, account for errors, assign responsibility, and redesign the surrounding work before technical capability becomes a reliable economic result.

The video’s elevator and airline examples show why this sequence matters. Automatic controls largely removed the need for dedicated elevator operators. Cockpit automation took over portions of manual flying, while trained pilots retained responsibility for safety, judgment, and exceptional conditions. Similar technical substitution produced different occupational outcomes because demand, regulation, workflow design, and human accountability differed.

The closing Maxinomics forecast—that an AI user may replace a non-user—could prove true in particular roles. It is not the only possible result. An employer might keep staffing unchanged and produce more, leave a vacancy unfilled, lower prices, reduce hours, or discover that verification and integration consume much of the apparent gain.

The causal chain therefore runs from possible exposure to adoption, from adoption to task redesign, and from redesign to a possible productivity gain. Management, markets, ownership, and institutions then determine the employment and welfare effects. No link is automatic.

## Three kinds of evidence make three different claims

The current debate mixes observed workplace results, modelled exposure, and forecasts. Keeping them separate produces a less dramatic headline and a more useful executive picture.

**Observed workplace evidence** shows that generative AI can improve performance on bounded tasks. In a [2025 peer-reviewed customer-support study](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658), economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond found that AI assistance increased issues resolved per hour by about 15% on average. Less-experienced workers gained the most. The study covered one company and occupation, so it demonstrates a mechanism rather than an economy-wide result.

A [2023 randomized experiment](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586) by researchers Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang involved 453 college-educated professionals completing defined writing assignments. ChatGPT reduced completion time by 40% and raised assessed quality by 18%. Again, faster performance on short tasks does not establish lasting firm productivity or lower demand for workers.

The boundary of those capabilities is unreliable. In a [peer-reviewed experiment involving 758 consultants](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700), management researcher Fabrizio Dell’Acqua and colleagues found that AI users completed 12.2% more tasks and worked 25.1% faster when assignments were within the system’s capabilities. On a task outside that boundary, users were 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer. The researchers call this the **jagged technological frontier**: an AI may perform one difficult task well and fail on an apparently similar one. Review time, rework, incidents, and accountable approval belong inside any honest productivity calculation.

The Danish records add a labour-market check. Despite rapid adoption and reported changes in work, the researchers found no adoption-linked change in employment, wage bills, job creation, separations, or early-career hiring. Early-career employment declined in several exposed occupations, but the study did not find that adopting firms caused those declines. This is strong evidence about the first two years in Denmark. It cannot settle a ten-year Nordic or global question.

**Modelled exposure** asks what technology could affect. The International Labour Organization, the United Nations agency for work and labour standards, [estimated in 2025](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) that one in four workers globally held an occupation with some generative-AI exposure. Only 3.3% of global employment was in its highest-exposure category, and the organization judged transformation more likely than wholesale replacement. Exposure was uneven: in high-income economies, 9.6% of women’s employment and 3.5% of men’s employment fell into the highest category.

The OECD, an intergovernmental economic-policy organization, [estimated in 2023](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2023_08785bba-en.html) that 27% of employment was in occupations at the highest risk when all automation technologies—not generative AI alone—were included. The International Monetary Fund, a global financial institution, [modelled in 2024](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity) that AI could affect almost 40% of jobs globally and about 60% in advanced economies. In that scenario, affected includes work AI complements as well as work it substitutes.

**Forecasts** cover what happens next: whether AI users replace non-users, occupations contract, new work offsets displacement, wages rise, or working time falls. Those outcomes remain open. Exposure is not adoption, adoption is not redundancy, and a task-level gain is not yet a labour-market law.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Observed evidence supports changing workflows and uneven task-level gains. Exposure models and long-term employment forecasts answer different questions.</p></div>

## Meanwhile in sci-fi

<div class="scifi"><span class="scifi__label">Meanwhile in sci-fi</span><p class="scifi__film">The Jetsons (1962)</p><p>The American animated television series imagined machines delivering an effortless life of abundance and leisure. It maps to this article’s argument because technical capacity alone cannot produce that outcome: workplace choices and economic institutions determine whether saved labour becomes free time, higher targets, or lost income.</p></div>

## Less necessary labour is a legitimate goal

A healthy economy should not preserve every inherited task merely to keep people occupied. The purpose of work is to produce goods, services, care, knowledge, and other outcomes people value. If the same outcome can be achieved with less unwanted effort, that is a potential gain.

The Maxinomics video also treats income as a source of options. People may value what their pay allows them to do more than they value every repetitive step in their current role. Removing search, transcription, scheduling, boilerplate drafting, routine reconciliation, or repetitive document retrieval could leave more attention for judgment, relationships, craft, care, and decisions.

The video’s custom-object example develops a second route to prosperity. AI-assisted computer-aided design—software used to specify a manufacturable object—combined with 3D printing, which builds an object layer by layer, made a one-off pyramid economical to produce. The example illustrates **nonconsumption**: someone goes without because the existing solution is too expensive, inaccessible, or difficult to arrange. No incumbent designer necessarily lost an order because the object would otherwise not have been made.

Lower costs could make personalized services, accessibility features, small-market products, and previously uneconomic research feasible. In those cases, AI expands output instead of simply dividing an existing market among fewer workers. Whether that expansion creates good jobs depends on demand, competition, complementary investment, and the time needed to build useful services around the technology.

There is an autonomy case too. Removing tedious steps can improve job quality when people gain more discretion over consequential work. Yet apparently routine tasks are not always disposable. Drafting, research, documentation, and manual checking often train junior employees, expose unusual failures, and build the judgment required for senior responsibility. Automating those activities without replacing their learning function can improve this quarter’s throughput while weakening the future talent pipeline.

Leaders should therefore ask what each task contributes beyond its immediate output. Some tasks should disappear. Others need to become supervised practice, simulation, rotation, or progressively harder review work so that the learning ladder remains intact.

The final possibility is **working-time reduction**: fewer paid hours without a proportional loss of income. A system saving 30 minutes on a task does not deliver that outcome by itself. Schedules, workloads, staffing, and pay have to change as well.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Taking work away can create output, autonomy, and leisure, provided organisations preserve the learning, judgment, responsibility, and income attached to good work.</p></div>

## Every saved hour is an allocation decision

The **productivity dividend** is the additional value or time created from the same inputs. Once AI saves an hour, several claimants appear:

- Customers may receive more output, better service, or lower prices.

- Firms and owners may receive higher margins, investment capacity, or capital returns.

- Workers may receive higher pay, profit sharing, additional leave, training, or shorter hours.

- Employers may remove a vacancy, redeploy a person, or make a position redundant.

- New demand may support a product, task, company, or occupation that was not previously viable.

- The public may receive tax revenue for services, transition income, or retraining.

Several destinations can coexist, but the same hour cannot be counted twice. A business case cannot promise both 30% more output and 30% more free capacity unless it explains how the gain is divided. The Danish evidence suggests that the immediate workplace default is often additional output: most users who saved time redirected it to other work.

This distribution problem predates generative AI. A [2019 analysis across OECD economies](https://www.insee.fr/en/statistiques/fichier/4253019/510_511_512_Pak_Pionnier_Schwellnus_EN.pdf) found that real wage growth had separated from productivity growth in many countries as the **labour share**—the portion of economic value paid to workers rather than owners—declined. The study is not evidence about generative AI. It explains why higher productivity cannot be treated as shared prosperity by definition.

Ownership shapes the result. When control of models, cloud infrastructure, valuable data, and intellectual property is concentrated, much of the dividend can flow to a small group of firms and investors. **Bargaining power** is the ability of workers or employers to influence pay, hours, and working conditions. If workers have little of it, augmentation may arrive as higher quotas, tighter surveillance, reduced discretion, and responsibility for errors produced by systems they do not control.

The number of jobs is an incomplete measure. New work may offer lower pay, weaker security, worse hours, or fewer routes to advancement. It may appear too slowly for displaced workers, demand skills they have had no chance to acquire, or emerge in a region they cannot reasonably move to. An economy can become more productive while particular occupations, demographic groups, and communities carry concentrated losses.

A redundancy creates unpaid time, not leisure. Displacement becomes a gain only when lower labour requirements are connected to purchasing power, security, agency, and a fair claim on the resulting value.

## History gives us mechanisms, not reassurance

Maxinomics opens with the cash machine because it frustrates a simple prediction. On 27 June 1967, Barclays, a British bank, [unveiled a cash dispenser](https://home.barclays/news/2017/06/from-the-archives-the-atm-is-50/) at its Enfield branch in north London. The machine automated services previously performed by tellers, which made occupational elimination seem like the obvious outcome.

The first phase was more complicated. Economist and legal scholar James Bessen found that [average teller staffing in urban US branches fell from 20 to 13 between 1988 and 2004](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm), while the number of urban branches increased by 43%. Lower staffing costs made more branches viable, and teller work shifted towards customer relationships and sales.

That outcome did not last forever. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics, the federal agency responsible for labour data, recorded about 347,400 teller positions in 2024 and [projects a 13% decline from 2024 to 2034](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/tellers.htm). The first figures are observed history; the decline is a projection. Together they show that expanding demand can offset labour savings during one period without protecting an occupation indefinitely.

Technology has also created new work. A [2024 study led by MIT labour economist David Autor](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389) found that roughly 60% of US employment in 2018 was in job titles absent from the 1940 Census occupational index. That is a 78-year comparison, not evidence of rapid adjustment. A new title does not necessarily represent a wholly new occupation, a net new job, or work of comparable quality for the people displaced.

Earlier computerization contributed to **occupational polarization**: growth at the high- and low-wage ends alongside contraction in routine middle-income work. Autor and economist David Dorn documented that US pattern in a [2013 labour-market study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15150). New work can therefore coexist with a weaker path between occupations and a less equal distribution of rewards.

Some automation waves caused clear local harm. Economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo found in [peer-reviewed research on US industrial robots](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285) that one additional robot per 1,000 workers reduced the local employment-to-population ratio by about 0.2 percentage points and wages by roughly 0.42%. Industrial robots are not generative AI, and US commuting zones are not Nordic labour markets. The result still demonstrates how national gains can coexist with regional losses.

The **China shock** was the concentrated rise in Chinese import competition after China joined the World Trade Organization, the global trade body, in 2001. It was a trade event rather than automation, but it reveals how slowly labour markets can adjust. A [2021 longitudinal study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29401) found that adverse employment and income effects remained visible in exposed US regions through 2019. **Skill mismatch**—a gap between displaced workers’ capabilities and available job requirements—can persist after an economy has found new sources of growth.

Generative AI may follow none of these paths exactly. Software can spread across cognitive workflows faster and at less additional cost than physical machinery. Previous waves identify mechanisms worth planning for: demand expansion, new occupations, polarization, wage pressure, and persistent regional loss. They do not tell us which combination AI will produce.

## The Nordic advantage has to be used

Nordic countries possess institutions capable of shaping the transition, although those institutions offer no immunity. Nordics.info, a research platform hosted by Denmark’s Aarhus University, reports that [more than 80% of Nordic employees are covered by collective agreements](https://nordics.info/themes/nordic-labour-markets). **Collective bargaining** allows employers and worker representatives to negotiate pay, hours, training, and working conditions. Denmark’s **flexicurity** model combines relatively flexible hiring and separation with income protection and active help finding new work. The arrangements differ across Sweden, Denmark, Finland, Norway, and Iceland.

Those institutions become an advantage only when they enter AI decisions before displacement. A serious programme should pass five tests:

- **Map the role before discussing headcount.** Record its purpose, tasks, decisions, learning functions, and accountability. State whether the project is intended to augment employees or substitute for positions, and name the executive responsible for that choice.

- **Measure the complete workflow.** At pilot level, track task time, quality, checking, rework, incidents, and customer outcomes. At portfolio level, track vacancies, internal mobility, training, workload, pay, and recorded hours. At board level, track how gains are divided among customers, investment, owners, workers, and working time. Break material outcomes down by occupation, age, gender, contract type, and location.

- **Protect learning and responsibility.** Replace automated junior tasks with supervised practice, simulations, rotations, and progressively harder assignments. Keep named human authority where decisions can materially affect people, safety, rights, or public trust.

- **Include transition costs in the investment case.** Involve employees and unions before workforce decisions are fixed. Fund paid retraining, income support, redeployment, and regional or mobility assistance. A project has not created social value by moving its costs from the company ledger to displaced workers and municipalities.

- **Allocate verified gains before scaling.** Define **gain sharing** as a concrete division of productivity improvements through pay, employee ownership, bonuses, training budgets, additional leave, or shorter hours. Publish the allocation internally and revise it when measured results replace pilot assumptions.

Governments and social partners—public authorities, employer organisations, and unions—must handle risks that individual companies cannot. Their work includes portable training rights, adequate transition income, regional support, effective competition, and tax capacity if income shifts from labour towards capital. **Active labour-market policy** means training, placement, and mobility support designed to keep people connected to employment. Training without time and income security will exclude those least able to carry transition risk.

Will AI take our jobs? It is already taking parts of them. Some positions will disappear, many occupations will change, and current evidence cannot yet establish the eventual total. Nordic leaders should plan for uneven effects rather than wait for a single national number.

Why could that be good? Producing what people value with less necessary labour can expand prosperity, autonomy, and time outside work. It becomes progress when the people carrying the transition retain income, dignity, agency, learning, accountability, and a fair share of the productivity dividend.

The useful goal is not to preserve every task. It is to make less work necessary without making people less secure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Nordic leaders can turn AI-driven labour savings into progress by governing task design, measurement, learning, transitions, ownership, and gain sharing before displacement occurs.</p></div>
