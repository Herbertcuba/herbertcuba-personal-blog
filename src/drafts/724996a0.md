A saved hour is not yet an hour of freedom.

In a [Danish study revised in March 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777), economists Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard linked surveys of about 25,000 workers at 7,000 workplaces to national employment records. They studied 11 occupations highly exposed to chatbots during the first two years after ChatGPT—OpenAI’s conversational generative-AI product—was released publicly in November 2022. Generative AI is software that creates text, images, code, or other content from prompts.

Among users who said chatbots saved them time, 85% redirected it to other work tasks. Average earnings and recorded hours did not change detectably, and the researchers ruled out average effects larger than 2% for those outcomes. The work had changed. Leisure and higher pay had not followed.

That result sharpens the question posed by [Maxinomics in its July 2026 video *AI Was Supposed To Take Your Job. Why Hasn’t It?*](https://youtu.be/zRv5kW5mAxM). Maxinomics, a YouTube economics channel, begins with the arrival of the cash machine, treats jobs as collections of tasks, contrasts elevator operators with airline pilots, and shows how AI helped make a one-off physical product economical. It closes with a forecast that an AI user may displace an otherwise similar person who refuses the technology. The creator presents augmentation as a tentative judgment, acknowledges the limits of current systems, and asks viewers to check the argument.

For Nordic technology and business leaders, the defensible answer is conditional. AI is already taking tasks and will probably remove some positions. It may shrink some occupations while expanding others. That could be good if less human effort produces more useful output, greater autonomy, or shorter working time. It becomes bad when the same gain produces weaker bargaining power, insecure transitions, and concentrated returns.

This turns the executive question from *Will AI take jobs?* into five practical tests: Which task disappears? What new value appears? Who remains responsible? Who carries the transition cost? Who receives the saved hour?

## A job disappears only after several decisions

Maxinomics’s strongest idea is that a job is not a single activity. The video uses nurses, writers, programmers, and chefs to show how people turn inputs into outcomes through many separate steps.

A **task** is one unit of that work. A **position**, or job, is one person’s role at an employer, containing several tasks and responsibilities. An **occupation** is the wider labour-market category covering similar positions across employers. AI can automate document retrieval without eliminating a lawyer’s position. Only broader changes across many employers can transform or contract the occupation.

**Automation** means technology performs a task with less human labour. **Augmentation** means it helps a person perform the task. **Productivity** means producing more or better output from the same input. None of these outcomes automatically changes wages, working hours, or headcount.

Technical **exposure** comes earlier still. It means an AI could affect some tasks in an occupation. Before exposure becomes an employment result, an organisation must adopt the technology, integrate it into a workflow, manage errors, assign responsibility, and decide what to do with any gain. The chain runs from exposure to adoption, from adoption to redesign, from redesign to possible productivity, and from productivity to an allocation decision. Every link can break.

The video’s elevator and airline examples make the distinction concrete. Automatic controls largely removed the need for dedicated elevator operators. Cockpit automation took over portions of manual flying, while trained pilots retained responsibility for safety, judgment, and exceptional conditions. Similar task substitution produced different occupational outcomes because demand, regulation, workflow design, and human accountability differed.

The closing Maxinomics prediction—that an AI user may replace a non-user—is therefore a plausible scenario, not an observed labour-market law. An employer could instead retain its workforce and produce more, leave a vacancy unfilled, reduce hours, lower prices, or discover that checking and integration consume much of the apparent saving.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI capability affects employment only after adoption, workflow redesign, productivity, and allocation decisions. Tasks, positions, and occupations are different units.</p></div>

## Current evidence is narrower than the headlines

Three kinds of evidence are routinely mixed together: observed workplace results, modelled exposure, and forecasts. They answer different questions.

Observed results show that generative AI can improve performance on bounded tasks. A [2025 peer-reviewed customer-support study](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658) by economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond found that AI assistance increased issues resolved per hour by about 15% on average, with larger gains for less-experienced workers. The study covered one company and occupation, so it demonstrates a mechanism rather than an economy-wide outcome.

A [2023 randomized experiment](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586) by economists Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang involved 453 college-educated professionals completing defined writing tasks. ChatGPT reduced completion time by 40% and raised assessed quality by 18%. Faster performance in a short experiment does not establish lasting firm productivity or lower demand for workers.

The boundary is also unreliable. In a [peer-reviewed study involving 758 consultants](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700), management researcher Fabrizio Dell’Acqua and colleagues found that AI users completed 12.2% more tasks and worked 25.1% faster when assignments were within the system’s capabilities. On a task outside that boundary, users were 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer. The researchers call this irregular boundary the **jagged technological frontier**. Review time, rework, incidents, and accountable approval belong inside any honest productivity calculation.

The Danish records provide a wider check. Despite rapid adoption and reported workflow changes, the researchers found no adoption-linked change in employment, wage bills, job creation, separations, or early-career hiring. That is strong evidence about the first two years in Denmark. It cannot settle a ten-year Nordic question.

Modelled exposure asks what technology could affect. The International Labour Organization, the United Nations agency for work and labour standards, [estimated in 2025](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) that one in four workers globally held an occupation with some generative-AI exposure. Only 3.3% of global employment was in its highest-exposure category, and the agency judged transformation more likely than wholesale replacement. The risk was uneven: in high-income economies, 9.6% of women’s employment and 3.5% of men’s employment fell into the highest category.

The OECD, an intergovernmental economic-policy organisation, [estimated in 2023](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2023_08785bba-en.html) that 27% of employment was in occupations at the highest risk when all automation technologies—not generative AI alone—were included. The International Monetary Fund, a global financial institution, [modelled in 2024](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity) that AI could affect almost 40% of jobs globally and about 60% in advanced economies. In that scenario, *affected* includes work AI complements as well as work it substitutes.

Forecasts cover what happens next: whether occupations contract, new work arrives quickly enough, wages rise, or working time falls. Exposure is not adoption. Adoption is not redundancy. A task-level gain is not yet a labour-market law.

## Less necessary labour could be the point

A healthy economy should not preserve unwanted effort simply because income is currently attached to it. The purpose of work is to produce goods, services, care, knowledge, and other outcomes people value. If the same result can be achieved with less tedious or hazardous labour, that is a potential gain.

Maxinomics uses a familiar five-level needs pyramid—a later popularization of American psychologist Abraham Maslow’s 1943 theory of human motivation—to argue that income provides security and options beyond work. It then gives this idea a material example. AI-assisted computer-aided design, software for specifying a manufacturable object, combined with 3D printing, which builds objects layer by layer, made a one-off pyramid economical to produce.

The example illustrates **nonconsumption**: someone goes without because the available solution is too expensive, inaccessible, or difficult to arrange. No incumbent designer necessarily lost an order because the object otherwise would not have been made. Lower production costs can support new accessibility features, personalized services, small-market products, and research that was previously uneconomic.

There is an autonomy case too. Removing search, transcription, boilerplate drafting, scheduling, and repetitive reconciliation can leave more attention for judgment, care, relationships, craft, and consequential decisions. But routine does not always mean worthless. Drafting, research, documentation, and manual checking often train junior employees and expose unusual failures. Automating those tasks without replacing their learning function may improve this quarter’s throughput while weakening the future talent pipeline.

Leaders therefore need to ask what a task contributes beyond its immediate output. Some tasks should disappear. Others should become supervised practice, simulation, rotation, or progressively harder review work.

The most ambitious option is **working-time reduction**: fewer paid hours without a proportional loss of income. Saving 30 minutes inside a workflow does not produce that outcome. Schedules, workloads, staffing, and pay must change as well.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Taking work away can create output, autonomy, and leisure, provided organisations preserve the learning, judgment, responsibility, and income attached to good work.</p></div>

## The saved hour has several owners

Call the additional value or time created from the same inputs the **productivity dividend**. Once AI saves an hour, it can flow in several directions:

- Customers may receive more output, better service, or lower prices.
- Firms and owners may receive higher margins or capital returns.
- Workers may receive higher pay, profit sharing, additional leave, or shorter hours.
- Employers may redeploy a person, remove a vacancy, or make a position redundant.
- New demand may support a product, company, task, or occupation that was not previously viable.
- The public may receive tax revenue for services, transition income, or retraining.

Several destinations can coexist, but the same hour cannot be counted twice. A business case cannot promise both 30% more output and 30% more free capacity without explaining the split. The Danish evidence suggests that the immediate workplace default is often more output: most users who saved time redirected it to other work.

This distribution problem predates generative AI. A [2019 analysis across OECD economies](https://www.insee.fr/en/statistiques/fichier/4253019/510_511_512_Pak_Pionnier_Schwellnus_EN.pdf) found that real wage growth had separated from productivity growth in many countries as the **labour share**—the portion of economic value paid to workers rather than owners—declined. The study predates generative AI, but it explains why higher productivity is not shared prosperity by definition.

Ownership and bargaining matter. **Bargaining power** is the ability of workers or employers to influence pay, hours, and working conditions. If control of models, cloud infrastructure, valuable data, and intellectual property is concentrated while worker bargaining weakens, augmentation can arrive as higher quotas, tighter monitoring, reduced discretion, and responsibility for errors produced by systems employees do not control.

Nor is a new job automatically a good replacement. New work may pay less, offer weaker security, or remove paths to advancement. It may arrive too slowly, require skills displaced workers have had no chance to acquire, or emerge in regions they cannot reasonably move to. Women, clerical workers, younger employees, contractors, and communities dependent on a few employers can face different risks even when national employment looks stable.

A redundancy creates unpaid time, not leisure. Displacement becomes a gain only when lower labour requirements remain connected to purchasing power, dignity, agency, and a fair claim on the resulting value.

## History offers mechanisms, not a promise

On 27 June 1967, Barclays, a British bank, [unveiled a cash dispenser](https://home.barclays/news/2017/06/from-the-archives-the-atm-is-50/) at its Enfield branch in north London. The early automated teller machine performed cash services previously handled by people, making teller elimination seem like the obvious result.

The first phase was more complicated. Economist and legal scholar James Bessen found that [average teller staffing in urban US branches fell from 20 to 13 between 1988 and 2004](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2015/03/bessen.htm), while the number of urban branches rose by 43%. Lower staffing costs made more branches viable, and teller work shifted towards customer relationships and sales.

That mechanism did not protect tellers forever. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics, the federal agency responsible for labour data, recorded about 347,400 teller positions in 2024 and [projects a 13% decline from 2024 to 2034](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/tellers.htm). The 2024 employment count is observed; the projected 2024–34 decline is a forecast.

Technology has also created new work. A [2024 study led by MIT labour economist David Autor](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389) found that roughly 60% of US employment in 2018 was in job titles absent from the 1940 Census occupational index. That is a 78-year comparison. A new title does not necessarily represent a wholly new occupation, a net new job, or work of comparable quality for the person displaced.

Earlier computerization also contributed to **occupational polarization**: growth at the high- and low-wage ends alongside contraction in routine middle-income work. Autor and economist David Dorn documented that US pattern in a [2013 labour-market study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15150). New work and weaker career ladders can coexist.

Other technologies caused direct local harm. Economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo found in [peer-reviewed research on US industrial robots](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285)—programmable machines used in manufacturing—that one additional robot per 1,000 workers reduced the local employment-to-population ratio by about 0.2 percentage points and wages by roughly 0.42%. Industrial robots are not generative AI, and US commuting zones are not Nordic labour markets. The result still shows how aggregate gains can coexist with regional losses.

The **China shock**, the concentrated rise in Chinese import competition after China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, was a trade event rather than automation. It nevertheless reveals how slowly labour markets can adjust. A [2021 longitudinal study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29401) found that adverse employment and income effects remained visible in exposed US regions through 2019. **Skill mismatch**—a gap between displaced workers’ capabilities and available jobs—can persist long after an economy finds new sources of growth.

Generative AI may follow none of these paths exactly. Software can spread across cognitive workflows faster and at lower additional cost than physical machinery. History identifies mechanisms worth planning for—demand expansion, new work, polarization, wage pressure, and persistent regional loss—but does not choose among them.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Past technologies expanded demand and created work while also causing polarization and lasting local losses. History supplies risks and mechanisms, not reassurance.</p></div>

## The Nordic advantage has to be negotiated

Nordic countries have institutions capable of shaping the transition, although those institutions provide no immunity. Nordics.info, a research platform hosted by Denmark’s Aarhus University, reports that [more than 80% of Nordic employees are covered by collective agreements](https://nordics.info/themes/nordic-labour-markets). **Collective bargaining** lets employers and worker representatives negotiate pay, hours, training, and working conditions. Denmark’s **flexicurity** model combines relatively flexible hiring and separation with income protection and active help finding new work. Sweden, Finland, Norway, Iceland, and Denmark use different arrangements.

Those institutions become an advantage only when they enter AI decisions before displacement. A serious programme should pass five tests:

1. **Map the role before discussing headcount.** Record its purpose, tasks, decisions, learning functions, and accountability. State whether the project is intended to augment employees or substitute for positions.

2. **Measure the complete workflow.** Track time, quality, checking, rework, incidents, customer outcomes, vacancies, internal mobility, workload, pay, and recorded hours. Break material effects down by occupation, age, gender, contract type, and location.

3. **Protect learning and responsibility.** Replace automated junior tasks with supervised practice, simulations, rotations, and progressively harder assignments. Keep named human authority where decisions affect people, safety, rights, or public trust.

4. **Include transition costs in the investment case.** Involve employees and unions before workforce decisions are fixed. Fund paid retraining, income support, redeployment, and regional or mobility assistance. A project has not created social value by moving its costs from the company ledger to workers and municipalities.

5. **Allocate verified gains before scaling.** Define **gain sharing** as a concrete division of productivity improvements through pay, employee ownership, bonuses, training budgets, additional leave, or shorter hours. Publish the allocation internally and revise it when measured results replace pilot assumptions.

Governments and social partners—public authorities, employer organisations, and unions—must address risks individual firms cannot. Their tools include portable training rights, adequate transition income, regional investment, competition policy, and **redistribution**, meaning taxes, transfers, social insurance, and public services that share costs and gains. **Active labour-market policy** adds training, placement, and mobility support intended to keep people connected to employment. Retraining without time and income security will exclude those least able to carry transition risk.

Will AI take our jobs? It is already taking parts of them. Some positions will disappear, many occupations will change, and current evidence cannot yet establish the eventual net effect on total employment.

Why could that be good? Producing what people value with less necessary labour can expand prosperity, autonomy, and time outside work. It becomes progress only when the people carrying the transition retain income, dignity, agency, learning, accountability, and a fair share of the productivity dividend.

The point is not to preserve every task. It is to reduce necessary labour without reducing the security and freedom of the people whose labour is no longer required.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Nordic leaders can turn AI-driven labour savings into progress by governing task design, measurement, learning, transitions, ownership, and gain sharing before displacement occurs.</p></div>
