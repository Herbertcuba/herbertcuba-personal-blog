Two propositions sit at the heart of an AI-first company. Every role acquires a technical dimension. And companies built around [AI agents](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf), software systems that use context and approved tools to carry work through several steps, can move more ownership toward the people closest to the work and operate with fewer coordination layers.

Arvind KC, [Chief People Officer at OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), the company behind the ChatGPT AI chatbot, put the first proposition plainly in a [DECODE business interview published on 31 May 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ): “Every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately added that the technical role is applied to a particular domain. KC also described innovation at OpenAI as bottom-up, which connects the two propositions: people need enough technical understanding and ownership to improve the work where it happens.

That connection creates a real organizational possibility, but it does not prove that managers can simply be removed. A company becomes defensibly flatter only when agents eliminate meaningful execution and coordination across complete workflows, while decision rights, controls, and the human transition become more explicit than they were before.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Distributed technical judgment and agent-supported work can support a flatter company, but the workflow must prove the case and authority must remain visible.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment moves to the edge

Technical judgment is the business ability to decide what an AI system can handle reliably, which information it needs, how failure will show up, and when a person must decide. It does not mean that everyone becomes a programmer. It means that domain expertise now includes a practical understanding of the technology acting inside the domain.

A salesperson needs to know when an account recommendation rests on thin evidence. Finance needs to distinguish a plausible explanation from an approved accounting decision. Marketing has to recognize where automated personalization crosses a policy boundary, while support teams need to know when confidence is too low for an automated resolution. These are business decisions with a technical dimension.

Once that judgment exists inside functions, improvement no longer has to wait for a central AI team to translate every problem. To leaders accustomed to approvals moving upward, the result may look disorderly. A domain map makes the edge accountable by showing which team owns the outcome, which decisions it may make, and where an exception returns to a named person.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to govern AI in its own work, while domain ownership keeps bottom-up action accountable.</p>
</div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the old company

Benjamin Simkin's 2026 business book, [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls the mistake of adding AI to an old operating structure the “Retrofit Trap.” The term is useful because a faster task can leave the surrounding organization untouched.

Consider a commercial contract renewal. An agent may compare the current agreement with proposed terms and draft a recommendation in minutes. Yet the case can still move through email for discount approval, wait for legal review, lose its current version between systems, and require someone to repair customer, contract, and billing records at the end. The analysis improved, but the company still pays for routing, waiting, checking, and repair.

This reveals part of what hierarchy does. Management layers provide judgment, coaching, and accountability, but they also carry information upward, return approvals downward, and direct exceptions to the right place. An agent-supported workflow can absorb record gathering, completeness checks, routine routing, state updates, and draft recommendations. Human authority remains necessary for policy exceptions, consequential commitments, contested judgment, and responsibility for the outcome.

The architectural question is therefore precise: has the new workflow removed enough coordination work to change the purpose of a layer, or has it merely made one task faster inside the old structure?

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Retrofit Trap appears when AI accelerates a task but leaves manual approvals, routing, record repair, and organizational waiting in place.</p>
</div>

## A workflow has to earn a flatter structure

The useful unit of evidence is a correctly closed case. For a contract renewal, that means the approved terms are signed, the customer and billing records agree, required obligations are scheduled, every exception is resolved, and the audit trail is complete. A fast draft that leaves people to reconcile the case later is not a closed-workflow gain.

Before testing structural change, I would ask the executive team to approve an illustrative decision record like the one below. The bracketed fields are deliberately local. They are not published benchmarks, and they should be fixed before results are known.

| Decision field | Locally replaceable entry |
| --- | --- |
| Scope and owner | [Workflow, business unit, country, accountable executive] |
| Representative case mix | [Routine, exception-heavy, and high-consequence cases] |
| Baseline and comparator | [Historical period or concurrent process used for comparison] |
| Review window and volume | [Start date, end date, and enough cases to expose the chosen mix] |
| Full cost boundary | [Budget ceiling covering models, integration, controls, people, and incident work] |
| Outcome measures | [Correct closure, quality, elapsed time, and downstream accuracy] |
| Coordination measures | [Human routing touches, waiting, repair effort, and residual judgment] |
| Control limits | [Locally approved tolerances and automatic stop conditions] |
| Decision rule | [Acceptance criteria, treatment of uncertain results, scale-or-stop date] |

This record makes the claim testable, including the possibility that the redesign is wrong. Results should be broken out by case type because a strong average can hide failures in rare but consequential work. The comparison also needs total cost and human repair; otherwise the company may celebrate automation while quietly moving effort into exception queues.

The available evidence for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer. That absence should stay visible in a Nordic board discussion. It means each company has to predeclare its own acceptance rule, abandon the design when the rule is missed, and resist borrowing certainty from an unrelated demonstration.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Structural change needs predeclared local evidence from correctly closed cases, including coordination, repair, controls, total cost, and the judgment that still belongs to people.</p>
</div>

## Authority needs an address

A flatter organization cannot rely on vague empowerment. Each business domain needs a named owner for the outcome, explicit decisions that teams and agents may make, and a short path for exceptions. The agent can interpret evidence and propose an action within those boundaries; ordinary software should enforce permissions, required approvals, and the official record of the case.

That official record is the authoritative state: the trusted answer to where a case stands now. In the renewal example, software may allow a contract to move from drafted to reviewed, approved, signed, and activated. It should reject a jump from drafted to activated, and activation should be impossible without a valid signature and approved commercial terms.

Retries need a similar rule. The established engineering term is *idempotency*: repeating a request has the same intended effect as making it once. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods), the 2022 standard for how web requests behave, defines that property. Applied here, it means a repeated activation request cannot create a duplicate order or duplicate downstream obligation.

These controls are established software practice rather than special powers granted to a model. [NIST Special Publication 800-53 Revision 5](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/53/r5/upd1/final), a 2020 United States government catalog of security and privacy controls, includes controls for enforcing approved logical access and recording audit information. An AI-first architecture still needs those foundations, plus an auditable override, a named person who can stop the workflow, and rollback or reconciliation procedures that return systems to a safe state or repair effects that cannot simply be undone.

Ownership should be visible before production handover:

| Role | Non-delegable responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Executive sponsor | Business outcome, test authorization, funding, and final structural proposal |
| Domain and workflow owner | Correct closure, policy, case quality, and exception ownership |
| Production service owner | Reliability, monitoring, incidents, support, and ongoing run budget |
| Security, privacy, risk, and procurement owners | Boundaries, supplier terms, control approval, and unresolved risks |
| Workforce lead and employee representatives | Role design, consultation, learning, redeployment, and transition terms |
| Stop authority and review forum | Suspension, evidence for restart, rollback, scale, or abandonment |

The sequence matters. The sponsor authorizes the test; domain and control owners approve its boundaries; employee representatives shape role consequences; the service owner accepts the production duty; and only the named review forum can restart a stopped workflow. Distributed ownership works because the points of control are clearer, not because control has disappeared.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Agents may interpret and propose, while explicit software rules enforce permissions and state and named people own exceptions, stopping, recovery, production service, and restart.</p>
</div>

## The old pyramid was also a career system

Coordination-heavy roles will change if routing, status collection, follow-up, and routine exception handling shrink. Some roles can move toward domain judgment, coaching, risk ownership, or service stewardship. Others may lose enough of their former purpose that the company has to redesign or remove them. Calling all of this “empowerment” would hide the cost.

The old pyramid also supplied promotion steps, pay progression, apprenticeship, visibility, and a place for ambitious people to aim. If a company removes layers without replacing those functions, it can become flatter and weaker at the same time. Senior experts need credible advancement without taking on artificial spans of control, and new employees need named coaches, protected learning time, and work that develops judgment rather than leaving them to supervise agent output they do not yet understand.

A responsible transition therefore belongs in the authorization pack. It should fund paid learning, define expert and people-leadership paths, explain how pay changes with responsibility, involve employee representatives early, and state the options for redeployment. Where a viable role does not remain, the company owes people a fair and explicit exit process. HR cannot carry this alone because the CIO and business owners are choosing the architecture that changes the work.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Flattening rewrites careers as well as workflows, so learning, coaching, progression, pay, consultation, redeployment, and fair exits must be funded parts of the design.</p>
</div>

## What the CIO is authorizing

The board question is not whether AI will eventually make organizations flatter. It is whether a particular workflow has produced enough evidence to redistribute work and authority now, within a transition the company is prepared to fund.

I would take one authorization pack into that discussion. It should contain the predeclared workflow test and its results, the residual decisions reserved for people, the domain and production owners, the enforced permission and state model, the exception and audit path, the stop and restart authority, and the costed workforce plan. Privacy, security, risk, procurement, finance, HR, and employee representatives should record unresolved objections rather than letting them dissolve into a general steering-committee approval.

If the workflow closes the chosen case mix more reliably, materially reduces routing and repair under the local acceptance rule, stays inside its control and cost boundaries, and leaves residual judgment with accountable people, then a coordination layer may genuinely have less work to do. If those conditions are absent, changing the hierarchy would turn a technology hypothesis into an organizational fact before the company has earned it.

An AI-first company can distribute technical judgment and become materially flatter. The defensible architecture appears only when workflow evidence, visible authority, production ownership, and the employment bargain all support the same decision.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A CIO should approve hierarchy change only when one authorization pack proves the workflow, names every consequential owner, funds production and workforce transition, and defines when to stop.</p>
</div>
