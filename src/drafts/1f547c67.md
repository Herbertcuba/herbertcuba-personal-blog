Two claims sit at the center of an AI-first company. Every business role acquires a technical dimension, and organizations can become flatter as ownership moves closer to the people who understand the work. Technical judgment here means knowing what an AI system can handle, how to test it, and when a person must take over. Bottom-up ownership means giving those domain experts more authority to improve the work rather than making them wait for a central team.

The claims are linked, but the second does not follow automatically from the first. An AI agent, meaning a system that can interpret a request, use approved tools, and act across several steps, may remove routine execution and coordination. It may also add reviews, exceptions, and hidden dependencies. I think a responsible CIO should treat a flatter company as a possible operating result, not as the starting objective.

That changes the executive question. The issue is whether evidence from a complete workflow, from an incoming request to a correct and controlled close, supports a new division of work. Only then should leaders redraw decision rights, which specify who may decide what and within which limits, or remove a layer from the organization.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI-first design joins distributed technical judgment with the possibility of a flatter company, but workflow evidence must prove that agents have removed coordination rather than moved it elsewhere.</p>

## Technical judgment belongs in the business

In a recorded discussion about OpenAI, an AI research and product company, people leader Arvind KC said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” His next point supplied the important context: the technical dimension is applied to a domain. A finance professional does not need to become a software engineer, but does need to judge which reconciliations an AI system can prepare reliably, what evidence should be checked, and which discrepancy requires human review.

The same logic applies across the company. A salesperson must know when AI-assisted account research is dependable enough to guide preparation, while customer commitments remain a human responsibility. A support specialist needs to understand which requests can move through an automated path and which signs point to fraud, hardship, or an unusual customer situation. KC connected this distributed capability to bottom-up innovation: people close to the work can shape useful applications instead of waiting for a central AI team to define every one.

OpenAI is an unusual company, so its operating pattern is not evidence that a bank, manufacturer, or public agency can copy its structure. There is, however, broader evidence for the judgment problem. An experiment involving 758 management consultants found that access to GPT-4, a large language model released by OpenAI in 2023, improved performance on tasks inside the model’s “capability frontier” but reduced correctness on a task outside it. That frontier is the uneven boundary between work the model handles well and similar-looking work it cannot complete reliably. Business experts have to learn where that boundary sits in their own work.

This is also where Benjamin Simkin’s Retrofit Trap matters. Simkin, author of *The AI First Company*, uses the term for adding AI to inherited processes while leaving the surrounding operating model intact. Imagine an agent drafting a response to a refund request while an employee still finds the order, checks policy, seeks approval, updates the case, and repairs duplicates. The drafting step is faster, yet the business still pays for the old route around it.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Technical judgment combines domain expertise with an understanding of AI reliability; it does not require everyone to code. That capability creates value only when the company redesigns the surrounding workflow instead of accelerating one isolated task.</p>
</div>

## Flattening has to be earned by the workflow

One practical way to read a hierarchy is as an information, approval, and exception-routing system. Managers also set direction, develop people, and carry accountability, so reducing their role to routing would be a serious mistake. Still, many layers exist partly because someone must collect status, reconcile conflicting inputs, approve standard deviations, and send unusual cases to the right owner.

An agent-supported workflow can absorb some of that coordination. In the refund example, an agent may gather the order history, compare the request with policy, propose an amount, update the customer record, and send only a genuine exception to an authorized person. If it merely produces a summary that several people must inspect and pass along, the coordination load has not disappeared.

The evidence therefore belongs at the level of correctly closed cases, not impressive demonstrations. I would ask how many cases reached the right outcome, how many human routing touches remained, how much repair work followed, and whether quality or control failures increased. Total cost must include model use, integration, monitoring, and exception handling. The final measure is residual judgment: the decisions that still require a person because the context is novel, the trade-off is material, or accountability cannot be delegated.

These measures form a local operating test, not an established benchmark. I have no verified Swedish or Norwegian production case that supports a universal numerical threshold for removing a management layer. Each company should define its own quality and risk conditions before a pilot, then observe the workflow under sustained real demand. Flattening becomes defensible only when routine coordination falls without a hidden rise in repair, risk, or cost.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A management layer should change only after complete-workflow evidence shows fewer routing touches and less routine coordination, while correctly closed cases, control performance, total cost, and human repair remain acceptable.</p>
</div>

## Govern the edges instead of hiding the hierarchy

A flatter company still needs architecture. Start with domains: bounded areas of business responsibility such as customer refunds, supplier onboarding, or credit review. Each domain needs a named owner for the outcome and policy, a clear set of decisions the agent may take, and an identified person who owns exceptions. This allows authority to move toward the edge without making ownership vague.

The control boundary should then be enforced by ordinary rule-based software rather than left to free-form model reasoning. The agent can interpret a customer’s message and propose an allowed action. Software should enforce access permissions, the refund ceiling, and the current case state, meaning the recorded status and valid next steps. A retry should be idempotent, a technical term meaning that receiving the same instruction twice cannot produce a second refund.

The same design must make intervention visible. An audit trail should show which person or system did what, using which evidence and rule. An override is an authorized human decision that replaces the proposed action. Stop authority names who can pause the workflow, while rollback provides a tested route to restore the last valid state or reverse an action safely. Agents can work within these boundaries; they should not invent the boundaries while a case is moving.

This structure may look less orderly than a chain in which every decision travels upward. In practice, the domain map reveals where authority already sits and makes its limits inspectable. The CIO owns the shared control architecture, domain leaders own business outcomes, and accountable humans retain the decisions whose consequences require judgment. That is how bottom-up ownership becomes governed rather than concealed.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The replacement for coordination-heavy hierarchy is visible domain ownership: agents interpret and act within limits, ordinary software enforces permissions and state, and accountable people retain exceptions, overrides, stop authority, and rollback.</p>
</div>

The useful mapping concerns hidden authority rather than hostile machines. An enterprise workflow can appear neutral while its instructions quietly decide whose interests matter, which exceptions are ignored, and who is allowed to stop the process. If those choices cannot be inspected and challenged, the formal org chart says little about where power actually resides.

## A flatter company needs a new people contract

When routine routing falls, coordination-heavy roles will change. Some work may shrink, while other work shifts toward domain judgment, exception ownership, coaching, and control. Leaders should be honest about that uncertainty. It is not credible to announce empowerment while leaving employees to discover later that progression, status, or pay still depends on the number of people they manage.

The old pyramid also carried a career system. A flatter model needs respected specialist and domain-owner paths with explicit expectations, pay progression, and access to senior decisions. Coaching needs an owner and protected time if fewer managers remain. Otherwise the organization removes a layer that developed people and assumes that learning will somehow continue on its own.

A credible transition begins before role removal. It identifies which tasks are changing, how people will be selected for redesigned roles, what learning time and support they receive, and how pay and progression will work. It also provides a route to challenge decisions and clear terms for people whose work cannot be redeployed. For a Nordic CIO, I would place employee representatives and applicable consultation requirements inside the design process from the start, rather than treating them as communication after the decision.

This will not make the transition painless. It does make the trade-offs discussable and gives the company a chance to preserve valuable domain knowledge while changing how that knowledge is used. The workforce plan is part of the operating architecture because the new model depends on human judgment at precisely the points where routine processing ends.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Flattening changes careers as well as workflows. Leaders must redesign progression, pay, coaching, learning, consultation, and transition support so that domain judgment gains a real path rather than becoming extra responsibility without status or security.</p>
</div>

## The org chart is the last decision

Before taking a redesign to the board, a CIO should be able to answer five questions. Has the agent completed a meaningful workflow under real operating conditions? Which routing and approval work has actually disappeared? Who owns policy, permissions, exceptions, overrides, stop authority, and audit? What technical and operational complexity has been added? What commitment is the company making to people whose work and prospects will change?

The answers should be specific enough to survive a failure. The board should know which signals pause the workflow, how the organization recovers, who is accountable for customer or employee harm, and whether the projected economics include repair and control costs. It should also see the workforce transition beside the technology case, because delaying the people decision does not remove it.

The original architectural possibility remains real. Technical judgment can spread through the business, agents can absorb meaningful execution and coordination, and authority can move closer to the domain. A flatter design becomes defensible when operating proof, control ownership, and the workforce commitment all point in the same direction. The CIO’s job is to make that case before the chart moves.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Take an organizational redesign to the board only with complete-workflow proof, named control owners, understood complexity, tested recovery paths, and a funded people transition. The org chart should record a change already proven in the work.</p>
</div>
