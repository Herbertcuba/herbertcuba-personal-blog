Two claims sit at the center of an AI-first company. Every business role is acquiring a technical dimension. And when AI agents absorb enough execution and coordination, decisions can move closer to the work and the company can operate with fewer layers.

An AI agent is software that uses a language model to interpret a goal and take bounded steps across tools. Its arrival makes the first claim increasingly practical. The second remains a hypothesis about organizational design, not a promised consequence of buying the technology.

At a May 2026 founders dinner in San Francisco, [Arvind KC, chief people officer at the AI company OpenAI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s), said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately qualified the point: technical work is “applied to a particular domain.” In the same recorded conversation, he said innovation should come “bottom up.” That is a leadership observation at one company, not proof that agents let a mature enterprise remove management layers safely.

My argument is conditional. An AI-first company can become materially flatter, but only after agents remove coordination across complete workflows, authority becomes more explicit, and the transition for affected people is treated as part of the operating model. The org chart comes after that proof.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Distributed technical judgment is already becoming a business requirement. A flatter organization is the possible result of proven coordination removal, not the starting assumption.</p></div>

## Technical judgment belongs inside every function

Technical judgment means knowing what an AI-enabled system can do reliably, what evidence it needs, how it can fail, and when a person must decide. It does not mean that every salesperson, controller, marketer, or operations lead must learn to code.

A sales leader needs to know whether an agent can prepare an account brief without inventing customer facts. A finance owner must decide which discrepancies can follow a standard rule and which require interpretation. An operations team has to identify the official state of a case and the conditions that allow it to move forward. These are domain decisions with technical consequences.

That knowledge cannot live only in a central AI team. The central team can provide shared platforms, security controls, and evaluation methods, but people inside each function understand the exceptions that carry commercial or human weight. Bottom-up ownership therefore means that domain teams can propose, test, and improve workflows within common boundaries. It does not grant them permission to deploy ungoverned agents.

This changes the role of management as well. A manager who once translated information between specialists and executives may spend less time carrying updates and more time setting boundaries, developing judgment, and owning difficult exceptions. Whether that shift removes a layer depends on what happens to the surrounding workflow.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first work distributes technical judgment without turning everyone into a programmer. Domain teams own the meaning and exceptions of their work, while shared controls keep local initiative inside enterprise limits.</p></div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the hierarchy

Benjamin Simkin, an entrepreneur and author, calls the failure to redesign work around AI the “Retrofit Trap” in his 2026 business book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5). The term describes an organization that adds AI to an old process while leaving the process itself intact.

Consider supplier onboarding. An agent may read an application and prepare a risk summary in seconds. If employees still copy its output into another system, email three control functions, chase two approvals, resolve duplicate records, and ask a manager where the exception belongs, one task became faster while the coordination system stayed the same.

A hierarchy does more than supervise people. It also moves information, grants approvals, resolves conflicting priorities, and routes exceptions. Agent-supported workflows may absorb some of that work by collecting evidence, maintaining the case record, requesting missing information, and sending a valid case to the right decision owner. Human authority remains necessary where the company accepts risk, makes a policy exception, resolves a contested judgment, or is accountable for a consequential outcome.

The useful unit of analysis is therefore the complete workflow: the path from a valid request to an accepted outcome, including exceptions, repairs, and handoffs. If an agent creates a new review queue or pushes repair work into another function, the hierarchy may look less busy without becoming less necessary.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up an isolated task while preserving manual routing, approval, and repair. Structural change becomes plausible only when an agent-supported system removes coordination across the complete workflow.</p></div>

## A flatter structure has to earn its evidence

The source set assembled for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a transferable numerical threshold for removing a management layer because of agents. A board should not borrow a number from an unrelated process or market. It needs a local baseline and a reversible trial.

The main outcome should be a correctly closed case: a case that reaches the intended business result, passes its quality and control checks, leaves a complete audit trail, and has no unresolved side effect. Measure it across the actual case mix, including awkward exceptions. Then compare elapsed time, human routing touches, repair effort, control failures, residual judgment, and total cost, including integration, model use, monitoring, and review.

A compact trial charter can put those commitments on one page. Its fields are proposed decision inputs, not universal benchmarks:

> **AI-first workflow trial: decision record**
>
> - **Workflow and case boundary:** ____________________
> - **Baseline period and case mix:** ____________________
> - **Allowed actions and operating limits:** ____________________
> - **Decision rights and stop authority:** ____________________
> - **Evidence sources and record owner:** ____________________
> - **Domain, control, technology, and workforce owners:** ____________________
> - **Review cadence and decision date:** ____________________
> - **Local acceptance conditions:** ____________________
> - **Reversal path:** ____________________

Stop authority means the named role with power to suspend automated action when evidence, control, or service quality falls outside the agreed boundary. The trial should also preserve failures rather than quietly excluding them from the result. At the decision date, the accountable executives should sign one of four choices: retain the current layer, extend the trial, redesign the workflow, or remove the layer and reassign its remaining duties.

This makes the organizational claim falsifiable. If correctly closed cases improve only because people absorb more hidden repair, the trial has not justified a flatter structure. If routine routing falls materially within locally agreed limits while quality, control, and total cost remain acceptable, the leadership team has evidence for redesign rather than enthusiasm for a tool.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Test complete workflows against a local baseline and preserve the exceptions. A management layer should move only when correctly closed cases show that coordination disappeared rather than shifting into hidden repair or new risk.</p></div>

## The replacement architecture makes authority visible

Removing a coordination layer does not remove its decisions. The replacement architecture must assign each one to a domain owner, a control, or an explicitly named exception owner. Decision rights are the documented answers to who may decide, within which limits, using what evidence, and who can override or stop the action.

The agent should interpret unstructured material and propose the next step. Ordinary software should enforce permission and state through repeatable rules. Before any action, that control layer verifies the person or service making the request and whose authority it is using. It then checks the permitted resource and action, current limits, expiry, revocation, and the official case record, also called the authoritative state.

This separation matters because language-model input is not trustworthy authority. [NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf), the US National Institute of Standards and Technology’s July 2024 risk profile for generative AI, describes indirect prompt injection: an attacker places instructions in data that an AI application may later retrieve. A supplier document telling an agent to ignore an approval rule must remain document text. It cannot become permission.

Every consequential step should leave an audit record linking the case version, evidence, policy, proposal, approval when required, attempted action, and observed result. Repeated requests also need protection. [Request for Comments 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods), the 2022 standard governing HTTP web requests, defines an idempotent request as one where multiple identical requests have the same intended effect as one. In business workflows, a unique action key can help prevent an agent from submitting the same instruction twice.

That key does not solve every failure. A connection can break after an external system accepts an action but before the company records the response. The workflow must reconcile with that external system before retrying, and route uncertain outcomes to a named person. Some effects cannot be rolled back, so the design also needs a corrective action, such as cancellation, recall, or an explicit follow-up, rather than a fictional promise of perfect reversibility.

Once these controls are visible, bottom-up initiative and enterprise governance can reinforce each other. People at the edge can improve how work is done because the limits, records, exception paths, and stop authority no longer depend on an informal chain of managers.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents may interpret and propose, while ordinary software enforces identity, delegated authority, limits, and current case state. Named humans retain exception, stop, and corrective authority, with an audit trail connecting every consequential action to its evidence.</p></div>

The mapping is organizational: an agent can appear empowered while an unseen instruction, inaccessible policy, or supplier-controlled restriction determines what it may actually do. An AI-first company needs the opposite design, with ownership and limits exposed before the system acts.

## Redesign the career system with the workflow

A flatter company changes more than reporting lines. Coordination-heavy roles may lose work built around collecting updates, chasing responses, checking standard cases, and translating information between levels. Some people can move toward domain ownership, coaching, exception judgment, or workflow design. Others may find that the organization needs less of their current role.

Leadership has to name that consequence before presenting flattening as empowerment. A credible transition covers which roles are changing, how people can participate in the design, what paid learning and redeployment are available, and how pay, status, and progression work on expert paths. It must also state what support applies when no suitable role remains. If management headcount is no longer the main route to advancement, senior craft, domain, and control roles need real authority and compensation.

Participation is also part of the operating process in a Nordic context. In Sweden, [section 11 of the 1976 Co-Determination Act](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-1976580-om-medbestammande-i_sfs-1976-580/), formally *lag (1976:580) om medbestämmande i arbetslivet*, requires an employer bound by a collective agreement to negotiate on its own initiative with the employee organization before deciding on an important change to its activities or to the working or employment conditions of employees who belong to that organization. For an AI-first redesign, “before deciding” has practical force: the employer should convene the process when the workflow hypothesis and exposed roles are known, while scope, sequencing, learning, redeployment, and reporting lines can still change.

The CIO’s recommendation to the executive team should therefore bind three records together: the workflow evidence, the authority model, and the workforce commitment. If one is missing, keep the structure in place and continue the design work. If all three support the same conclusion, removing a coordination layer becomes a defensible operating decision rather than a leap from a successful demo.

A thinner org chart is not the objective by itself. The objective is an organization where technical judgment sits closer to the work, routine coordination is carried by governed systems, and human authority is clearer than it was before. That is the architecture worth approving.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Organizational redesign is defensible only when workflow proof, visible authority, and a funded people transition agree. Career paths, employee participation, learning, redeployment, and remaining human accountability belong in the decision before reporting lines change.</p></div>
