An AI-first company changes where judgment and coordination live. Every role acquires a technical dimension, while AI agents can move routine execution and ownership closer to the people who understand the business problem. Together, those shifts create the possibility of a flatter organization. They do not, by themselves, justify one.

An AI agent is software that uses a generative AI model to interpret information, choose steps and use approved tools toward an outcome. In a June 2026 [DECODE conversation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ), [Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), Chief People Officer at OpenAI, the AI research and product company behind ChatGPT, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He explained that the technology is applied within a particular domain and connected this broader shift with innovation rising from inside the organization.

That gives a CIO two decisions with different evidence requirements. Distributed technical judgment is a capability worth building now. A flatter hierarchy is a later structural choice, defensible only when agents have removed enough execution and coordination work to change what the organization actually needs from management.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first architecture begins with distributed technical judgment and more ownership at the edges. Flattening is a separate decision that must follow evidence, governance and a credible workforce plan.</p></div>

## The first redesign happens inside the role

Technical judgment means being able to frame a domain problem for AI, judge what the system can handle reliably, test its output and recognize when a human decision is still required. It does not mean that every salesperson, controller or support specialist must learn to code.

The judgment changes with the function. Sales needs to know which account data may support an offer and when an inferred need is too uncertain to use. Operations needs to distinguish a routine case from an exception with wider consequences. Finance must protect approval and accounting controls; marketing must judge source, rights and brand risk; support must know when empathy, discretion or an appeal belongs with a person.

A central AI team can provide approved models, security controls and shared tools, but it cannot own the meaning of every business decision. That knowledge sits in the domains. KC’s bottom-up point matters here: people close to the work need room to propose uses, define failure conditions and improve the workflow they operate.

From the center, this can look untidy because more decisions are made at the edges. Visible boundaries and accountability turn that apparent disorder into controlled decentralization. Pulling every use case back into a central AI office would remove the domain ownership that makes the system useful in the first place.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The technical dimension of a role is practical judgment about AI inside a business domain, not a coding requirement. Shared platforms should support that judgment while use-case ownership remains close to the work.</p></div>

## Use the org chart as a diagnostic

Business author Benjamin Simkin calls the habit of attaching AI to an old operating structure the [“Retrofit Trap” in *The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5). A task becomes faster, yet the approvals, handoffs and repair work around it remain. The company has improved a step without changing the way work moves.

Consider a hypothetical customer refund. An agent reads the message, checks the order and drafts a response. If an employee must then copy the facts into another system, ask a manager which policy applies, wait for finance approval, check whether the payment happened and repair duplicate attempts, the organization still carries the same coordination load. Faster drafting may be valuable, but it gives no reason to remove a management layer.

The org chart helps reveal why. In his 1974 paper [“Organization Design: An Information Processing View”](https://doi.org/10.1287/inte.4.3.28), organization theorist Jay R. Galbraith described hierarchy as a way to refer unusual situations upward to a level with a broader view. In practice, managers often collect information, resolve competing priorities, approve departures from policy and route exceptions when existing rules cannot settle the case.

An agent-supported workflow may absorb some of that coordination by gathering evidence, tracking status, applying an agreed routing rule and presenting a recommendation. Human authority remains necessary for setting policy, accepting material risk, hearing an appeal, resolving conflicts between domains and making employment decisions. Managers also coach, build capability and carry accountability. Those contributions cannot be treated as routing waste simply because routing is easier to automate.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up one activity while preserving the coordination system around it. A flatter structure becomes plausible only when complete workflows remove recurring information, approval and exception-routing demand without discarding human judgment.</p></div>

## Evidence must follow the whole case

The relevant unit of evidence is a correctly closed case: a business outcome completed to policy, recorded in the right systems and left without hidden repair work. For the refund, that means more than a good response. The customer receives the right decision, the payment and accounting records agree, no duplicate payment occurs, any exception is authorized, and the case can be audited later.

This distinction matters because task-level productivity can be real and still say little about organizational design. The 2023 [*Generative AI at Work*](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161), a working paper from the US National Bureau of Economic Research based on 5,179 customer-support agents, reported a 14% average productivity gain and a 34% gain among novice and lower-skilled workers. The authors explicitly did not claim that the study established overall employment or wage effects. It is useful evidence about supported work, but it cannot authorize the removal of a management layer.

A complete-workflow trial should compare similar cases before and after the change. It should measure elapsed time, human routing touches, review time, rework, customer or business quality, control failures, appeals and the residual decisions that still need human judgment. The economic view belongs in the same record: internal review and repair time, supplier fees, infrastructure, assurance work, transition and exit costs, and total cost per correctly closed case.

Pass and stop values must be local decisions set before the trial, not numbers chosen after seeing the result. The research available for this article does not establish a Swedish or Norwegian production threshold for safely removing management layers, so I am not offering one. The CIO needs a baseline, a defined set of cases, an independent check of outcomes and enough operating time to observe ordinary cases as well as exceptions.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Measure correctly closed cases across the complete workflow, including quality, human effort, failures and full cost. Task productivity is useful evidence, but it is not evidence that the hierarchy can safely shrink.</p></div>

## Authority needs a visible home

A flatter company still needs architecture. Start with a domain map: a clear record of the outcomes, data, policies and systems in each business area, together with their named owners. Then define decision rights, meaning who may decide, approve, override, stop and restart each part of the workflow.

The business owner sets the refund policy and acceptable outcome. The process owner carries performance and operating budget. Risk, legal and security owners set constraints and can halt the workflow when those constraints fail. A named operations leader owns exceptions and restart decisions. These responsibilities remain visible even when the agent performs most routine steps.

The technology should enforce the same separation. Ordinary deterministic software, which follows specified rules rather than generating a fresh interpretation, holds permissions and the authoritative state: the official record of where the case stands. The agent may interpret the customer’s request and propose a refund, but it does not grant itself access, rewrite policy or declare that money moved.

For example, the permitted states might be *received → assessed → approved → paid → closed*. A move straight from *assessed* to *paid* is rejected when approval is required. If the payment result is unknown, the case moves to reconciliation, where the system checks what happened before trying again.

That last control depends on idempotency, the property that repeating the same operation produces one business effect rather than two. A unique operation key is useful only when the receiving system binds it to the exact request and stored result, rejects the same key with changed details, and commits those records together. For an external payment, the payment service must offer equivalent protection or the workflow must record the intended payment and reconcile the outcome before retrying. Otherwise a stale approval check or an uncertain response can become a duplicate refund.

The boundary also has to treat customer messages and attached files as untrusted input. Prompt injection means instructions hidden in that content try to redirect the model away from its assigned task. The agent should receive only the data and tool permissions needed for the case, while software blocks unauthorized external actions and records every material proposal, approval, override and failure in an audit trail.

For European operations, this control record supports legal analysis rather than replacing it. The [EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj), is the European Union’s risk-based law for AI systems; the applicable duties depend on how a system is classified and used. The [General Data Protection Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2016/679](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj), governs the processing of personal data. Legal and privacy owners must map the actual workflow, data, human oversight and supplier responsibilities against both before production use.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Domain owners and decision rights make authority visible. Deterministic software should enforce permissions, case state and consequential actions, while agents interpret and propose within bounded access, auditable paths and human stop authority.</p></div>

The mapping is organizational: an AI recommendation becomes dangerous when uncertainty, exceptions and the right to challenge its conclusion are hidden. A governed AI-first company records the dissenting signal, names the person who can override the routine path and keeps consequential decisions open to review.

## The pyramid also carried a career system

When coordination-heavy work declines, the people consequence is immediate. Some management roles contain a large amount of status gathering, approval chasing and routine repair. They also contain coaching, conflict resolution, institutional memory, sponsorship and accountability. Leaders need to separate those forms of work before they conclude that a role has disappeared.

The old pyramid also supplied progression. Employees gained scope, pay and status by becoming managers; junior staff learned through observation and review; senior leaders had time allocated to coaching. If the company removes rungs without replacing those functions, it can create broader jobs with less support, fewer routes to higher pay and a thinner pool of future leaders. Bottom-up ownership without recognition or capacity becomes work intensification.

A credible transition therefore needs more than training. The Chief Human Resources Officer (CHRO), business leaders, legal counsel and employee representatives should define which work is changing, which roles remain, how people qualify for new roles, and what learning time and redeployment funding are available. Pay should reflect wider decision scope. Specialist and people-leadership paths should both offer progression, while coaching capacity needs an explicit owner and budget.

For Swedish operations, the 1976 [Co-Determination in the Workplace Act](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-1976580-om-medbestammande-i-arbetslivet_sfs-1976-580/) is the national framework for employer-union information and negotiation. Exact duties depend on the proposed change and jurisdiction, which is why participation should begin while options remain open. Where roles may disappear, the organization also needs clear selection, appeal, redeployment and exit terms before it treats workforce reduction as a benefit.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Flattening changes coaching, progression, pay and learning as well as reporting lines. Workforce participation, funded transition paths and replacement career structures are conditions of responsible redesign, not work to schedule after the decision.</p></div>

## Change the org chart last

The CIO’s board paper should separate three decisions. First, build distributed technical judgment through domain training, approved tools and clear accountability. Second, authorize a bounded complete-workflow trial with a named business owner, risk owners, employee participation, a durable budget and local pass and stop values. Third, consider structural change only after the evidence shows that recurring coordination demand has actually fallen.

The authorization should also survive beyond launch. Name the ongoing owner, the capacity available for review and repair, the spend categories, and a review cadence suited to the workflow’s risk. Each review should end with an explicit decision to scale, extend, change or stop the trial. Supplier exit, rollback, restart authority and preservation of business records belong in the operating plan from the beginning.

Before approving a new reporting structure, the executive team should be able to answer five questions:

1. Which complete workflow changed, and which recurring coordination work disappeared?
2. What quality, control, human-effort and cost evidence supports that conclusion?
3. Which decisions remain human, and who can approve, override, stop, restart and roll back the system?
4. How do regulation, privacy, security, suppliers and independent assurance constrain the design?
5. Which roles and career paths change, and which transition commitments have been funded?

I would approve the capability before the structure and treat the org chart as the last artifact to change. If complete-workflow evidence shows that agents have removed routine execution and coordination while the company preserves control, judgment and human development, a flatter organization becomes a defensible choice. If that evidence does not appear, the company can still keep the stronger technical judgment and better workflows without forcing an organizational conclusion the trial did not earn.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Build domain capability, run a governed workflow trial and revisit hierarchy only after sustained evidence and funded people commitments. The board should approve a structural change because coordination demand has demonstrably fallen, not because an AI pilot looked impressive.</p></div>
