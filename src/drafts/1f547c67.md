Count the human coordination that remains after an AI agent finishes. That reveals more about organizational change than the quality of a single output. A company can automate drafting or analysis and still preserve every queue, approval, and unclear handoff around the result.

By AI-first, I mean arranging work around capabilities that artificial intelligence can demonstrate reliably, with people positioned where judgment is still necessary. OpenAI, the company behind the generative AI assistant ChatGPT, describes one implementation in its [Agents SDK](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/), a software toolkit for developers: an agent combines a language model, the software that interprets and generates language, with instructions and approved tools. It then works through several steps until the task finishes or a person must intervene. Organization design enters the picture when the system can move a complete case forward while preserving its context.

I think the case for a flatter company is therefore conditional. A layer can become less useful when agents remove the routine coordination that supported it, while people close to the work gain enough technical judgment and authority to handle what remains. Yet routing is only one job that management has carried. Authority may move into software permissions, and career development may lose the ladder that once made it visible.

The cited public material does not show that companies have already removed management layers safely at scale. That missing operating evidence is the empirical frontier. Until real deployments publish baseline and trial data for routing, repair, control failures, and full cost, the coordination test remains a falsifiable proposal rather than proof that the org chart should change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Measure the human routing and repair left around completed work. A flatter structure becomes credible only when that burden falls, while authority, management responsibilities, and career development receive an explicit new home.</p></div>

## Begin with a case that can close

In his independently published May 2026 book, *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, author Benjamin Simkin calls it the “Retrofit Trap” when a company adds AI to a process designed for manual work but leaves the surrounding structure intact. The tool becomes faster while people still move information between systems, seek approval, repair mistakes, and record the result. Activity changes, but the operating burden survives.

Consider one clearly bounded case: a customer refund request. An agent-supported workflow could retrieve the order and policy, check whether the request falls inside an approved limit, request missing evidence, propose a permitted refund, update the account, and send a final explanation. A case outside policy would move to a named owner with the relevant records attached, then wait for that person’s decision.

The stages matter because they show whether the system has carried the work. A request might move from received to checked, then either to approved or to exception review, and finally to paid and closed. The language model can interpret the request and propose the next action, while ordinary software validates the permission and the allowed change of state. It should reject an attempt to move an exception directly to paid, and repeating the same request should never create a second payment.

The case record, rather than the model’s conversation, must remain the authoritative version of what has happened. The model can propose an action such as approving a refund, but ordinary software should check the current state and permission, then commit the payment and state change together. Each command needs a unique request key so the software can recognize a retry instead of issuing a second payment. If the stored state has changed since the model read it, the command should stop and the workflow should read the case again.

That control boundary predates generative AI. The [process-engine documentation from Camunda](https://docs.camunda.org/manual/latest/user-guide/process-engine/process-engine-concepts/), a business-process automation platform, explains how conventional workflow software stores the state of a case and follows a defined process. A language model adds flexible interpretation inside those controls; it does not make the controls unnecessary.

Now the structural test becomes clearer. If employees still search for records, chase the exception owner, correct most decisions, or close the case in another system, the agent has automated a task while the company still pays for coordination. The practical unit of measurement is a correctly closed case.

The measures should remain separate. Routing touches count handoffs, queue moves, and status chases. Repair time counts the human minutes spent correcting an invalid action or recovering a case, while judgment time records decisions that were defined in advance as requiring a person. Segment all three by risk or exception type so a simple average cannot hide where the workflow fails.

Managers also coach people, resolve conflict, allocate scarce resources, and develop judgment. Those functions do not vanish because a refund workflow improves. I think leaders should separate them from routine routing before making any claim about flattening, then ask which responsibilities still require a role and which existed mainly because information was fragmented.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Test a complete unit of work, including evidence, permissions, exceptions, payment, and closure. Track routing, repair, and genuine judgment separately per correctly closed case, and keep the management responsibilities that the workflow has not replaced.</p></div>

## Technical judgment has to travel with the work

Arvind KC is chief people officer at OpenAI. During a [May 2026 discussion with DECODE](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s), a community for founders, he said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” His point is broader than programming: people need enough understanding to judge the information a system receives, the actions it may take, the checks applied to its output, and the ways it can fail.

For the refund workflow, the customer operations owner needs to know which records count as evidence, which policy limits are fixed, and which cases require human judgment. Engineers remain responsible for reliable execution, access controls, and the tools the agent can use. Neither side can define a sound workflow alone. Business judgment without technical understanding creates vague rules, while technical implementation without business ownership turns policy into guesswork.

This shared judgment changes the role of a central technology team. It should provide dependable systems, common controls, and specialist help for hard problems. If every local exception still travels to engineering for interpretation, however, the company has rebuilt a coordination queue around the people who understand the technology.

Each resolved exception can improve the next cycle. A correction can become a test case, a policy can become clearer, and a repeated failure can trigger a tighter permission or a different route. I think this is where a durable edge can develop: the company finishes the work and leaves behind a workflow that is easier to inspect and improve.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical responsibility spreads when business decisions depend on what an AI system knows, what it may do, and how mistakes surface. Judgment should remain close to the work, while engineering supplies reliable foundations instead of becoming the new approval queue.</p></div>

## A flatter company needs a visible constitution

Removing a handoff redistributes power. Decision rights specify who may authorize an action, when an override is valid, who holds emergency stop authority, and who remains accountable for the outcome. In a conventional hierarchy, some of that power is visible in reporting lines. In an agent-supported workflow, it can disappear into permissions and thresholds unless leaders make it deliberate.

The owner of the business outcome should set the operating rule, and engineering should make its execution dependable. A model can propose an outcome, while ordinary software blocks payments above a set limit and other actions with high cost, legal consequence, or no easy reversal. Each fact that affects the decision needs a named system of record, meaning the source that people and software treat as definitive.

The audit record should reconstruct the action rather than pretend to reveal a model’s inner reason. It needs the model and system versions, the evidence provided, tool inputs and outputs, the authorization applied, state changes, retries, and any human override. That record allows an independent reviewer to replay what the system received and check whether the committed action followed the rule.

Law and supplier risk belong inside the same design. In the European Union, the [General Data Protection Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj), or GDPR, governs the processing of personal data. The [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) sets obligations according to how an AI system is used and the risk it creates. A legal owner must decide what applies to the actual workflow rather than assume that a vendor’s general compliance statement settles the matter.

The procurement questions are concrete. What risk category applies to this use? Which data can the supplier access, where is it handled, how long is it retained, and which subcontractors touch it? What evidence proves human oversight and rejected actions? Can the company withdraw access, export the records needed for review, and return to the previous process if the supplier fails? A tested exit route is part of control, not paperwork after the purchase.

Authority is only half of the human design. Coordination roles have also carried pay, status, exposure to difficult decisions, and a familiar route into leadership. If software removes routine routing, a company needs other ways for people to gain broader responsibility and show they are ready for it. Deeper specialist work, accountability for a larger or riskier business outcome, and a formal role in developing other people’s judgment can support progression, but only when titles, compensation, and funded learning recognize them.

I think this requires a decision forum rather than a quiet sequence of technology choices. Before a trial, the business sponsor, operations owner, engineering, HR, finance, and employee representatives where applicable should agree on the work boundary, stop authority, and likely effect on roles. The same group should review independently checked results and agree on changes to responsibility, learning, titles, or pay before the org chart moves.

I don’t know what share of people now paid to coordinate work will want, or be able, to take on deeper expertise or wider ownership. Some will grow into the new responsibilities, some will choose another path, and some jobs will lose their central purpose. Leadership becomes credible when it states those possibilities early and shares the cost of transition instead of presenting a smaller structure as neutral progress.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A flatter company needs visible decision rights, enforceable limits, replayable records, applicable legal and supplier controls, and a named person who can stop the workflow. It also needs funded and recognized routes to responsibility for people whose old career ladder depended on coordination.</p></div>

## Run the coordination test

Choose the first workflow for the quality of evidence it can produce, not for the polish of its demo. Rank candidates by case volume, reversibility, exception rate, data sensitivity, and whether a named business owner can answer for the outcome. A useful first trial has enough cases to expose variation, a clear start and finish, actions that can be reversed, exceptions common enough to test, data that can be controlled, and an owner with the authority to stop it.

The trial should also be difficult to reinterpret after the results arrive. Its brief needs to name the sponsor, exact workflow, current process used as the baseline, fixed time period or case count, and complete cost measure, including software, review, routing, repair, judgment, and oversight. It also needs an approver, an independent verifier, a decision forum, and a tested route back to the previous process.

No Swedish or Norwegian production case is cited here with before-and-after values for these measures. That absence matters. The scorecard below is an evidence contract to test and challenge, not a benchmark or a claim that flattening has already worked at scale. Each company must derive numerical thresholds from its operating baseline and from the consequences of getting the workflow wrong.

| Decision field | Lock before launch | Evidence that blocks flattening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Candidate fit | Case volume, reversibility, exception rate, data sensitivity, and named business owner | The workflow is too rare to test, too hard to reverse, or has no owner who can answer for the result |
| Workflow boundary | Start and end states, permitted actions, exception routes, and what counts as a correctly closed case | People still complete hidden steps outside the stated workflow |
| State and command contract | Authoritative case record, actions the agent may propose, permission and current-state checks, unique request key, and changes that must commit together | The model’s conversation acts as the record, or a stale or duplicate command can change the case |
| Routing | Human handoffs, queue moves, and status chases per correctly closed case, segmented by risk or exception type | Routing does not fall below the baseline-derived threshold |
| Repair | Human minutes spent correcting or recovering a case, kept separate from predeclared judgment | Repair stays flat, moves to hidden workers, or exceeds the threshold in a material risk class |
| Judgment | Human time spent on decisions defined in advance as requiring discretion | The trial relabels necessary judgment as waste or removes it to make the coordination number look better |
| Outcome and control | Time to correct closure, wrong-outcome limit by risk or exception type, and an immediate stop trigger | The quality limit is missed or a defined control breach occurs |
| Cost | Full cost per correctly closed case, including every software and human operating expense | The trial shifts cost into uncounted human work or misses its cost threshold |
| Authority and proof | Accountable owner, stop authority, independent verifier, and records needed to reconstruct actions | No one can identify who approved an action or verify the evidence, authorization, and state changes that preceded it |
| Reversibility | Rollback route, supplier exit, and restoration of access to the previous process | The company cannot stop safely or recover the workflow and its records |
| Structural decision | The responsibility or role that may change if repeated trials pass | Evidence supports faster tasks but does not show that a responsibility moved or disappeared |

Success thresholds should be numerical and agreed before launch. A stop trigger should follow the consequence of the workflow: a payment beyond the approved limit, access outside the stated permission, or another defined breach can end the trial immediately rather than becoming an average hidden inside a dashboard. This is also why one universal benchmark would be false precision. A low-risk refund and an irreversible employment decision cannot share the same tolerance for error.

Verification should examine the business outcome, not merely whether software marked a case closed. The verifier needs enough records to confirm that valid cases reached the right result, exceptions reached the right owner, prohibited actions were rejected, stale or repeated commands had no duplicate effect, and the final case record matched the committed action. The full cost per correctly closed case should include the invisible work that often makes a pilot look cheaper than production.

One successful trial still says little about an entire management layer. Leaders need to repeat the test across several workflows, watch whether repair returns under pressure, and separate routine coordination from coaching, resource allocation, conflict resolution, and judgment. I think the chart should change only when this evidence shows that a responsibility has moved or disappeared, and when the new authority and career system already works in practice.

My take is that coordination is the practical test for an AI-first company. Agents change the organization when they remove routine routing without burying authority or pushing repair onto invisible human hands. A flatter chart may follow, but completed work with accountable decisions is the proof.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Select a bounded and reversible workflow, then lock the baseline, separate measures, numerical thresholds, stop trigger, owner, verifier, full cost, and rollback route before launch. Change the chart only after repeated evidence shows that routine coordination is gone and its human responsibilities have a credible new home.</p></div>
