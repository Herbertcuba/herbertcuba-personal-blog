Many org charts are also maps of delay. They show where information stops, who translates it, which person approves it, and how the answer travels back to the work. That routing function is one reason companies built management pyramids in the first place.

AI agents matter because coordination is becoming a software capability. An [AI agent](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/) is software that gives an AI model instructions and access to approved tools, then allows it to continue across several steps until a task is complete or a person needs to intervene. It can produce an answer, but it can also remember where a case sits, update a record, apply a permission, and route an exception.

In a [2026 conversation hosted by the founder community DECODE](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s), Arvind KC, chief people officer at OpenAI, the AI company behind the generative AI assistant ChatGPT, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” His point was that technology has become a common part of work even when the area is people, finance, sales, or operations.

Benjamin Simkin describes the process side in his independently published May 2026 book, *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*. He uses “AI-first” for a company designed around what AI can actually do, and calls the easier alternative the “Retrofit Trap”: adding AI to work built for manual human operation while leaving the old process intact.

I think these ideas point to the same structural possibility. When agents absorb execution and routine coordination, people can concentrate on designing systems, judging exceptions, and developing others. The hierarchy may flatten as a result, but only if authority stays visible and the people who once carried the work between boxes have a credible place in what comes next.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Management pyramids carry information and decisions as well as people. Agents can take on part of that routing function, creating a flatter company only when technical judgment, human authority, and career progression are redesigned around the work that remains.</p></div>

## Hierarchy is partly a routing system

A management layer often connects fragmented tools, incomplete context, and competing priorities. Managers collect status, translate requests, and route unusual cases because the organization cannot otherwise keep the process moving.

Consider a hypothetical sales request for a discount outside the standard range. An agent could retrieve the customer’s contract history, calculate the effect on margin, check the current pricing policy, and prepare the proposed terms. A fixed business rule would enforce the lowest permitted price. Only a true exception would reach a commercial owner, after which the agent could record the decision and notify the people who need it.

The important change is continuity. The agent carries the request through several stages while preserving a human route for judgment. If a person still has to gather the data, chase the decision, repair the record, and tell every team what happened, the company has automated analysis while keeping the coordination burden.

This makes the structural claim testable. Run one bounded workflow against an agreed baseline and observation period. Measure the time and full cost of a completed request, human repairs and hidden handoffs, serious exceptions, and the commercial outcome. Name the business owner, decide which failure stops the trial, and keep a clear route back to the previous process.

I think a layer becomes less useful only when this evidence shows that its routine relay has disappeared. Coaching, conflict resolution, and difficult choices about scarce resources may still justify management. Those responsibilities have to be judged separately from the information routing that agents can absorb.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A management layer loses part of its purpose when software reliably carries the information, actions, and exceptions that the layer once routed. Prove that change in a bounded workflow, including human repair and failure, before treating it as organization design.</p></div>

## Technical judgment moves to the edge

KC’s claim about technical roles reaches far beyond coding. Technical literacy is the ability to understand what information an AI system used, where its permissions end, how failure becomes visible, and when a person must take over.

A commercial leader who owns the discount process needs that literacy at the level of the decision. The leader should know which pricing source is authoritative, what permits a concession, and which cases require human approval. Sending every question to engineering would rebuild the routing layer elsewhere.

Decision rights make the boundary explicit: who may approve, override, stop, and answer for the result. Engineers can own system reliability while the commercial owner sets pricing policy and receives serious exceptions. The model may propose the next action, but fixed rules should enforce permissions for costly or irreversible steps.

A seemingly flat company can still hide a rigid hierarchy. Software permissions, thresholds, and escalation paths may move power into code, where fewer people can challenge it. A named owner, a readable record, and a clear appeal path bring that power back into view.

I think a flatter organization depends on explicit authority. People near the work can move faster when they understand the system and may decide within a defined boundary. Managers remain useful where they allocate resources, develop people, or resolve conflicts across domains; their case weakens when they mainly pass information between capable people.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>As decisions move closer to the work, technical literacy and decision rights must move with them. A person should be able to understand the agent, act within a visible boundary, stop the process, and remain accountable for the consequence.</p></div>

## The career ladder cannot stay a pyramid

The argument becomes shallow if it ends with fewer layers. A management role combines coordination with a route to higher pay, greater influence, wider responsibility, and the experience of developing other people.

When agents absorb routine routing, the company may remove work that once trained future leaders. A promise that everyone will become more strategic gives them no replacement. Progress needs visible forms such as ownership of a more consequential area, deeper expertise, responsibility for higher-risk decisions, or the ability to improve other people’s judgment. Those paths need titles, pay, and credible evidence of achievement.

The transition needs an owner and a budget. A serious redesign states which activities are ending, which responsibilities remain human, what learning the company will fund, and how employees or their representatives will be consulted. It also names who approves new expectations and compensation. Otherwise, a flat structure becomes a cost decision dressed as a design principle.

I don’t know how many people whose careers center on coordination will want, or be able, to move into broader ownership or deeper expertise. That uncertainty deserves a direct account of the choices. Some people will grow into the new work; others may decide it is not for them, and some roles may end because the work has disappeared.

Treat this as a structural hypothesis that must survive operations. A successful demonstration gives too little evidence for a reorganization. I think the case earns credibility when the workflow shows fewer hidden handoffs, acceptable repair and exception rates, clear stop authority, and a funded path for people whose work changes. Each condition can fail, which makes the decision defensible.

My take is that an AI-first company becomes flatter where software genuinely removes coordination work and people can exercise judgment without waiting for the old relay. When authority disappears into permissions, human cleanup stays hidden, or advancement still means collecting direct reports, the pyramid survives inside software, harder to see and harder to challenge.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>When agents absorb routine coordination, redesign progression, pay, learning, and employee participation before removing rungs from the ladder. A flatter company is real only when responsibility and opportunity are clearer than they were inside the pyramid.</p></div>
