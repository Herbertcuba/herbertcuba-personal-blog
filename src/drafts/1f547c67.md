*This is an operating memo for deciding what to change now, what to prove before removing a coordination layer, and which authority and workforce commitments must survive the redesign.*

An AI-first company begins with two linked propositions. Every business role develops a technical dimension. As AI agents, systems that interpret context and use approved tools to carry work through several steps, absorb more execution and coordination, ownership can move closer to customers and operations. A flatter company becomes possible.

In “The 2 Things OpenAI’s Chief People Officer Looks For in Every Hire,” a video published by the business interview channel DECODE on 1 June 2026, [Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), chief people officer at OpenAI, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” OpenAI is the company behind ChatGPT, its generative AI assistant for working with language and other content. KC immediately explained that the technical role is applied to a particular domain, meaning a business area with its own outcomes, rules, and expertise.

That observation supports the capability claim. The structural claim is mine: when domain expertise is combined with agents that remove enough routine work, more initiative can come from the edges and some coordination layers may no longer be needed. I would act on the first claim now and treat the second as a hypothesis that each workflow must prove.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Build technical judgment across the business now, but treat a flatter hierarchy as a conditional operating-model decision that must earn its case.</p></div>

## Make technical judgment a business capability

Technical judgment is the ability to decide where AI is useful, what evidence it needs, how its output should be checked, and when a person must take over. It does not require everyone to write software. It does require people to understand enough about the system to remain accountable for work done with it.

The expression changes by function. Sales must know whether an AI-generated offer respects pricing and contract boundaries. Marketing must judge source quality and approval status before publishing AI-generated creative content. Finance must prevent a model’s classification from becoming an unauthorized payment, while operations and support must recognize when an unusual case falls outside the process the agent can handle safely.

This capability cannot sit only in a central AI team. Domain teams need room to design workflows, the paths from an incoming need to a finished business outcome, as well as improve instructions, challenge poor outputs, and propose new uses. They understand the consequences of a mistake. Bottom-up ownership therefore means authority within boundaries, not uncontrolled experimentation. From the center, that can look less orderly because initiative no longer waits to travel upward. Visible boundaries make the difference between an empowered edge and unmanaged risk.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical judgment means supervising AI-enabled work inside a business domain, including its limits and consequences; coding is optional, accountability is not.</p></div>

## Trace the work that holds the hierarchy together

Author Benjamin Simkin calls a common failure the “Retrofit Trap” in his 2026 book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5). The term describes putting AI inside an unchanged operating structure instead of rebuilding the structure around what the technology can now do. It matters because a fast task can remain trapped inside a slow end-to-end process.

Consider a warranty claim as a structural example. A model can extract the product number and summarize the customer’s message in seconds, yet the claim may still move through a shared inbox, manual assignment, policy review, approval, data entry, and a final check. The extraction improved. The operating model did not.

A redesigned workflow could let an agent gather the evidence, check the claim against approved policy, propose a resolution, update the case, and prepare the customer response. Ordinary cases would no longer need a manager to forward information or chase status. Ambiguous, high-impact, or policy-changing decisions would still move to a person with the authority and context to decide.

That distinction matters because hierarchy does more than route work. Managers also set direction, resolve conflicts, coach people, allocate resources, and carry accountability. Agents may absorb routine information gathering, standard routing, record updates, and follow-up. They do not make the remaining human authority disappear, so a layer should change only when its coordination load has measurably fallen and its other responsibilities have a new home.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up a step while preserving the surrounding handoffs. Structural change becomes plausible only when an agent-supported workflow removes recurring coordination rather than hiding it elsewhere.</p></div>

## Make the workflow prove the structural case

Task speed is weak evidence for changing an organization. The useful unit is a correctly closed case: the intended outcome was reached, required controls passed, the official record was updated, the customer or colleague received the right response, and no uncounted human repair was needed afterward.

Measure the whole path against a baseline. Count correctly closed cases, human routing touches, repair time, quality failures, control breaches, and total cost, including tools, integration, oversight, training, and support. Then identify residual judgment: the decisions people still make after routine routing has been removed. These are proposed decision measures, not established industry benchmarks.

The verified material available for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that supplies a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer. That gap should remain visible. Each company must set local pass and stop values before a trial, then expose the result to independent review rather than fitting the criteria to a preferred reorganization.

A one-page board evidence card can make that discipline concrete:

| Board evidence card | What the authorization record must state |
|---|---|
| Workflow | Named start and end, accountable owner, current baseline, and excluded cases |
| Trial | Fixed window, expected volume, and a representative mix of routine, ambiguous, high-impact, and failed inputs |
| Review | Independent reviewer, sampling plan, reviewer hours, and enough capacity to inspect exceptions and failures |
| Measures | Correct closure, routing touches, repair effort, quality, control failures, full cost, and residual judgment |
| Economics | Tool, model, integration, oversight, training, and support spend, with one budget owner |
| Authority | Owners for exceptions, override, stop, restart, correction, and rollback, meaning a defined corrective path to a safe state |
| People | Affected-work map, employee-representative consultation, paid learning and redeployment capacity, and fair selection criteria |
| Decision | Local pass and stop values fixed in advance, plus a scheduled date to scale, extend, or stop |

The card does not prove that flattening is wise. It prevents a board from approving a structural conclusion on the strength of a demonstration that measured only the agent’s fastest step.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Judge a complete workflow through correctly closed cases, hidden human work, control performance, full cost, and residual judgment; set local criteria before the trial because no Nordic production threshold in the available evidence can make the decision for you.</p></div>

## Put authority where everyone can see it

Removing routing work creates a design obligation. Every workflow needs a business owner for the outcome, a control owner for policy, a technical owner for the service, a data owner for permitted use, and a named person who can stop operations. One person may hold more than one role, but none of the authority should be implied by a model instruction or vendor setting.

The boundary between agent and system should be equally clear. The agent may interpret documents, assemble evidence, and propose or perform allowed steps. Ordinary software should enforce permissions and authoritative state, the official current record of where the case stands. A deterministic control applies an explicit rule to an action, rather than asking a language model, the generative AI component that interprets or produces content from instructions, to decide whether its own proposed action is permitted.

Consequential actions also need idempotency, a software property that prevents the same authorized operation from creating a second business effect when it is retried. For the warranty workflow, the payment request receives a unique operation identifier tied to its amount and recipient. If the payment provider’s response is uncertain, the case pauses for reconciliation, which means checking the provider and internal records. The system does not simply try again and risk paying twice.

An audit trail should let a reviewer reconstruct four things: identity, decision basis, state change, and outcome. That means recording the case and operation identifiers, the human or agent actor, the evidence and policy used, relevant model and policy versions, the before-and-after state with time and reason, and any external response, override, or reconciliation. If a claim marked “awaiting evidence” is sent directly to “paid,” the control should reject and record the illegal transition.

Finally, stop and rollback must describe real powers. Stop authority pauses new actions; restart authority confirms the conditions for resuming. Rollback follows the corrective path back to a safe state, although an external payment or message may require compensation and reconciliation rather than a literal undo. These controls keep complexity in visible architecture instead of concealing it inside free-form model reasoning.

## Redesign the career system at the same time

A flatter structure changes more than reporting lines. Coordination-heavy roles often combine routing with judgment, coaching, conflict resolution, and institutional memory. The responsible first step is to map that bundle of work, identify what the agent-supported workflow actually removes, and assign every remaining responsibility before discussing positions.

Career architecture then needs attention. If advancement has meant managing a larger team, fewer layers can turn the future into a narrow contest for the remaining management jobs. Organizations need credible paths for deep domain expertise, workflow ownership, responsibility for safeguards, and coaching, with pay and status that make those paths real rather than decorative.

Some people will need paid time to learn a changed role. Others may move into different work, and some transitions may still end in an exit. A serious plan funds learning and redeployment, publishes selection criteria, and explains how pay and progression will change. In a Nordic organization, employee-representative consultation and local labour obligations belong before the scale decision, while alternatives are still open.

This is not a frictionless transition, and a board should not approve it as one. Workforce readiness is part of operating readiness because the redesigned workflow still depends on people who understand the domain, handle exceptions, improve policy, and carry accountability when the system reaches its boundary.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Removing a layer is defensible only when judgment, coaching, progression, pay, learning, consultation, and fair transition have been redesigned alongside the workflow.</p></div>

The mapping is narrow: an enterprise creates the same structural danger when an agent can act across a workflow but nobody can see the governing instruction, identify the accountable owner, or exercise reliable stop authority. The lesson is about visible decision rights, not machine personality.

## Put two resolutions before the board

The first resolution should authorize capability building across the company. Give domain teams approved tools, training, workflow ownership, and clear control boundaries so that technical judgment becomes part of ordinary business work. This decision does not need a promise to remove management layers.

The second resolution should be conditional and workflow-specific. It authorizes a structural change only after the evidence card shows that recurring coordination has genuinely fallen, correctly closed cases and controls meet values fixed in advance, the remaining human judgment has named owners, and the workforce transition is funded and ready. The record should name the independent reviewer, budget owner, stop authority, consultation status, and date for the scale, extend, or stop decision.

That is the architecture I would defend: stronger technical judgment throughout the business, more ownership at the edges, and fewer coordination layers where the work has earned that design. A flatter structure is justified only when routine coordination has verifiably gone and every remaining authority has a visible home.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Authorize distributed technical judgment now; authorize a flatter structure later, one proven workflow at a time, with evidence, controls, owners, and a credible people transition recorded before the org chart changes.</p></div>
