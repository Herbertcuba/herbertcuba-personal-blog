Two linked claims sit at the heart of an AI-first operating model. Every role acquires a technical dimension, and more work can be owned from the bottom up as agents absorb routine execution and coordination.

The first claim has a useful source. In a [DECODE interview published on 1 June 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ), Arvind KC, chief people officer at OpenAI, the AI company behind the generative assistant ChatGPT, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately placed that technical role inside a business domain. His point was broader than software development.

The second claim is an organizational hypothesis. Bottom-up ownership means that people closest to a business domain can shape decisions and improve workflows instead of sending every change through a central technical function. I think that can support a materially flatter company, but the arrival of agents proves nothing about whether a management layer is safe to remove. The mechanism has to work first.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Technical capability is spreading into business roles, while flatter structures remain a conditional design choice that must be earned through operating evidence.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment belongs in the domain

Technical judgment is the ability to reason about what an AI system can do reliably, where it is likely to fail, and which decisions still require a person. It does not require everyone to code. It requires each function to understand the capability boundary well enough to own an outcome.

A sales leader should recognize when an AI-generated proposal changes a commercial commitment. A finance specialist should know which variance can be explained automatically and which one needs investigation. Operations and support teams should be able to identify unusual cases, poor source data, and decisions that carry consequences beyond the agent’s mandate. The people who understand the domain define what good work means and which failures matter.

That changes the relationship between business and technology. A central team can provide platforms, controls, and specialist support, but it cannot be the only place where technical reasoning happens. Domain-mapped ownership gives a named business owner responsibility for the result, the exceptions, and the improvement of the workflow. This is the practical basis for bottom-up innovation.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to own AI-supported outcomes, while central technology teams provide shared systems and controls rather than becoming a queue for every decision.</p>
</div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the old company

Benjamin Simkin’s 2026 book, [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls the structural failure of adding AI without rebuilding around its capabilities the “Retrofit Trap.” The term matters because faster activity can leave the operating model untouched.

Consider supplier onboarding. An agent might read submitted documents and produce a risk summary in seconds. Yet procurement may still copy data into another system, security may wait for an email, legal may chase a missing clause, and finance may create the supplier record by hand. One task moved faster, while the surrounding routing, approvals, and repair work survived.

An AI-first design examines the whole path from request to an accepted or rejected supplier. The agent may gather context, ask for missing evidence, recommend a risk class, and route an exception to its named owner. People retain decisions involving risk appetite, contested interpretation, and commitments that bind the company. Traditional hierarchy often carries information, approvals, and exceptions; a flatter structure becomes plausible only when the workflow absorbs enough of that coordination without hiding it elsewhere.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Task acceleration does not change the organization when people still carry the case between systems, chase approvals, and repair the agent’s work.</p>
</div>

## Make the workflow earn the hierarchy change

The unit of evidence should be a correctly closed case across the complete workflow. “Correctly closed” means the outcome, required evidence, approvals, and official record are all right. Response speed alone cannot show whether coordination disappeared or merely moved to an unmeasured team.

For one bounded workflow, write the decision record before the test begins:

- **Outcome:** define the baseline, representative case mix, independent review, and the quality required for a correct close.
- **Coordination:** count human routing touches, waiting between owners, and repair work across the full path.
- **Control:** record unauthorized actions, missed exceptions, retries, repeated runs, and where failures concentrate.
- **Economics and dependency:** include model licensing, integration, oversight, data-residency constraints, and the cost of replacing or leaving a provider.
- **Residual judgment:** name the decisions that still need human interpretation and the role accountable for each one.

These are proposed decision fields, not an established benchmark. The test should also state its review window, acceptance conditions, and the forum empowered to scale, extend, or stop it. Among the evidence examined for this argument, no verified Swedish or Norwegian production case establishes a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer. Any threshold therefore has to be local, explicit, and open to challenge.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Judge a bounded workflow by correct closure, hidden human work, control failures, full cost, and remaining judgment; do not borrow an unsupported delayering threshold.</p>
</div>

## Put authority into the operating architecture

A domain map names the owner of the business outcome and the people allowed to decide exceptions. Explicit decision rights then define what an agent may recommend, what it may execute, and what always needs approval. This makes ownership visible before the system starts moving work.

The enforcement boundary belongs in ordinary software: rule-based systems that hold permissions and the authoritative state, meaning the official record of where a case stands. An agent can interpret supplier documents and propose “approved,” but software should reject that state change if mandatory evidence is missing. The language model supplies judgment within a mandate; it does not invent the mandate while acting.

This separation uses established software and security concepts. [NIST Special Publication 800-53 Revision 5](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-53r5), a 2020 US government catalog of security and privacy controls, includes access enforcement and requirements for audit-record content. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods), the 2022 standard defining core HTTP semantics, uses *idempotency* for a request whose repeated execution has the same intended effect as executing it once. Applied here, a repeated agent request must not create duplicate supplier records or duplicate external actions.

The audit path should show the request, evidence used, rule applied, model and software version, human intervention, and resulting state. A named owner needs the power to stop the workflow, while restart conditions are agreed in advance. Rollback means restoring software or configuration to the last approved state and reconciling consequences that cannot simply be undone.

From the center, more decisions at the edge can look chaotic. Visible owners, permitted actions, official state, and stop paths turn that apparent disorder into governed autonomy.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Agents may interpret and propose, while named people own decisions and ordinary software enforces permissions, state changes, duplicate protection, audit, stop, and recovery.</p>
</div>

The mapping is about organizational authority, not machine consciousness. A company creates the same design failure in smaller form when it lets an agent’s ability to act expand faster than named ownership, enforced permissions, and a credible stop path.

## Rebuild the human system with the workflow

A coordination-heavy role is one in which much of the work consists of moving information, collecting approvals, and repairing handoffs. Some of that work may shrink when an agent-supported workflow performs those functions reliably. The same role may also contain coaching, conflict resolution, contextual judgment, and accountability that the workflow has not absorbed. Leaders need to separate those activities before declaring the role obsolete.

Flattening also breaks familiar career ladders. If progression has depended on acquiring a larger team, the company needs credible expert and domain-owner paths with real authority, pay, and status. Coaching must have an owner even when there are fewer line managers. Technical judgment will spread only if people receive paid time to learn and a fair way to demonstrate it.

The transition will create difficult choices. A responsible plan includes transparent role criteria, funded learning, redeployment capacity, employee-representative consultation, and fair exit terms where work genuinely disappears. These commitments belong in the authorization decision because a workflow redesign that ignores its human consequences is incomplete by design.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Removing coordination work requires new career paths, explicit coaching ownership, funded learning, consultation, and an honest plan for roles that materially change or disappear.</p>
</div>

## Change the org chart last

Before proposing a flatter structure, the CIO should ask the executive operating committee to authorize one bounded workflow test. That forum needs the authority to scale, extend, or stop the work, and its record should answer six questions:

1. Did representative cases close correctly from intake to final record?
2. Did human routing and repair fall across the complete workflow?
3. Is the remaining human judgment named and owned?
4. Did permissions, state controls, audit, duplicate protection, and stop paths hold under repeated use and failure?
5. Does the full cost include oversight, supplier dependency, data location, and a workable exit path?
6. Is there a funded and credible transition for the people whose work changes?

If those answers support a redesign, the company can test wider spans of control or fewer coordination layers in stages, with a review point and a way to correct course. If they do not, the organization should improve or stop the workflow while continuing to build technical judgment in the domains.

A flatter org chart may follow. The defining achievement is a defensible transfer of work and authority, with the evidence, controls, and human commitments visible to the people expected to approve it.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Authorize a bounded operating test before delayering, and change the hierarchy only when complete-workflow evidence, enforceable authority, full economics, and the workforce plan survive executive review.</p>
</div>
