An AI-first company rests on two connected propositions. Every business role gains a technical dimension, because decisions in sales, finance, operations, marketing, and support increasingly depend on knowing what artificial intelligence can do reliably. If AI agents also remove enough routine execution and coordination, more ownership can sit with the people closest to the work and the organization may need fewer layers devoted to routing information and approvals.

I use *AI agent* in a narrow sense: software that gives a language model, a system trained to interpret and produce language, instructions and approved tools so it can work through several steps toward a goal. [OpenAI's Agents SDK](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/), a developer toolkit from the AI company behind the ChatGPT assistant, documents one implementation of that pattern, including points where execution pauses for human approval. An AI-first operating model designs a complete flow of work around such capabilities and their limits. It does not simply add a model to one task.

The two propositions are plausible, but neither gives a CIO permission to remove a management layer. Agents can make coordination less expensive while leaving accountability, conflict, coaching, and risk acceptance untouched. They can also make power harder to see by burying it in permissions, model instructions, or supplier controls. I think the real leadership question is therefore conditional: what must the operating evidence prove, and what architecture must replace the old hierarchy, before a flatter company becomes defensible?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An AI-first company distributes technical judgment and may need fewer coordination layers, but the org chart should move only after complete workflows prove the change and authority and workforce obligations have a visible home.</p></div>

## Technical judgment moves into the business

[Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), OpenAI's chief people officer, appeared in a [recorded May 2026 discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s) hosted by DECODE, a community for company founders. He said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” The wording is taken from the recording's English automatic captions. He immediately connected the technical role to a particular domain, meaning an area of work with its own knowledge and outcome.

That does not mean everyone becomes a programmer. It means a finance leader must understand which figures an AI system is allowed to use, how uncertainty appears, and which decisions still require a person. A sales leader needs to know when an automated recommendation is dependable and when context from a relationship changes the answer. Operations and support leaders need the same judgment about cases, policies, and failure, at the level where they already make business decisions.

I think this capability is best described as technical judgment: the ability to reason about what the system can do, what evidence supports its output, how it can fail, and where human discretion begins. Engineers still build dependable tools and access controls. Domain experts define the business meaning of a good outcome, including the exceptions that cannot be reduced to a general rule.

That division supports domain-mapped ownership. A domain owner is a named person with authority over one bounded business outcome, its operating rules, its evidence, and its risks. The central technology function provides shared platforms and specialist help, but it should not become the queue through which every uncertain business case must travel.

KC's remark supports the first proposition. The second is a design hypothesis I am making here: if governed workflows remove enough routine coordination, decisions can move toward domain owners and fewer routing layers may be necessary. The sources cited in this article do not show enterprises removing management layers safely at scale, so bottom-up ownership should be tested as an operating design, not repeated as a fact.

From the center, that arrangement can look chaotic because several domain owners act in parallel instead of waiting for one approval chain. The apparent disorder is defensible only when those empowered edges operate inside visible boundaries. Without clear rights and controls it is unmanaged risk; with them, it is the surface effect of ownership moving closer to context.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical judgment is business-side reasoning about AI capability, evidence, failure, and human discretion. It belongs with named domain owners close to the outcome, while engineers provide the reliable systems that let those owners act.</p></div>

## The work between tasks sustains the hierarchy

Benjamin Simkin's independently published May 2026 book, *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, calls a common design failure the Retrofit Trap. One activity becomes faster, but the surrounding structure remains. People still find missing information, move the case between teams, chase approval, repair the output, and close the record by hand.

Imagine a maintenance request in a large industrial company. A model reads a technician's note and drafts a fault diagnosis in seconds. Yet a coordinator still checks the asset history, asks whether production can release the machine, finds an approved contractor, collects a safety permit, and updates the maintenance record after the work. The diagnosis is quicker, while the workflow and the roles supporting it are almost unchanged.

An AI-first version starts with the complete case. An agent may gather the relevant records, interpret the note, propose a standard job, request missing evidence, and route an unusual safety condition to the right owner. Ordinary software then checks permissions and the current status of the asset before it creates or changes anything. A person remains accountable for a high-risk exception and has the power to stop the flow.

This example reveals why hierarchy and workflow are connected. Management layers often aggregate information, allocate resources, resolve exceptions, translate between functions, and move approvals. Agents may absorb some of that movement. They do not automatically replace the manager who coaches a team, settles a conflict, accepts a business risk, or answers for the result.

I think the word *flatter* becomes useful only after those responsibilities are separated. If an agent removes routine routing, a coordination-heavy role may narrow or disappear. If the same judgment and people responsibilities remain, the organization still needs owners for them, even when their titles or reporting lines change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Retrofit Trap makes an isolated activity faster while preserving the manual work around it. A flatter structure becomes plausible only when the complete workflow removes a named coordination responsibility and the remaining human duties are reassigned explicitly.</p></div>

## Prove the coordination is gone

The evidence unit should be a correctly closed case: one complete piece of work that reached the right business outcome with its required permissions, controls, and records intact. For the maintenance request, a good diagnosis is insufficient if a person still reconstructs the asset history or repairs the final record. The case closes only when the permitted work is completed or rejected, the authoritative maintenance record reflects what happened, and exceptions have a documented owner.

A fair trial needs three matched designs. Compare the current process, the strongest conventional automation option, and the agent-supported workflow on the same case mix, outcome standard, period, and total-cost boundary. Conventional automation means rules and process software that follow defined paths without asking a language model to interpret open-ended material. If it removes the same coordination with less cost, risk, or supplier dependence, the agent has not justified its extra complexity.

Before the trial starts, the executive sponsor should approve a short evidence contract:

- **Case and ownership:** Define the start, correct finish, permitted actions, business owner, control owner, and exception owner.
- **Matched alternatives:** Freeze the current, conventional, and agent-supported designs, along with the same ordinary and difficult cases for each.
- **Operating evidence:** Count correctly closed cases, elapsed time, human routing touches, repair work, quality failures, prohibited actions, and residual judgment that was declared human in advance.
- **Full cost and resilience:** Include software, integration, verification, human repair, supplier dependence, and the tested route back to a safe process.
- **Decision rule:** Set the trial period or case count, thresholds derived from the company's baseline and risk tolerance, immediate stop triggers, independent verifier, and decision date.

This is my proposed leadership test, not an industry benchmark. The task-local evidence reviewed for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case with public before-and-after values for routing, repair, control failures, outcome quality, and full cost. I think preserving that gap is more useful than borrowing a confident number from a different process or market.

The conclusion must remain proportional to the evidence. In a 5,000-person enterprise, a bounded trial can support a decision about that workflow; it cannot establish that an organization-wide layer is redundant. The comparison needs to be repeated across material workflows and periods of higher volume or unusual exceptions. If throughput improves while human routing stays in place, the experiment has supported a better tool, not a new hierarchy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Judge complete cases, not model outputs. The agent-supported workflow must beat both today's process and the strongest conventional automation on outcomes, human coordination, control, resilience, and full cost before its results can support an organizational change.</p></div>

## Make authority more visible than the hierarchy did

A reporting line once signalled at least part of the authority around a decision. In an agent-supported workflow, the decisive rule may instead sit in a tool permission, a threshold, a supplier account, or an instruction that few business leaders can inspect. The replacement architecture therefore needs an operating constitution: a clear account of who may approve, override, stop, restore, and answer for each material action.

The domain owner should define the outcome and policy. The agent can interpret unstructured material and propose an allowed next step. A control owner specifies the safeguards and evidence, while ordinary workflow software enforces permissions and the current state of the case. A named human decides exceptions that require discretion and holds stop authority. These roles may sit in different functions, which is precisely why their decision rights must be written down.

State means the place where the authoritative business record says the case stands. Stateful workflow software is established practice: [Camunda](https://docs.camunda.org/manual/latest/user-guide/process-engine/process-engine-concepts/), a business-process automation platform with a public platform release dating to [2013](https://github.com/camunda/camunda-bpm-platform/tree/7.0.0-Final), documents process instances that follow a defined process. A language model can interpret a technician's note, but it should not decide by free-form reasoning whether the machine is already locked for maintenance or whether the requester has permission to release it.

Idempotency is another necessary control. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110#section-9.2.2), a 2022 Internet standard for web requests, describes an idempotent request as one whose intended effect is the same whether it is sent once or repeatedly. In the maintenance flow, an external contractor booking that times out should remain pending until the system reconciles what happened. The integration should recognize the same unique request key on a retry so it cannot create a second visit for the same case.

Auditability means the organization can reconstruct the action from evidence rather than trust a fluent account from the model. The record should include the input evidence, system and model versions, tool actions, permission checks, state changes, retries, human decisions, and final outcome. Rollback means the company has tested how to stop the workflow, withdraw access, correct records, and return safely to the previous process.

I think these controls create stronger bottom-up ownership because they let domain owners act inside known boundaries without sending every decision upward. For a material workflow, the decision forum should include the business owner, control owner, technology owner, HR, employee representation where applicable, and an independent verifier. The forum agrees the stop trigger and expected role effect before launch, then takes the verified result to the steering committee or board body that can authorize the structural change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Distributed ownership requires explicit decision rights, ordinary software that enforces permission and state, and named people who own exceptions and stops. The audit, recovery path, and decision forum must exist before the hierarchy is asked to carry less control.</p></div>

## Replace the career system before changing the chart

A hierarchy is also a career system. Coordination roles often carry pay, status, access to difficult decisions, responsibility for coaching, and a visible route into broader leadership. Remove the routing work without rebuilding those functions and the company may become flatter while becoming worse at developing people.

Start by breaking each affected role into its real responsibilities. Separate routine movement of information from business judgment, conflict resolution, resource allocation, coaching, and risk ownership. That makes it possible to see whether the role has vanished, become narrower, or changed into a different kind of work.

New progression can lead toward deeper specialist authority, ownership of a broader domain, control and exception responsibility, or people leadership focused on developing judgment. Those routes are credible only when selection criteria are public, learning time is funded, and titles and pay recognize the added responsibility. Otherwise the company has removed a ladder and replaced it with encouragement.

I don't know what proportion of people in coordination-heavy roles will want or be able to make that move. Some will grow into new authority, some roles will lose their purpose, and some people will leave. Responsible leadership states that possibility before the reorganization and gives employees a fair chance to prepare. In Sweden, the 1976 [Employment (Co-Determination in the Workplace) Act](https://www.government.se/government-policy/labour-law-and-work-environment/1976580-employment-co-determination-in-the-workplace-act-lag-om-medbestammande-i-arbetslivet/) is commonly called MBL. Subject to the Act's exceptions, employers bound by a collective agreement generally must initiate negotiations with the relevant employees' organization. Section 11 places that duty before decisions about significant changes to the employer's activities or represented employees' working conditions. The exact duties vary by country and situation, but employee participation belongs in the design rather than after the announcement.

The CIO's case to the executive team or board is ready only when repeated complete-workflow evidence beats both the current process and strong conventional automation, and shows that a specific coordination responsibility has moved or disappeared. Domain, control, exception, and stop authority must already operate, with audit and rollback tested. The organization must also have funded the new career and transition arrangements.

My take is that an AI-first company should become flatter only as the consequence of proven work. Leaders should not alter the hierarchy to discover whether coordination can disappear. They should prove that it has disappeared, build the authority and careers that replace it, and then let the org chart acknowledge reality.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Changing the hierarchy also changes progression, coaching, pay, and employment conditions. Demand repeated operating proof, a working authority model, proper employee participation, and a funded transition first; the org chart should record the redesign only after the new system has earned it.</p></div>
