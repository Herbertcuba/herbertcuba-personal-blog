An AI-first company begins with two linked propositions. Every business role acquires a technical dimension, and an organization can become flatter when AI systems absorb enough execution and coordination for ownership to move closer to the people who understand the domain.

Those propositions do not have equal support. Arvind KC, OpenAI's chief people officer, [said in a May 2026 recording of a DECODE founders dinner, a filmed conversation for company founders](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s), that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately qualified that as technical work “applied to a particular domain.” The recording supports the first proposition, but it does not establish that agents have already made OpenAI flatter or shifted innovation from the top down.

The second proposition should therefore be treated as a testable organizational hypothesis. A responsible leader changes reporting lines only when complete-workflow evidence shows that coordination work has genuinely disappeared, authority has a visible new home, complexity remains controlled, and the people affected have a credible path through the change.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Technical judgment can spread now. A flatter hierarchy remains a hypothesis until whole workflows prove that coordination work has actually been removed.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment belongs inside the business

Technical judgment is the ability to decide what an AI system can do reliably, what evidence it needs, where it is likely to fail, and when an accountable person must decide. It does not mean that every salesperson, controller, marketer, or service specialist must become a programmer.

It does mean that those people can no longer hand every AI question to a central technology team. Sales must judge whether an account summary is faithful enough to support an offer. Finance must know whether a reconciliation is based on complete records. Marketing must understand which claims require human review, while customer support must know when a proposed remedy crosses a policy or regulatory boundary. The technical dimension is inseparable from the business decision because the domain expert understands the consequence of being wrong.

This changes where improvement can begin. People closest to the work can identify a weak handoff, change the instructions or evidence available to the system, and test a better path within agreed boundaries. From the center, that degree of local ownership may look untidy. It becomes disciplined bottom-up innovation when domains share control rules, outcome measures, and exception paths while retaining room to improve their own work.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>AI literacy for business roles is practical judgment about reliability, evidence, failure, and consequence. Shared controls can turn that distributed judgment into bottom-up improvement without requiring everyone to code.</p>
</div>

## The Retrofit Trap leaves the hierarchy intact

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book *The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*, [calls the act of bolting AI onto an old structure the “Retrofit Trap”](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5). The term matters because task speed can improve while the operating model stays exactly where it was.

Consider an illustrative enterprise discount request. A generative model may draft the commercial justification in seconds, yet the request still moves by email from the account executive to sales operations, finance, legal, and a commercial director. People copy data between systems, ask for missing context, check policy, chase approvals, and repair exceptions. The draft became faster, but the hierarchy still carries information and authority along the same route.

Here, an AI agent means a software system that uses a generative model to interpret a case, choose among permitted next steps, and call business tools. Such an agent might collect the account history, apply the current discount policy, prepare an in-policy offer, record its evidence, and send unusual cases to a named owner. If it only produces the document and creates another review queue, it has absorbed a task rather than a workflow.

That distinction explains when flatter structures become possible. Hierarchy does more than route forms: it sets priorities, resolves conflicts, authorizes exceptions, coaches people, and carries accountability. Agents may remove status gathering, reminders, routine checks, and standard routing. A layer can shrink only when those coordination duties have fallen away and its remaining human duties have been deliberately reassigned.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up an isolated task while preserving the approvals, routing, repair, and accountability around it. Delayering becomes plausible only when a complete operating path needs materially less coordination.</p>
</div>

## Flattening must be earned by complete-workflow evidence

A complete workflow runs from a real business trigger to a verified outcome, including exceptions and control evidence. A correctly closed case reaches the right outcome, records the required evidence, and leaves the business system in a valid state. That is a more useful unit than the number of drafts produced or agent steps completed.

The baseline and the trial must use a comparable case mix, meaning the same balance of routine, difficult, and exceptional cases. For each group, measure correctly closed cases, human routing touches, repair time, outcome quality, control failures, total cost, and residual judgment that still requires accountable human discretion. An attractive average can hide a system that works only because people quietly rescue its hardest cases.

No Swedish or Norwegian production case among the sources supporting this article supplies a transferable numerical threshold for safely removing a management layer because of agents. A board should not borrow one. Acceptance conditions need to be chosen locally before the trial, against the organization's own risk, service, cost, and workforce obligations.

A compact **delayering evidence contract** can make that decision auditable. This is a proposed leadership record, not an industry benchmark:

- **Decision under test:** If `[workflow]` meets `[predeclared conditions]`, move `[named coordination duties]` from `[role or layer]` to `[new owner or system]`.
- **Boundary and case mix:** Start event `[ ]`; verified end state `[ ]`; exclusions `[ ]`; routine, difficult, and exceptional case distribution `[ ]`.
- **Baseline and provenance:** Comparison period `[ ]`; source systems `[ ]`; extraction owner `[ ]`; known data gaps `[ ]`.
- **Run limits:** Duration `[ ]`; volume `[ ]`; permissions `[ ]`; spending or capacity ceiling `[ ]`; stop owner `[ ]`.
- **Acceptance conditions:** Correctly closed cases `[ ]`; quality `[ ]`; human touches and repair effort `[ ]`; control failures `[ ]`; total cost `[ ]`.
- **Review and reversal:** Review date `[ ]`; decision makers `[ ]`; stop, extend, redesign, or delayer options `[ ]`; responsibility-restoration path `[ ]`.
- **People commitments:** Roles affected `[ ]`; employee-participation date `[ ]`; learning and redeployment funding `[ ]`; pay and progression decisions `[ ]`.

At the scheduled review, the decision makers should see the failed and repaired cases as well as the successful ones. If coordination merely moved into an unofficial support queue, the organization has not earned a flatter shape.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Measure verified business outcomes across a representative case mix, including hidden human repair, control failures, residual judgment, and total cost. Set local acceptance and reversal conditions before the trial rather than inventing a universal delayering threshold.</p>
</div>

## Authority has to become more visible

Removing a routing layer creates an authority problem unless the replacement architecture is explicit. Each workflow needs a named business-domain owner, and its decision rights must state who may decide what, within which limits, and who owns an exception. The agent can interpret and propose inside that design; it cannot be the source of its own permission.

For the discount workflow, rules-based software should hold the authoritative state, meaning the current verified status of the opportunity, customer, policy, and approval. Before an action, it checks the acting principal, the verified person or service making the request, whose authority that principal is using, the permitted customer and action, the financial limit, the expiry, and the current case version. The delegation record must also say what happens to work already under way when authority is revoked.

That boundary matters because the model will read untrusted material. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology's 2024 generative-AI risk profile, [NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf), describes indirect prompt injection: instructions placed in material likely to be retrieved by an application can redirect the model. A customer attachment can contribute evidence to a case, but permissions must come from the control system rather than from text the model is reading.

Every attempted action should leave an audit record connecting the request, evidence, policy version, model proposal, enforcing rule, result, and any human override. Stop authority is the named power to block new actions, contain uncertain cases already in flight, and decide how service resumes. Rollback can restore internal state when an action is reversible; an offer already sent outside the company may instead require reconciliation and a corrective action owned by a person.

Duplicate execution needs the same care. The 2022 web standard [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods) defines idempotency as multiple identical requests having the same intended effect as one. A unique action key can prevent the pricing system from issuing a second rebate when a request is retried, but it cannot guarantee that every external effect and internal status update occur together. If a connection fails after an external system acts, the workflow should pause, check that system's record, and route unresolved cases rather than guessing and retrying.

Finally, the company should retain its case state, control evidence, and audit history in systems it governs. Model suppliers can change, so contracts and architecture should support log export, service-change notice, continuity, and an orderly exit without making a provider's model the owner of the business process.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Domain owners set decision rights, ordinary software enforces current state and delegated limits, and named people own exceptions, stops, reconciliation, and corrective action. Agent autonomy is defensible only inside that visible chain of authority.</p>
</div>

The organizational mapping is direct: delegated authority does not disappear when it is embedded in a system. An enterprise agent should reveal whose objective it serves, whose authority it uses, and who can stop it, or apparent autonomy will conceal the hierarchy that still governs the work.

## Change the career system before the org chart

When routine routing and follow-up decline, coordination-heavy roles will change. Some people can move toward domain ownership, difficult exception judgment, coaching, or control design; some roles may shrink or end. A credible transition states that possibility early instead of presenting delayering as a frictionless productivity gain.

The old pyramid also carried ambition. Management titles often provided pay progression, status, access to decisions, and opportunities to coach or be coached. A flatter company needs expert and domain-owner paths with comparable recognition, explicit pay bands, protected coaching responsibilities, and deliberate learning work for people whose entry-level tasks are increasingly automated. Otherwise, “bottom-up ownership” can amount to more responsibility without matching authority or reward.

For a Swedish organization, employee participation has a specific legal anchor. [Section 11 of the Co-Determination in the Workplace Act (1976:580)](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-1976580-om-medbestammande-i-arbetslivet_sfs-1976-580/) requires an employer bound by a collective agreement to initiate negotiation with the relevant employee organization before deciding an important change to operations or to employees' working or employment conditions. For this kind of redesign, the business owner and people leader should convene that participation while role scope, timing, training, redeployment, and evaluation remain open to influence. The exact process still requires local legal and agreement-specific review.

The CIO owns the evidence and control architecture, but cannot own the human transition alone. The domain executive owns the operating outcome, the people leader owns the role and participation process, line leaders own coaching, and finance must fund learning, redeployment, or fair exit support. Those commitments belong in the same decision record as quality and cost because they are part of whether the redesign is viable.

Before approving a new reporting structure, an executive team should be able to answer five questions:

1. Which complete workflow now closes correctly with less human coordination across the real case mix?
2. Which duties disappeared, and which were merely moved to another team or supplier?
3. Who grants permission, owns exceptions, can stop execution, and resolves uncertain external actions?
4. What evidence, acceptance conditions, review date, and reversal path were agreed before the trial?
5. How will affected people participate, learn, progress, redeploy, or leave on defensible terms?

My recommendation is to approve a bounded operating-model trial before approving delayering. If the review shows that coordination has genuinely fallen, authority is clearer, total cost is acceptable, and the workforce commitments are funded, a flatter structure becomes defensible. If those conditions fail, retain the layer and redesign the workflow rather than asking the org chart to create a capability the operation has not yet proved.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>A flatter company needs a new career and participation system as much as a new control architecture. Approve delayering only when workflow evidence, visible authority, reversibility, and funded people commitments all support the same decision.</p>
</div>
