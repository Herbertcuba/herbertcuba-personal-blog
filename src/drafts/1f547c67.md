The architecture of an AI-first company begins with two linked propositions. Every business role acquires a technical dimension, and organizations can become flatter when more ownership moves to the people closest to the work.

AI agents make the connection possible. An agent is software that uses a generative AI model, which produces text or other content from instructions, to interpret a goal, choose steps, and act through approved tools. If agents absorb meaningful execution and coordination, bottom-up ownership becomes easier because people no longer need the same number of handoffs to move work forward.

That is a design possibility, not a prediction that managers will disappear. A hierarchy carries information, grants approval, routes exceptions, and holds accountability. A CIO should change it only when those functions have either been removed from the workflow or reassigned to a visible owner.

## Technical judgment becomes part of every role

Arvind KC, who held a people-leadership role at OpenAI, an artificial intelligence research and product company, described future roles as fundamentally technical and then tied that technical dimension to a business domain. His related point about innovation was bottom-up: people close to sales, finance, support, or operations will find valuable uses that a central AI team is unlikely to specify in advance.

Technical does not mean that everyone must code. It means that a sales leader can judge when an agent may prepare account research but cannot make a customer commitment, while a finance specialist can distinguish a routine reconciliation from an exception that needs human review. The capability is technical judgment: understanding what AI can do reliably, where its limits sit, and how the surrounding work must change.

A 2023 field experiment involving 758 consultants and carried out by researchers from Harvard Business School, Harvard University's graduate business school, and other universities showed why this judgment matters. Participants using generative AI performed better on tasks within the technology's capability boundary, but correctness fell on a task outside it. The researchers called this uneven boundary the “jagged technological frontier.” Domain expertise is what helps a person notice which side of that boundary a case is on.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Every role needs enough technical judgment to set useful boundaries for AI inside its own domain; this is a business capability, not a demand that every employee become a programmer.</p>
</div>

## Flattening has to be earned by workflow evidence

Benjamin Simkin, the author of *The AI First Company*, calls isolated automation the “Retrofit Trap.” A company inserts AI into one step but preserves the old queues, approvals, handoffs, and repair work around it. The task becomes faster while the operating model remains largely untouched.

Consider a customer refund. An agent might draft a response in seconds, yet the case can still wait for classification, a policy check, managerial approval, a system update, and manual correction when information is missing. Measuring drafting time would make the intervention look successful even if customers wait just as long and employees spend more time fixing bad cases.

This is also why an agent-supported workflow does not automatically remove a manager. Agents may gather context, apply routine policy, update records, and route unusual cases. Humans still have to set the policy, decide acceptable risk, resolve novel exceptions, make high-impact or irreversible choices, and answer for the outcome.

The evidence unit should therefore be the complete workflow, from its trigger to a correctly closed case, where the intended outcome is reached and recorded without an unresolved error. I would examine human routing touches, repair effort, quality, control failures, total cost, and the judgment still required at the end. These are proposed decision measures, not established benchmarks. There is no Swedish or Norwegian production benchmark in the evidence behind this argument, so each company must set thresholds that match its own obligations and risk.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>A faster AI-assisted task does not justify a flatter company. Leaders need end-to-end evidence that routing and repair work truly fell while quality, cost, and control remained acceptable.</p>
</div>

## Authority must be visible at the edges

A flatter model needs more than capable agents. It needs domain-mapped ownership: every important workflow belongs to a named business owner who is accountable for its outcome and rules. Decision rights then state what an agent may propose, what it may execute, which exceptions require human approval, and who can stop the workflow.

Reliable software should enforce permissions and workflow state, meaning the authoritative record of where a case currently stands. It should also provide safe retries, known technically as idempotency, so that repeating an action cannot create a second payment, order, or refund. Because a generative AI model can give different answers to similar inputs, it may interpret an email or propose a next step but should not decide its own access or whether a transaction has already happened.

Overrides and stops need named owners. So do rollbacks, which restore the last known safe state, and an audit trail that records what changed, who approved it, and why. From the center, distributed decisions may look disorderly. Visible boundaries make empowered edges governable without recreating every old approval layer.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>The replacement architecture combines domain ownership and explicit decision rights with software-enforced permissions, state, safe retries, audit, override, stop, and rollback paths.</p>
</div>

## The people system must change with the pyramid

When routine coordination shrinks, coordination-heavy roles will change. Some status gathering, routing, and follow-up may go away, but coaching, conflict resolution, resource choices, domain learning, and accountable judgment do not vanish with a management layer. Leaders must decide where each of those responsibilities moves before they remove the role that used to carry them.

The evidence does not support a simple workforce forecast. *Generative AI at Work*, a working paper distributed by the US National Bureau of Economic Research, a nonprofit organization that circulates economics research, studied 5,179 customer-support agents. AI assistance increased measured productivity by 14 percent on average and by 34 percent among novice and lower-skilled workers, but the authors explicitly said the study did not establish aggregate employment or wage effects. It supports a claim about one workflow and a possible learning benefit, not a conclusion about headcount.

A credible transition therefore covers career ladders, coaching, progression, pay, learning, and ambition. If junior coordination work or traditional management rungs narrow, employees need another route to build domain depth and take on greater responsibility. Affected people and their representatives should be involved early, with clear choices about training, redeployment, selection, and support.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Removing coordination work also removes familiar learning and progression routes. A responsible redesign reassigns essential human duties and gives affected employees a credible path through the change.</p>
</div>

## Change the org chart only after the operating proof

Before taking a flatter model to the board, the CIO should require one decision package with three parts. The operating proof shows complete-workflow results against a baseline, including human effort, errors, controls, and total cost. The authority contract names the domain owner, permitted agent actions, exception approver, stop authority, audit record, and rollback path. The people commitment explains which work changes, which human responsibilities remain, and how careers, consultation, training, and transition will be handled.

The change should also be staged so that the organization can reverse it when evidence deteriorates. If agents remove real coordination while the controls hold, fewer layers and stronger domain ownership become defensible. If the queues merely move or hidden repair work grows, the workflow needs another redesign before the hierarchy does.

An AI-first company is defined by how work and authority are rebuilt around the technology's actual capabilities. Its flatter shape, when justified, is the consequence of that operating proof.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>A board-defensible redesign requires complete-workflow proof, an explicit authority contract, a credible people transition, and a reversible path. Only then should a CIO change the hierarchy.</p>
</div>
