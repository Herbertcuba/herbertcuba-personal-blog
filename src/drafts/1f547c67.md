*For a chief information officer (CIO), the immediate decision is to build distributed technical judgment; the later decision is whether the evidence justifies a different hierarchy.*

An AI-first company changes two things together: who can exercise technical judgment and how work moves through the organization. In a [June 2026 recorded conversation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ), Arvind KC, chief people officer at OpenAI, the artificial intelligence company behind the ChatGPT assistant, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately placed that role inside a domain, meaning a business area with its own knowledge and accountability.

That observation supports the first originating claim. Sales, finance, operations, marketing, and support all need people who can judge what generative AI can do reliably, where it can fail, and when a person must decide. My second claim is organizational: when that judgment sits closer to the work, more improvement can originate at the edges of the company instead of waiting for a central programme to design every use.

A flatter company can follow, but it is not the starting assumption. An AI agent, in this article, means software that uses a generative AI model to interpret information, choose steps, and call approved tools, meaning connected business systems or functions that retrieve data or take an action, toward an outcome. Agents may absorb routine execution and coordination. The hierarchy should change only after complete-workflow evidence shows that the coordination has actually gone, while authority, cost, control, and the people transition remain defensible.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Distributed technical judgment is a capability to build now. A flatter hierarchy is a separate structural decision that agents must earn through operating evidence.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment belongs in the business

Technical judgment means understanding the boundary between a useful model response and an authorized business outcome. Programming remains specialist work. A salesperson should know when an AI-drafted proposal makes a claim the company cannot support, while a finance controller should be able to ask what evidence sits behind a suggested classification. An operations planner should recognize when an exception falls outside the data the agent has seen.

The same principle applies in marketing and support: the person closest to the domain defines quality, spots missing context, and knows which consequences deserve escalation. Technology teams still build shared platforms, security, and controls, but they cannot supply every piece of domain judgment from the centre.

This changes how improvement travels. A domain team can shape an agent-supported workflow inside approved boundaries, test it against its own cases, and own the result. From a central office, that can look less orderly than one transformation queue. Yet distributed ownership remains controlled when the company makes permissions, evidence requirements, and stop authority common and visible.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Every role gains a technical dimension because business specialists must judge AI reliability and consequences in their own domain. Shared controls stay central; useful ownership moves closer to the work.</p>
</div>

## A faster task can leave the company unchanged

Business author Benjamin Simkin calls the habit of attaching AI to an old operating structure the “Retrofit Trap” in [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5). The phrase matters because task speed and organizational change are easy to confuse.

Consider a request for a non-standard customer discount. An agent can gather account information and draft an exception note in seconds. If a sales manager still checks the source data, finance re-enters the numbers, legal reviews a routine clause, and several people chase the approval status, the surrounding coordination remains. One activity became faster; the company did not become AI-first.

Part of a hierarchy exists to move information and exceptions. In his 1974 paper [“Organization Design: An Information Processing View”](https://doi.org/10.1287/inte.4.3.28), organization theorist Jay R. Galbraith described recurring situations being handled through rules while unusual situations moved upward to a level with a broader view. Modern managers do more than route cases, but routing, status gathering, approval chasing, and first-pass repair still consume real capacity.

An agent-supported workflow may gather evidence, compare a request with policy, prepare a recommendation, route a routine case, and keep its status current. Human owners still set pricing policy, accept material risk, decide novel exceptions, and stop the process. Flattening becomes plausible only when the first category shrinks without smuggling the second into a model.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up one activity while preserving the handoffs around it. A flatter structure becomes possible only when agents remove routine coordination without inheriting human authority.</p>
</div>

## Make the hierarchy earn its redesign

The unit of evidence should be the complete workflow, from an incoming request to a recorded and usable outcome, including exceptions, correction, and follow-up. Within that workflow, a correctly closed case is one that reaches the intended result, meets policy and quality requirements, and is recorded in the official system. An agent declaring success is not enough.

I would ask for a baseline and a comparable set of cases before approving any structural inference. The scorecard below is a proposed local operating test, not an established benchmark:

| Board question | Evidence to collect |
| --- | --- |
| Did coordination fall? | Human routing touches, handoffs, approval waits, and status-chasing time across the full workflow |
| Did outcomes improve? | Correctly closed cases, elapsed time, reopened cases, and quality errors |
| Did control hold? | Unauthorized attempts, policy breaches, unresolved outcomes, overrides, and stop events |
| Did the economics improve? | Cost per correctly closed case, including internal review and repair time, supplier fees, infrastructure, monitoring, training, and the expected cost of supplier exit or returning to the previous process |
| What still needs a person? | The type, frequency, and consequence of exceptions that require human judgment |

The delivery team should not grade itself alone. A named owner in risk, internal audit, or operations should inspect a sample against the official records and report failures as carefully as successes. The trial also needs enough ordinary and unusual cases to reveal where the workflow breaks; one smooth demonstration says little about an operating model.

No Swedish or Norwegian numerical threshold is offered here because none is established in the verified material available for this article. The CIO therefore needs locally approved pass and stop values, set before the trial begins. If repair work moves to another team, costs rise after supplier pricing changes, or managers continue handling the same exceptions off-system, the evidence does not support removing a layer.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Measure the recorded outcome of the whole workflow, including hidden human work and full cost. Local thresholds and independent checks must precede any conclusion about hierarchy.</p>
</div>

## Put authority where the model cannot move it

A responsible replacement architecture begins with a domain map. For each consequential decision, it names the business owner, the governing policy, the data and tools the workflow may use, the permitted outcomes, and the person who owns exceptions. The end-to-end process owner remains accountable for performance after the trial, with a fixed review schedule and a named budget owner for monitoring, repair, supplier costs, and eventual exit.

The technical boundary should be equally plain. The agent interprets a request and proposes the next step. Ordinary rules-based software verifies identity, enforces permissions, records the authoritative state, and commits external actions. Authoritative state means the official answer to where a case stands, rather than the model's account of what it thinks happened. Durable workflow software can record and resume each step, while access-control software limits what each identity may do; AI does not replace those established controls.

A retry after a network failure must not create a second consequential action. Engineers call this property [idempotency](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods): repeating the same request should have its intended effect once. A request identifier helps only when the receiving system enforces uniqueness, rejects reuse with changed details, and binds the request, effect, and stored result as one indivisible update. If an external system cannot do that, the workflow should record the intended action before sending it and then verify the result. An unknown outcome pauses for checking against the official record; it does not trigger a blind retry.

For the discount example, even a small proposed state map makes that boundary visible:

| Current state | Permitted next move | Rejected move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discount request received | Check the required evidence | Issue a customer offer |
| Exception approved by its human owner | Issue one offer with the approved terms | Change the terms during issue |
| Outcome unknown after an issue attempt | Check the official quoting system and resolve the case | Retry the issue blindly |

Inputs need boundaries too. A prompt injection is a hidden or misleading instruction inside a document, message, or webpage that tries to redirect the model. Such input must not expand tool permissions, disclose protected data, or authorize an external effect. The audit record should show the input, model and policy version, actions requested through approved tools, human intervention, and final recorded outcome. Named people need the power to override, stop, restart, and roll back the workflow; rollback means returning to the last safe process or system state.

For a European business, this map also supports legal assessment. The [European Union (EU) AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj), assigns duties according to an AI system's role and risk, while the [General Data Protection Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj), applied since 2018, governs personal-data processing and places conditions on some solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects. The exact duties depend on the use case. A commercial discount, an employment decision, and a credit decision should not inherit the same approval path merely because all three use AI.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Business owners decide policy and exceptions; rules-based software enforces permissions, official state, and consequential actions; agents interpret and propose within those limits. Audit, stop, rollback, review, and supplier exit need named owners.</p>
</div>

## A flatter structure rewrites the people deal

Visible authority matters just as much to the workforce. If agents genuinely absorb routing, status collection, routine checking, and repair, coordination-heavy roles will change and some may disappear. Yet management also carries coaching, conflict resolution, priority setting, context, and accountability. Those duties need a new home before a reporting layer is removed.

The old pyramid gave many employees a familiar route to progression: become a manager, gain direct reports, and move upward. A flatter company needs credible alternatives. Domain mastery, end-to-end workflow ownership, risk judgment, coaching, and responsibility for reviewing agent performance should have defined levels, pay ranges, and promotion criteria. Otherwise the organization removes rungs from the ladder while continuing to praise ambition.

The transition plan belongs in the operating case. It should map affected work rather than rely only on job titles, state which skills the future roles require, reserve paid learning time, fund redeployment, and explain selection or exit decisions honestly. Dedicated funding must remain available through the trial, review, repair, and transition, rather than ending when the software goes live.

For a Nordic company, participation begins during design. In Sweden, the [1976 Co-Determination in the Workplace Act](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-1976580-om-medbestammande-i-arbetslivet_sfs-1976-580/) creates information and negotiation duties in specified situations involving significant workplace changes. Legal duties vary by country and circumstance, but the leadership principle is broader: involve employee representatives while the workflow and choices can still change. A frictionless promise would be dishonest. A visible process, funded options, and clear accountability make the transition credible.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Removing coordination layers also removes career rungs and redistributes coaching and accountability. Role design, pay, learning, redeployment, employee participation, and honest exit terms are part of the architecture.</p>
</div>

## The CIO should authorize two decisions, not one

The first decision is whether to run a bounded operating-model trial. Its authorization record should name the workflow and end-to-end owner, the baseline and comparable cases, local pass and stop values, permitted tools and outcomes, exception and stop authority, regulatory review, supplier exit path, workforce commitments, ongoing review schedule, and the budget owner. It should also set a date when the executive team will choose to stop, extend, or scale the workflow.

The second decision is whether the resulting evidence supports structural change. That case should show that routine coordination has fallen across the complete workflow, quality and control remain acceptable, total cost improves, and the remaining human judgment has a clear owner. It should also show that these results persist across the volume and variation the business expects, rather than appearing only in a hand-picked trial.

This sequence preserves the ambition of the AI-first company without turning it into a headcount thesis. Technical judgment can spread immediately. Agents can create room for more bottom-up ownership, and some organizations may eventually need fewer layers. The board should approve that last step only when the operating record and the people commitments make it a defensible consequence, not a target in search of evidence.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Authorize capability and structural change at separate gates. The org chart changes only after the full operating record proves that coordination has gone, authority remains accountable, economics hold, and the workforce transition is funded.</p>
</div>
