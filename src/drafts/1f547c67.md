An AI-first company begins with two linked claims. Every business role acquires a technical dimension as people decide where AI can act reliably, where it needs supervision, and where human judgment must prevail. If agents then absorb meaningful execution and coordination, ownership can move closer to the work and the organization may become flatter.

An AI agent is software that uses a generative model to interpret a goal and take steps through connected tools within defined limits. That definition matters because drafting an answer is a task improvement; moving a customer case through checks, decisions, and recovery is an operating model. The second can change the work previously carried by hierarchy, but only if the whole system performs under real conditions.

For a CIO, this makes organizational redesign a separate decision from AI deployment. Remove a coordination layer too early and unresolved exceptions simply move to already busy specialists. Keep every approval and handoff after the workflow has proved it can carry them, and the company pays for both the old structure and the new system.

<p>AI-first architecture links distributed technical judgment to a conditional possibility: the company may flatten when agent-supported workflows demonstrably absorb execution and coordination.</p>

## Technical judgment moves into the business

[Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), chief people officer at OpenAI, an artificial intelligence company, said in a [May 2026 interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ) that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately qualified the point: the technical role is applied to a particular domain. The same conversation presents AI innovation as increasingly bottom-up, with useful applications emerging close to the work rather than only through a central program.

Technical judgment does not require every employee to become a programmer. It means knowing enough to challenge what the system is doing. A salesperson should recognize when an agent has turned an assumption into a customer promise; a finance professional should know which exception needs accountable approval; and a support specialist should see when an apparently correct answer creates a relationship or conduct risk.

That capability changes where ownership can sit. Domain experts can shape workflows, test failures, and improve local decisions without waiting for a technology team to translate every need. From the center, many domains changing their own workflows can look like disorder; the difference between an empowered edge and unmanaged chaos is whether outcome ownership, interfaces, and escalation rights remain visible. I treat KC’s observation as a signal for this shift, while the claim that management layers can be removed remains an organizational hypothesis that must earn its evidence.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Technical judgment is the business ability to test AI against domain reality; it supports bottom-up ownership without turning every role into a coding role or proving that hierarchy is obsolete.</p>
</div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the old work

The product description for Benjamin Simkin’s 2026 book, [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls the act of bolting AI onto an old structure the “Retrofit Trap.” The term is useful because it directs attention beyond the automated step to the routing, approvals, and repairs around it.

Consider an illustrative business-customer onboarding workflow. An agent may read a signed agreement and prepare account data in seconds. Yet the operating model has barely moved if operations staff re-enter that data, risk decisions travel through chat, a manager chases missing approvals, and billing repairs the first invoice after the account goes live.

Traditional hierarchy does more than exercise judgment. It also gathers information, allocates work, checks status, routes exceptions, and confirms that somebody has acted. An agent-supported workflow can potentially absorb evidence gathering, routine follow-up, status reporting, and routing under declared rules. Leaders should retain accountable human authority for policy exceptions, material trade-offs, people decisions, coaching, and any action whose consequences the company is unwilling to delegate.

The distinction is practical: task speed says little about organizational capacity. A company becomes AI-first when the surrounding workflow changes and the old coordination burden actually falls, rather than being hidden in a different team.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Retrofit Trap accelerates a visible task while preserving manual coordination; an AI-first model must remove work across the complete workflow without delegating accountable judgment by accident.</p>
</div>

## Flattening needs operating proof

The right unit of evidence is a complete workflow, from an accepted business request to a valid business outcome, including its exception paths. For onboarding, that could run from an approved agreement to an active account and a correct first invoice. A correctly closed case is one where the outcome is valid, required controls passed, business records agree, and no unrecorded repair is waiting downstream.

Measure elapsed time, human routing touches, repair effort, outcome quality, control failures, and total cost across that full path. Total cost includes model use, software, oversight, exception handling, and the capacity kept for recovery. The review should also identify residual judgment: the decisions, relationships, and coaching that still require people even when the case closes correctly.

No Swedish or Norwegian production case in the material available for this article establishes a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer. That absence should prevent borrowed certainty, not local experimentation. Executives can set local acceptance limits before a controlled trial begins, expose difficult cases to the workflow, and refuse to rewrite the limits after seeing the result.

A useful decision record can be completed without pretending to offer a universal benchmark. In an illustrative onboarding trial, production might receive conditional approval after eligible cases close correctly and an exercised stop restores manual handling with its history intact. Structural change would still be withheld if exceptions continue to return through team leads or if coaching demand has not been measured. The record should carry the evidence date, system and model versions, named owners, unresolved conditions, and the date of the next review.

The commitment sequence matters. The business and risk owners first agree the outcome, forbidden actions, exception path, and stop conditions. Technology and finance then fix the system boundary, continuity plan, and cost accounting; people leadership and employee representatives map role consequences before production approval. Only after the evidence is reviewed should executive leadership consider changing reporting layers.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Judge correctly closed workflows, full cost, exceptions, and residual human work against local rules set in advance; production approval and organizational redesign require different evidence and different decisions.</p>
</div>

## Authority must be visible in the system

A flatter model needs stronger ownership and controlled complexity, not vague autonomy. Start with a domain map, meaning a set of bounded business areas such as customer onboarding, and assign an accountable business owner to each outcome. Decision rights then state who may approve an exception, change a rule, override the workflow, stop it, or return to manual operation.

The agent should interpret documents, assemble evidence, and propose or perform actions inside those rights. Ordinary software should enforce permissions and preserve the authoritative state, the single accepted record of where a case currently stands. Workflow systems that preserve progress across interruptions can manage [state, checkpoints, retries, and recovery](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-functions/durable/durable-functions-overview), while [policy software](https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs/latest/) can apply declared permission rules without asking a generative model to invent them each time.

The onboarding state makes that boundary concrete. After risk clearance, the system may allow a case to move to account creation. It should reject a jump from “agreement received” directly to “billing active,” even if an agent argues that the customer looks safe. Idempotency means that a retry cannot create the same business effect twice: the workflow must store a unique request key and its account-creation result together, then return that recorded result on a retry. The key alone does not provide the control.

Audit history should show the evidence, rule, actor, model version, and resulting state for each consequential action. Stop authority is the named power to halt new actions; an override is an accountable decision to take a permitted exceptional path; and rollback means returning to the last valid state or issuing a correcting action when the original effect cannot literally be undone.

These controls need rehearsal. Before production, the team should stop the workflow during a live-like case, preserve the trace, restore the manual route, and recover from the last valid state. The technology owner should own supplier substitution and retained history, the risk owner should approve material model or rule changes, and the business owner should keep the manual route usable. If coordination demand returns after a structural change, the chief operating officer needs explicit authority to restore capacity rather than wait for another reorganization.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Domain owners hold accountability, agents act within declared limits, and ordinary software enforces state and permissions; exercised stop, recovery, audit, and continuity paths make that authority real.</p>
</div>

## The old pyramid also carried careers

Removing coordination work changes more than headcount. The management pyramid has also distributed coaching, status, pay progression, access to senior decisions, and a story about ambition. If leadership removes layers without rebuilding those functions, the company may gain a cleaner chart while weakening the system that develops judgment.

Coordination-heavy roles therefore need a task-level review. Routine status collection and handoff management may shrink, while conflict resolution, capability building, difficult judgment, and accountability remain. Some managers can move toward domain ownership, expert coaching, or control stewardship; other roles will change substantially or disappear. A credible plan says this plainly.

The replacement should include expert career paths with pay and status that do not depend on accumulating direct reports. It should also fund paid learning, role matching, redeployment, and fair exits where no durable role exists. Employee representatives should enter while the operating model is still open to change, especially in a Nordic company, because participation after the structural decision has been made cannot shape the decision.

This people work belongs in the business case. The expected operating benefit, transition cost, temporary capacity, and effect on coaching must be reviewed together. Otherwise, apparent efficiency in one budget can reappear as slower learning, weak succession, and overloaded domain owners somewhere else.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A flatter company must replace the career, coaching, learning, and progression functions carried by the old pyramid, with funded transitions and early employee participation rather than a frictionless headcount assumption.</p>

## Make the org chart the last approval

The executive team should ask two separate questions. First: may this workflow run in production within its agreed scope? Second: has it removed enough recurring coordination, over a long enough local evidence period, to justify changing roles or reporting layers? A yes to the first does not answer the second.

Before approving structural change, the board or executive committee should receive the dated workflow evidence, signed decision rights, results of the stop-and-recovery drill, supplier and model continuity commitments, full operating cost, and a funded workforce plan. It should also see where residual judgment and coaching will sit after the change. These are local decision requirements, not published benchmarks.

The post-change review needs an owner before the chart changes. The chief operating officer can own that review, with the CIO supplying workflow and control evidence, the people leader reporting capacity and coaching load, and the risk owner reporting exceptions and failures. On the agreed review date, the chief operating officer should be able to restore team-lead coverage, manual routing, or other coordination capacity if repair work and unresolved exceptions return.

My conclusion is conditional but firm. Agents can support a materially flatter company when they carry execution and coordination that managers previously had to carry. Leadership earns the redesign by proving the work, exposing authority, and protecting the organization’s ability to recover, including the people whose working lives are being changed.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Approve the workflow first and the structural change separately; require dated evidence, signed authority, tested recovery, funded workforce commitments, and a named executive who can restore coordination capacity.</p>
</div>
