Count the human coordination that remains after an AI agent finishes. That reveals more about organizational change than the quality of a single output. A company can automate drafting or analysis and still preserve every queue, approval, and unclear handoff around the result.

By AI-first, I mean arranging work around capabilities that artificial intelligence can demonstrate reliably, with people positioned where judgment is still necessary. OpenAI, the company behind the generative AI assistant ChatGPT, describes one implementation in its [Agents SDK documentation for developers](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/): an agent combines a language model with instructions and permitted tools, then works through a sequence until the task finishes or human involvement is required. Organization design enters the picture when the system can move an entire case forward while preserving its context.

I think the strongest case for a flatter company is therefore conditional. A layer can become less useful when agents remove the routine coordination that supported it, while people close to the work gain enough technical judgment and authority to handle what remains. The public material behind this argument does not show that companies have already removed management layers safely at scale. It gives leaders a mechanism they can test before changing the company around it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The useful measure of an agent is how much human routing, repair, and follow-up remain around completed work. A flatter structure becomes plausible only when that coordination falls and the company rebuilds judgment, authority, evidence, and careers around the new workflow.</p></div>

## Begin with a case that can close

In his independently published May 2026 book, *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, author Benjamin Simkin calls it the “Retrofit Trap” when a company adds AI to a process designed for manual work but leaves the surrounding structure intact. The tool becomes faster while people still move information between systems, seek approval, repair mistakes, and record the result. Activity changes, but the operating burden survives.

Consider a customer refund request as a bounded example. An agent-supported workflow could retrieve the order and policy, check whether the request falls inside an approved limit, request missing evidence, issue a permitted refund, update the account, and send a final explanation. A case outside policy would move to a named owner with the relevant records attached, then wait for that person’s decision.

The stages matter because they reveal whether the system has really carried the work. A request might move from received to checked, then either to approved or to exception review, and finally to paid and closed. Software should reject an attempt to move an exception directly to paid before the accountable owner resolves it. That single blocked transition says more about production readiness than a fluent model response because it shows that authority survives the automation.

This discipline predates generative AI. Conventional workflow software already stores the condition of a case and enforces which step may follow; the model adds flexible interpretation inside those boundaries rather than replacing them.

Now the structural test becomes clearer. If employees still search for records, chase the exception owner, correct most decisions, or close the case in another system, the agent has automated a task while the company still pays for coordination. A management layer has only lost part of its function when that routing work disappears in normal operation, including the awkward cases.

Managers also coach people, resolve conflict, allocate scarce resources, and develop judgment. Those functions do not vanish because a refund workflow improves. I think leaders should separate them from routine routing before making any claim about flattening, then ask which responsibilities still require a role and which were artifacts of fragmented information.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Test a complete, bounded unit of work rather than an isolated AI task. Coordination has genuinely fallen only when the workflow carries evidence, permissions, exceptions, decisions, and closure without people quietly rebuilding the missing links.</p></div>

## Technical judgment has to travel with the work

Arvind KC is chief people officer at OpenAI. During a [May 2026 discussion with DECODE](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s), a community for founders, he said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” His point is broader than programming: people need enough understanding to judge the information a system receives, the actions it is allowed to take, the checks applied to its output, and the ways it can break.

For the refund workflow, the customer operations owner needs to understand which records count as evidence, which policy limits are fixed, and which cases require human judgment. Engineers remain responsible for reliable execution, access controls, and the tools the agent can use. Neither side can define a sound workflow alone because business judgment without technical understanding creates vague rules, while technical implementation without business ownership turns policy into guesswork.

This shared judgment changes the role of a central technology team. It should provide dependable systems, common controls, and specialist help for hard problems. If every local exception still travels to engineering for interpretation, however, the company has rebuilt a coordination queue around the people who understand the technology.

Each resolved exception can also improve the next cycle. A correction can become a test case, a policy can become clearer, and a repeated failure can trigger a tighter permission or a different route. I think this is where a durable advantage can develop: the company does not merely finish more work, but leaves behind a workflow that is easier to inspect and improve.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical responsibility spreads when business decisions depend on what an AI system knows, what it can do, and how mistakes surface. Judgment needs to remain close to the work, while engineering supplies reliable foundations instead of becoming the new approval queue.</p></div>

## A flatter company needs a visible constitution

Removing a handoff redistributes power rather than removing it. Decision rights spell out who can authorize an action, when an override is valid, who holds emergency stop authority, and who remains accountable for the outcome. In a conventional hierarchy, some of that power is visible in reporting lines. In an agent-supported workflow, it can disappear into permissions and thresholds unless leaders make it deliberate.

The owner of the business outcome should set the operating rule, and the engineering team should make its execution dependable. A model can propose an outcome, while ordinary software blocks payments above a set limit and other actions that carry high cost, legal consequence, or no easy reversal. Each material fact needs a named system of record, meaning the source that employees and software treat as definitive. The action log should show what happened, which evidence mattered, and why a person or the agent made the decision.

Law and supplier risk remain part of that design. In the European Union, the [General Data Protection Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj), or GDPR, governs the processing of personal data, while the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) sets obligations according to how an AI system is used and the risk it creates. A legal owner must determine which duties apply to the actual workflow. Supplier terms should also make clear what data the service can access, where it is handled, how long it is retained, and how access can be withdrawn.

Authority is only half of the human design. Coordination roles have also carried pay, status, exposure to difficult decisions, and a familiar route into leadership. If software removes routine routing, a company needs other ways for people to gain broader responsibility and prove that they are ready for it. Deeper specialist work, accountability for a larger or riskier business outcome, and a formal role in developing other people’s judgment can all support progression, but only when titles, compensation, and funded learning recognize them.

I think this requires a decision forum rather than a quiet sequence of technology choices. Before a trial, the business sponsor, operations owner, engineering, HR, finance, and employee representatives where applicable should agree on the work boundary, stop authority, and likely effect on roles. After the evidence is reviewed independently, the same group can decide whether to expand the workflow. Any change to responsibilities, learning commitments, titles, or pay should be agreed before the org chart moves.

I don’t know what share of people now paid to coordinate work will want, or be able, to take on deeper expertise or wider ownership. There will be people who grow into the new responsibilities, people who pursue a different path, and jobs whose central purpose no longer exists. Leadership becomes credible when it states those possibilities early and shares the cost of transition instead of presenting a smaller structure as neutral progress.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A flatter company needs visible decision rights, enforceable limits, inspectable records, applicable legal and supplier controls, and a named person who can stop the workflow. It also needs funded, recognized routes to responsibility for people whose old career ladder depended on coordination.</p></div>

## Run the coordination test

A useful trial should be difficult to reinterpret after the results arrive. Its brief needs to name the sponsor, the exact workflow, the current process used as a comparison, a fixed time period or case count, and a full cost envelope that includes software, review, repair, and oversight. It should also identify the person who approves the trial, the independent verifier, the decision forum, and the route back to the previous process.

The success thresholds should be numerical and agreed before launch. They should cover the time to a correctly closed case, the rate of wrong outcomes, the share of cases requiring human repair, serious control failures, and full cost per valid result. A stop trigger should follow the consequence of the workflow: an action beyond an approved financial limit, access outside the stated permission, or another defined breach can end the trial immediately rather than becoming an average hidden inside a dashboard.

Verification should examine the business outcome, not merely whether software marked a case closed. The verifier needs enough records to confirm that valid cases reached the right result, exceptions reached the right owner, prohibited transitions were rejected, and action logs can reconstruct what happened. Thresholds will differ by workflow, which is precisely why they must come from the operating baseline rather than from a vendor’s general benchmark.

One successful trial still says little about an entire management layer. Leaders need to repeat the test across several workflows, watch whether repair work returns under pressure, and separate routine coordination from coaching, resource allocation, and judgment. I think the chart should change only when this evidence shows that a responsibility has moved or disappeared, and when the new authority and career system already works in practice.

My take is that coordination is the practical test for an AI-first company. Agents change the organization when they remove routine routing without burying authority or pushing repair work onto invisible human hands. A flatter chart may follow, but completed work with accountable decisions is the proof.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Lock the baseline, time horizon, numerical thresholds, approver, independent verifier, stop trigger, and rollback route before the trial begins. An AI-first company earns a new shape only after repeated evidence shows that routine coordination is gone and its human responsibilities have a credible new home.</p></div>
