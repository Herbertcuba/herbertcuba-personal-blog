An AI-first company begins with a hard design question: if software can carry both a task and the coordination around it, which parts of the existing organization still earn their place?

Here, AI-first means designing work around what artificial intelligence can reliably do, rather than adding an AI feature to a process built for manual operation. An [AI agent](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/) is software that gives an AI model instructions and access to approved tools, then lets it continue through several steps until the task is complete or a person needs to intervene. That ability to act across a workflow matters more to organization design than the ability to produce a clever answer.

In a [May 2026 conversation hosted by DECODE](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s), a founder community, Arvind KC, chief people officer at OpenAI, the company behind the generative AI assistant ChatGPT, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” Author Benjamin Simkin approaches the same shift from the process side in his independently published May 2026 book, *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*. He calls the habit of adding AI while retaining the old manual workflow the “Retrofit Trap.”

I think the two ideas lead to one structural argument. As agents take on execution and routine coordination, technical judgment has to spread through the company and parts of the hierarchy can become unnecessary. A flatter chart is only the visible result, however. The real redesign concerns who understands the system, who may decide, and how people progress when coordination is no longer the main route to seniority.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents change organization design when they carry work and its routine coordination from start to finish. That can support a flatter company, provided technical judgment, human authority, and career progression are redesigned with the workflow.</p></div>

## Every role gains a technical dimension

KC’s claim extends technical work beyond programming. Technical literacy means understanding what information an AI system uses, what it can do with that information, where its permissions end, and how failure becomes visible. People need that knowledge at the level of the decisions they own.

A sales leader should be able to judge which parts of a funnel can run automatically and where a human conversation still matters. An operations leader needs to understand when a classification is reliable enough to move work forward. Finance should be able to separate an investment that improves a reusable process from one that merely produces more activity. None of these responsibilities requires writing code, but all of them now include reasoning about software.

This changes the relationship with a central technology team. Sending every commercial or operational question to engineers simply recreates the old routing layer around them. Engineers remain responsible for system reliability, while people in each business area need enough technical understanding to judge outcomes and define what good looks like.

I think this shared language is the first condition of an AI-first company. AI scales whatever the organization gives it, including vague policy, poor data, and unresolved disagreement. Technical literacy lets the people closest to a decision improve those inputs before the system reproduces their weaknesses at speed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every role becomes technical because more business decisions depend on understanding an AI system’s evidence, limits, permissions, and failures. Shared technical literacy keeps judgment close to the work instead of rebuilding a central queue around engineering.</p></div>

## Coordination is what holds the pyramid up

A management pyramid carries more than authority. It also carries information: managers collect status, translate between teams, route requests, chase approvals, and return decisions to the people doing the work. Those activities became layers because fragmented tools and incomplete context made direct coordination unreliable.

Agents can alter that mechanism because they can preserve context while moving a case through several steps. Consider the routine finance work of matching invoices to purchase orders and payments. An agent could retrieve the approved records, flag a mismatch outside a fixed tolerance, send that exception to the accountable finance owner, record the decision, and close the case. The workflow has clear stages such as received, matched, escalated, resolved, and closed, and software enforces which transitions are allowed.

The important result is completed work. If people still gather missing records, repair most matches, chase the exception owner, and update the system afterwards, the organization has automated one analytical step while leaving its coordination burden intact. That distinction explains why a polished demonstration says little about whether a management layer has lost its function.

A real test needs a bounded workflow, an agreed comparison with the current process, and a fixed observation period. Before the trial begins, the sponsor should set acceptable thresholds for service time, human repair, incorrect outcomes, serious exceptions, and full cost. Someone independent of the workflow should verify completion, while a stop condition and a route back to the previous process keep the change reversible.

The material cited here does not establish that companies can safely remove management layers at scale. It supports a mechanism that leaders can test. Coaching, conflict resolution, resource choices, and the development of other people may continue to justify managers even when routine routing no longer does.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Hierarchy partly compensates for broken information flow. A layer becomes less useful only when an agent-supported workflow reliably carries records, actions, exceptions, and closure, as measured against predeclared operating and cost thresholds.</p></div>

## Authority makes a flat company real

Removing handoffs does not remove power. It moves power into permissions, data sources, thresholds, and escalation rules. Decision rights make that power explicit by stating who may approve an action, override the system, stop the workflow, and answer for the result.

The person who owns a business outcome should set the policy boundary, while engineers protect the reliability of the system that applies it. An AI model may recommend an action, but a fixed rule should enforce limits around expensive, regulated, or irreversible steps. When the model encounters a genuine exception, the workflow should show the evidence, route the case to a named human, and wait for a decision.

The same design needs an authoritative source for each important fact, a readable record of every action and its reason, and clear limits on what an outside software supplier may access. Applicable legal and regulatory duties still govern the workflow. Most importantly, one person must have practical stop authority when the evidence, supplier, or system can no longer be trusted.

A company can remove boxes from its chart while concentrating control in settings that few employees can see or challenge. I think a genuinely flatter organization gives people near the work wider room to act and makes the boundary around that room easier to inspect. The resulting surface may look less tidy because local teams make more decisions. Useful variation still sits inside shared rules and clear ownership.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Flatter structures need visible authority, not invisible control in software. Business owners define policy, engineers protect reliability, records make decisions inspectable, and a named person retains the power to stop the workflow.</p></div>

## The advantage compounds through reusable work

Once knowledge of a business area and the ability to direct agents sit in the same role, each improvement can become part of the next cycle of work. An editor might turn recurring feedback into a reusable check before a draft reaches a person. A finance analyst might refine the rules that identify unusual transactions, reducing the time spent reviewing ordinary ones without relaxing control.

The effect compounds when better instructions, cleaner data, and recorded corrections remain inside the workflow. The next task starts from a stronger system, and the time or money saved can fund another improvement. Simkin’s strategic point is useful here: redesign creates a continuing return when recorded corrections improve the process itself. A faster tool inside the old sequence leaves less behind.

Any productivity gain remains an empirical question because agents can repeat a weak rule more consistently and spread bad information further. I think the durable edge comes from people who can improve the system as part of doing their jobs, then verify whether the change helped. The deeper gain is an increase in the number of decisions a company can make well without adding another approval layer.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agent-supported work compounds when corrections improve a reusable workflow and each cycle begins with better instructions, data, and controls. Technically literate people still have to judge the result, and any productivity gain has to be measured.</p></div>

## A flat company needs a different career ladder

The human problem begins where the structural argument becomes convincing. Management roles carry coordination, but they also offer higher pay, wider influence, experience in developing others, and a recognized path through a career. Removing routing work without replacing those benefits turns organization design into a cost cut with better language.

Routine coordination also trains future leaders. It exposes people to competing priorities, difficult exceptions, and the consequences of decisions across a business. When software absorbs that work, companies need deliberate ways to build the judgment that those assignments once developed.

Progress can come through ownership of a more consequential business area, deeper specialist skill, responsibility for higher-risk decisions, or a formal role in improving other people’s judgment. Those paths need titles, pay, and evidence of achievement. Because the organization is changing the work, it should fund learning and share the transition cost with people whose roles were designed for the previous system.

The change also needs a coalition with real authority. The business owner and operations team should define which work ends and which responsibilities remain human. HR and finance need to connect the new scope to learning, titles, and compensation, while employees or their representatives should shape the transition before decisions become irreversible.

I don’t know how many people whose careers center on coordination will want, or be able, to move into deeper expertise or broader ownership. Some will grow into the new work, some will choose another path, and some roles will end because their core function has disappeared. Serious leadership means stating those possibilities before presenting a flatter company as inevitable progress.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>When agents remove routine coordination, they also remove familiar routes to skill, status, pay, and leadership. Redesign those routes with funded learning, credible recognition, employee participation, and honest choices before removing layers from the chart.</p></div>

## The architecture becomes the strategy

The org chart should be one of the last things to change. Begin with a complete workflow, prove that routine coordination has actually fallen, expose the new decision rights, and watch the result long enough to see repairs and exceptions. A single successful workflow trial provides too little evidence for a reorganization, but it can show whether the mechanism deserves a broader test.

This sequence also clarifies Simkin’s warning about the Retrofit Trap. Adding an agent to one task preserves the surrounding delays and approvals. Redesigning the workflow asks which steps remain necessary once software can carry context, action, and exception handling together. Evidence from the work then determines whether a layer still serves a purpose.

My take is that the architecture of an AI-first company is defined by the shortest responsible path between evidence and decision. I think that path will often be flatter because agents can carry routine execution and coordination. It will only be better when people close to the work understand the technology, the authority inside it can be challenged, and those whose roles change have a credible future.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Test the workflow before redrawing the company. An AI-first organization earns a flatter shape when software has already made the old routing layer unnecessary and the replacement gives people clearer judgment, authority, accountability, and opportunity.</p></div>
