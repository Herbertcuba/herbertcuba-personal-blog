An AI-first company changes two things at once. Technical judgment spreads into every business role. At the same time, AI agents can take on enough execution and coordination for ownership to move closer to the work, which may allow the company to operate with fewer layers. An AI agent is software that uses a generative model to interpret a goal, choose steps, and work through approved tools with some autonomy.

In a recorded DECODE interview, Arvind KC, then a people leader at OpenAI, the AI research and product company behind ChatGPT, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He explained that this technical dimension is applied within a person’s domain. KC also portrayed innovation inside OpenAI as bottom-up, an observation about one unusual company rather than proof that delayering is safe elsewhere.

The two propositions are linked, but they are not interchangeable. Distributed technical judgment makes more local ownership possible. It does not show that the information, approval, coaching, and exception work inside a management layer has disappeared. I think a responsible CIO changes the structure only after the company can prove that complete workflows close correctly, authority remains visible, complexity is controlled, and affected employees have a credible path through the change.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>An AI-first company can distribute technical judgment and support more bottom-up ownership. A flatter structure still has to be earned through operating evidence, visible authority, and a funded workforce transition.</p>
</div>

## Start with a complete business case

Technical judgment is the ability to decide what an AI system should do, where it is reliable, how its output will be tested, and when a person must take over. Sales, operations, finance, marketing, and support teams need this capability in their own domains. They do not all need to become programmers, but they do need to understand the boundary between a plausible response and a valid business outcome.

Take supplier onboarding. A procurement specialist knows why beneficial ownership, sanctions screening, security evidence, payment terms, and local exceptions matter. An agent may gather documents, identify missing fields, and prepare a recommendation. The specialist must still recognize conflicting evidence, judge an exception, and accept responsibility for the decision. That division of work is a technical design choice made inside the business role.

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 book *The AI First Company*, calls it the “Retrofit Trap” when a company adds AI to an existing process without changing the process itself. A generated supplier summary may save time, yet employees can remain responsible for chasing requests, copying data, finding approvers, checking the final record, and repairing failed handoffs. The task improved while the operating model stayed in place.

The useful unit of redesign is therefore a complete business case, from the initial request to an approved or rejected supplier, a valid master record, and clear communication to the requester. A correctly closed case reaches the right outcome with the required evidence, permissions, approvals, records, and communication intact. That standard forces leaders to examine the surrounding work instead of celebrating one faster step.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Technical judgment belongs in each business domain, while the unit of redesign is the complete case. Escaping the Retrofit Trap means removing surrounding routing and repair, not merely accelerating one task.</p>
</div>

## A layer is a bundle of work

An organization chart shows reporting relationships, but every layer also performs recurring work. It compresses information, tracks status, grants approval, routes exceptions, resolves conflict, coaches people, and carries accountability. Before removing a box, a leader needs to separate that bundle into work that ends, work that technology takes on, work that moves to a domain owner, and work that still requires a manager.

Agents can gather evidence, prepare a decision, maintain a case history, and notify the next responsible party. Rule-based systems can enforce a standard approval path. Human authority remains necessary for setting direction, accepting risk, resolving contested cases, and supporting people. If those duties merely move into informal messages or unpaid repair work, the layer has been hidden rather than removed.

The test should compare a representative baseline with an agent-supported trial across the same case mix. Measure correctly closed cases, end-to-end time, human routing touches, repair effort, outcome quality, control failures, total cost, and the judgment that remains with people. Failed and ambiguous cases belong in the evidence because they reveal where coordination returns.

The evidence base here is deliberately narrow: KC’s account, Simkin’s description of the Retrofit Trap, established state-control concepts, and the Swedish participation law discussed below. It does not provide a Swedish or Norwegian production benchmark for safe delayering. The scorecard is my proposed decision tool, not an established standard, so leaders must declare their own acceptance conditions before the trial starts.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Treat each layer as a bundle of coordination, leadership, and accountability work. Structural change is justified only when representative evidence shows which duties ended, which moved, and which still need a named human owner.</p>
</div>

## Replace escalation with visible authority

More ownership at the edges can look disorderly from the center because fewer decisions travel upward. Visible boundaries distinguish empowered domains from unmanaged fragmentation. Each domain needs an accountable owner, a defined set of decisions, approved data and tools, and an escalation path for cases outside policy.

Rule-based control software should enforce identity, permission, spending limits, and authoritative state, meaning the company’s official record of a case at that moment. A generative model can interpret supplier documents and propose a classification within those boundaries. It cannot grant itself access, raise its own limit, or treat instructions found inside an email or attachment as authority.

Delegation also needs a complete record: the acting system, the person or role on whose behalf it acts, the permitted resource and action, its expiry, and the effect of revocation on work already under way. Every attempted action should preserve its evidence, policy version, approval basis, and result. Stop authority is the named right and duty to pause the workflow when risk or uncertainty crosses the agreed boundary. Overrides, rollback attempts, and corrective actions need the same audit trail.

Retries create a less visible problem. Idempotency is the engineering property that repeating a request has the same intended effect as making it once, a concept formalized for web requests in the Internet Engineering Task Force’s 2022 standard [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods). A unique action key can prevent two supplier records from being created. If an external system may have completed an action before the company’s case record was updated, the outcome should be marked unknown, further retries should stop, and a named owner should reconcile the systems before work continues.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A flatter company replaces informal escalation with domain ownership and explicit decision rights. Agents interpret within boundaries; rule-based software enforces them; named people own exceptions, stops, reconciliation, and corrective action.</p>
</div>

## The pyramid was also a career system

The old hierarchy did more than coordinate work. It distributed context, provided coaching, signaled status, and gave ambitious employees a route to greater scope and pay through larger teams. When coordination-heavy roles shrink, those functions do not repair themselves.

A responsible redesign identifies which human work remains, including goals, coaching, conflict, difficult judgment, domain improvement, and control ownership. It then creates credible paths for expert domain owners, coaches, and control specialists to gain scope, standing, and pay without collecting direct reports. Paid learning, redeployment, transition timing, and fair exit support need owners and budgets before structural savings are recognized.

Participation must begin while the design remains open to change. In Sweden, the 1976 [Co-Determination in the Workplace Act](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-1976580-om-medbestammande-i-arbetslivet_sfs-1976-580/), commonly called MBL, can require negotiation with the relevant employee organization before important operational or employment decisions. The exact duty depends on the employer and proposed change, while other Nordic countries apply their own rules. The CIO, HR leader, legal counsel, and employee-relations owner should agree early on what employees and their representatives can still influence: trial scope, monitoring, role design, learning, redeployment, pay, progression, and timing.

This will not be frictionless. Some employees will move into richer roles, some will need substantial time to learn, and some roles may end. A credible transition says so, funds the available paths, and makes the decision process reviewable rather than presenting a finished structure as participation.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Removing coordination layers also removes parts of the career and coaching system. Participation, new progression paths, paid learning, redeployment, and honest exit support belong in the architecture before savings are booked.</p>
</div>

## Use two gates before changing the structure

The final organization decision needs two gates. The operating gate asks whether complete cases close correctly across representative conditions and whether every remaining duty has an owner. The workforce gate asks whether participation duties, role paths, learning, pay, redeployment, and transition support are agreed and funded. Passing one gate cannot compensate for failing the other.

A CIO can capture both in one fillable decision record. Its fields are intentionally blank; the company must choose values that fit the workflow and risk rather than borrow a universal threshold.

| Decision field | Record before the trial |
| --- | --- |
| Decision and workflow | `[structural question, complete case, executive sponsor]` |
| Baseline and case mix | `[period, volume, case types, known exceptions]` |
| Evidence provenance | `[systems, logs, sampling method, evidence owner]` |
| Agent and control boundary | `[allowed actions, data, limits, rule-based checks]` |
| Human authority | `[domain owner, approvals, exceptions, override and stop owners]` |
| Trial limits | `[time, volume, cost, staffing, supplier dependencies]` |
| Acceptance conditions | `[correct closure, quality, repair, control failures, total cost, residual judgment]` |
| Workforce commitment | `[participation, affected roles, learning, redeployment, pay, progression, support]` |
| Review and reversal | `[review date, signatories, pause trigger, rollback or reconciliation, continuity owner]` |
| Signed decision | `[stop, redesign, extend, or delayer]` |

If a supplier sits in the operating path, the record should also cover access to logs, evidence about data location, notice of service changes, continuity arrangements, and termination assistance. At review, the signatories should explain why residual judgment still needs a role, can move to a domain owner, or has genuinely disappeared.

The legacy architecture remains a real possibility: technical judgment spreads through the business, agents absorb routine execution and coordination, and more ownership moves to the edges. The two gates make that possibility governable. They put the organization chart at the end of the decision, after leaders can show how work closes, where authority sits, and what the company has committed to its people.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Require signed operating proof and a funded workforce commitment before changing reporting lines. The evidence should lead to an explicit choice to stop, redesign, extend, or delayer.</p>
</div>
