An AI-first company changes where technical judgment sits before it changes how many managers it has. Its architecture begins with two linked claims: every business role acquires a technical dimension, and more ownership can move toward domain teams when AI agents absorb meaningful execution and coordination. By an AI agent, I mean software that can interpret a case, choose among permitted steps, and use approved business tools to carry them out.

Those claims should not be approved as one package. Distributed technical judgment is a capability decision that leaders can make now. A flatter structure is a hypothesis about work, and it becomes defensible only when evidence shows that information gathering, routine approvals, handoffs, and exception routing have actually declined. Hierarchy performs many valuable human functions, but it also acts as a coordination system. If that coordination remains, moving boxes on the chart merely hides it.

The CIO decision therefore has three stages. Change now by building technical judgment in every domain. Prove next that an agent-supported design can close a complete workflow with less routing and repair. Authorize structural change later, after decision rights, controls, economics, reversibility, and the workforce transition are explicit.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical judgment can move outward immediately. Structural authority should move only after agents have removed real coordination work and leadership can defend the controls, cost, and people consequences.</p></div>

## Change now: distribute technical judgment

In a [June 2026 video conversation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ), [Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), Chief People Officer at OpenAI, the AI research and product company behind ChatGPT, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately grounded “technical” in domain expertise: the technology is applied within a particular field.

I read that as a judgment requirement, not an instruction that everyone must code. Technical judgment is the ability to decide what AI can do reliably in a specific business setting, what evidence its output requires, where it tends to fail, and when a person must take over. The expertise remains commercial, financial, operational, or human. What changes is the expectation that domain professionals can shape how the technology participates in their work.

Sales and marketing teams, for example, need to judge whether AI-produced recommendations and claims are supported by approved evidence. Finance and operations need to know when exact records, reconciliation, and human approval matter more than a plausible answer. Support teams need to recognize policy exceptions and moments when customer context requires human discretion. None of this turns those teams into engineering departments. It makes them capable owners of AI-enabled work rather than passive recipients of a centrally chosen tool.

That capability creates room for more bottom-up ownership because the people closest to a domain can see its failure modes and redesign its workflows. Shared platforms, security controls, model-risk review, and control testing still need central coordination. The practical shift is that the center supplies safe foundations while domain teams own outcomes within clear boundaries. No management layer has to disappear for this work to begin.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to design, question, and own AI-enabled work in its domain. This supports bottom-up ownership without requiring everyone to program or forcing an immediate change to the hierarchy.</p></div>

## Prove next: test the complete workflow

Business author Benjamin Simkin calls the habit of attaching AI to an old structure the “[Retrofit Trap](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5)” in his book *The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*. The trap is easy to enter because a faster task is visible while the surrounding coordination is not. An AI system may classify a request or draft a response quickly, yet people still collect missing context, seek approval, copy information between systems, chase exceptions, and repair inconsistent records.

Consider a business-customer billing dispute. Improving the reply is useful, but the complete workflow begins when the dispute arrives and ends only when the contract and invoice have been checked, any adjustment has been authorized, the financial and customer records agree, the customer has been informed, and unresolved exceptions have a named owner. If managers still carry context between those steps, the organization has accelerated an activity without changing its operating model.

This is where the flatter-company thesis gets its mechanism. A traditional hierarchy often gathers information, interprets policy, approves action, and routes unusual cases upward. An agent-supported workflow may collect the relevant documents, compare them with policy, propose a resolution, update permitted systems in sequence, and involve a person only when the case falls outside a defined boundary. Policy changes, unusual commercial risk, ambiguous contracts, and accountability for the outcome remain human decisions.

The evidence should use the whole case as its unit. I would call a case “correctly closed” when it meets a predeclared quality and control standard, leaves every authoritative record in the expected state, and has no concealed repair queue. This is a proposed decision measure, not an established industry benchmark. The meaningful economic unit is total cost per correctly closed case, because a lower handling time means little if supervision, rework, integration, or supplier costs rise elsewhere.

A one-page record for the executive team or board should contain:

- **Decision and ownership.** Name the workflow owner, executive sponsor, funding owner, structural hypothesis, and decision date.
- **Scope and comparators.** Define the case cohort and trial period or case count, then compare the current process, the strongest conventional rules-based automation, and the agent-supported design on the same kind of work.
- **Outcome and economics.** Record correctly closed cases, elapsed time, human routing touches, repair effort, quality failures, control failures, residual human judgment, total cost, and cost per correctly closed case.
- **Control and verification.** Predeclare prohibited actions and the stop trigger. Name a verifier outside the delivery team, the exception owner, and the route back to the safe process.
- **Delivery and people.** Set the budget cap and required capacity from the domain, technology, control, and HR teams. State which role activities may change and what workforce commitments must be ready before a structural decision.

A successful bounded trial authorizes a broader test, not the removal of an enterprise-wide layer. The result must survive representative workload, awkward exceptions, service disruption, and the strongest practical alternative before leaders can conclude that coordination has disappeared rather than moved into hidden support work. The evidence behind this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a safe numerical threshold for removing management layers, so any threshold presented to a board must be identified as a local decision criterion rather than an external benchmark.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Measure the complete workflow against current practice and strong conventional automation. A board-ready record should expose cost per correctly closed case, human routing, repair, control failures, residual judgment, trial ownership, stop conditions, and role effects before any structural conclusion is drawn.</p></div>

## Make authority visible before moving it

Once the workflow evidence is credible, leadership still has to decide where authority will sit. Decision rights are explicit answers to four practical questions: who may decide, who may execute, who owns an exception, and who can stop the system. A flatter company needs these answers in a form that employees, control functions, and the board can inspect.

The replacement architecture separates interpretation from enforcement. A domain owner defines the acceptable outcome, policy boundaries, and exception classes. The agent interprets the request and proposes an action within those boundaries. Ordinary rules-based software holds the authoritative state, meaning the official facts and status that determine which action is allowed next, and enforces permissions before anything changes. A named person retains authority over cases the rules do not cover.

Each proposed action needs a case identifier, expected current state, requested action, required permission, and a unique retry key. The execution record then adds the external effect status, the committed new state, and the named exception owner. For the billing dispute, software should reject a proposed credit if the case is already closed or the amount falls outside delegated authority, regardless of how persuasive the agent's explanation sounds.

If the credit service times out, the external effect should be recorded as unknown until finance operations checks whether money actually moved. Only then may the system retry. This supports idempotency, a control against creating a second business effect when the same request is repeated. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-9.2.2), the 2022 standard defining the core meaning of the web's HTTP protocol, describes an idempotent request method as one where repeated identical requests have the same intended effect as one request.

The same design needs an audit trail, a durable record of the input, proposed action, permission check, human decision, system effect, and any override. Stop authority gives a named accountable person the power to suspend new actions. Rollback is the documented route to a safe process, including how already committed effects will be found and repaired. These controls belong in ordinary operating governance, not inside the agent's free-form reasoning.

Supplier dependence also needs owners. The platform owner should govern data boundaries, including where data is stored and processed, and be able to withdraw access. The commercial owner should hold the contract and exit plan. The workflow owner should be responsible for service interruption, the safe fallback, and restoration. With those boundaries visible, fewer decisions may travel upward even though the work remains controlled. What looks less orderly from the center can be stronger ownership at the edges.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Let agents interpret and propose, while rules-based software enforces permissions and official state. Named humans must own exceptions, audit evidence, supplier exit, stop authority, and rollback so that distributed ownership does not conceal control.</p></div>

## Authorize later: redesign the people system and the chart

Removing coordination work changes careers as well as cost. A management role may combine status gathering and approval routing with coaching, conflict resolution, resource choices, and accountability. Leaders should therefore break roles into activities before deciding that an entire position is redundant. Routine routing may disappear while the need for judgment, development, and responsibility remains.

The old career structure also needs a replacement. If progression and pay depend heavily on team size, a flatter organization can turn meaningful domain ownership into a perceived demotion. New paths should recognize the scope of decisions, quality of outcomes, control stewardship, coaching, and growth in expertise. The criteria, pay consequences, and available roles need to be public before people are asked to support the redesign.

A credible transition is funded work. A business executive and an HR leader should jointly own it, with protected learning time, a budget, and a date by which role and pay paths will be agreed. In a Nordic organization, employee representatives should participate while the design can still change. The plan must cover redeployment and the terms for roles that genuinely end, because ambition and trust cannot be repaired with a communication campaign after the chart is announced.

At the executive table, I would ask for three separate authorizations:

- **Change now.** Build domain-level technical judgment, map complete workflows, and identify where coordination and authority currently sit.
- **Prove next.** Run a bounded comparison with a named owner, budget cap, decision date, independent verification, stop trigger, safe fallback, and funded people workstream.
- **Authorize later.** Change roles or layers only after repeated complete-workflow evidence shows that coordination has materially declined, the control model works under failure, the economics hold, and the workforce commitments are ready.

The case for a smaller hierarchy rests on governed workflows that repeatedly close real cases with less routing, acceptable cost and control, and clear ownership of the judgment that remains. Agent capability alone cannot carry that decision. The org chart should be the final record of an operating change that has already been proved, governed, and made credible to the people who will live with it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Separate capability building, workflow proof, and structural authorization. A CIO should change the chart only when the operating evidence, authority model, economics, reversibility, career paths, employee participation, and transition funding are ready together.</p></div>
