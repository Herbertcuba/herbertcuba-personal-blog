Two claims define the architecture of an AI-first company. Every business role acquires a technical dimension, and organizations can become flatter as more execution and coordination move to AI agents. The claims belong together, but they do not carry the same burden of proof.

An agent here means an AI system that can interpret a goal and use software tools across several steps. I think leaders should develop the first capability now: people throughout the business need enough technical judgment to decide where an agent is useful, unreliable, or inappropriate. The flatter-company claim is more conditional. It becomes defensible only when operating evidence shows that coordination work has actually disappeared and the remaining authority has clear human owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Distributed technical judgment is an immediate capability decision. A flatter structure is a later organizational decision that agents must earn through evidence, controls, and a credible people transition.</p></div>

## Move technical judgment to the business edge

In a [video conversation published on June 1, 2026 in Central European time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ), [Arvind KC, Chief People Officer at OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), the AI research and product company behind ChatGPT, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately tied that claim to domain expertise: technology is applied within a particular field. In the same discussion, he described innovation becoming more bottom-up as people use the technology and develop ideas close to the work.

“Technical” should therefore describe judgment, not job title. A salesperson needs to know which account research can be trusted and which claim needs checking. Operations must understand where an agent can act and where an exception needs an accountable person. Finance must set evidence and approval boundaries; marketing must judge provenance and brand risk; support must know when automation should yield to empathy or discretion. None of this requires everyone to code.

It does require ownership to move closer to the domain. A central AI team can provide shared platforms, security controls, and assurance, but it cannot hold all the context needed to redesign supplier risk, credit control, campaign approval, or customer recovery. From the center, that distribution may look untidy. With named domain owners and common boundaries, however, it is bounded autonomy rather than unmanaged experimentation.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to set useful work, limits, and escalation points for AI. Shared controls can remain central, while the people closest to each business domain own how the capability changes the work.</p></div>

## Prove that coordination has left the workflow

Business author Benjamin Simkin calls the habit of attaching AI to an old process the [“Retrofit Trap” in his book *The AI First Company*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5). The term matters because task speed can hide operating-model inertia. A model may finish one activity quickly while people continue to route the result, seek approvals, reconcile systems, and repair exceptions around it.

Consider supplier onboarding. An agent could read submitted documents and draft a risk summary in minutes. If employees still chase missing evidence, forward the case between legal and finance, re-enter approved data into the supplier system, and resolve duplicate records, the organization has accelerated analysis without removing the coordination layer. The relevant unit is the complete workflow: the case from a valid request to an authorized, usable supplier record, including every handoff and correction.

Hierarchy often carries this otherwise invisible load. Managers gather information, decide which rule applies, route unusual cases, and secure approval from someone with wider authority. An agent-supported workflow may absorb routine interpretation, follow-up, status reporting, and routing. Human leaders still own policy, material exceptions, conflicts between objectives, and the right to stop the process. Flattening is justified only if enough of the first category disappears without concealing or overloading the second.

### The board evidence record

A bounded trial should produce one forwardable decision record. This is a proposed internal evidence contract, not an established industry benchmark, and it should contain:

- **Scope and ownership:** the complete workflow, accountable domain owner, funding owner, case cohort, duration or case count, budget cap, decision date, and committed capacity from business, technology, control, and HR teams.
- **Matched evidence:** the same kind of cases under the current process, the strongest feasible conventional automation using predefined software rules, and the agent-supported design. The comparison should use a declared outcome standard rather than whichever metric makes the agent look best.
- **Operating result:** correctly closed cases, meaning cases that reach the intended outcome and satisfy quality and control requirements; human routing touches; review and repair effort; service time; prohibited actions; control failures; and residual judgment that still needs a person.
- **Economics:** cost per correctly closed case, with the cost boundary stated in advance and including the relevant build allocation, software and supplier charges, oversight, exception handling, repair, and operational support.
- **Authority and reversibility:** permission and exception owners, a predeclared stop trigger, an independent verifier, the safe fallback process, supplier access-withdrawal and exit owners, and the specific organizational scope any positive decision may authorize.
- **People effect:** tasks removed or added, roles affected, learning capacity, employee-participation requirements, and the owner and date for proposed progression and pay changes.

No Swedish or Norwegian production benchmark is assumed here. Each organization must set its own thresholds before the trial, based on its risk, service obligations, and current performance. A successful result in one bounded workflow authorizes a decision about that workflow; it does not prove that an enterprise-wide management layer can safely disappear.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Measure the complete case, including routing, repair, control failures, residual judgment, and full cost. A bounded trial can support a bounded decision, while task-level speed or a successful demo cannot authorize a wider change to the hierarchy.</p></div>

## Make authority inspectable

Removing coordination layers does not remove the need for coordination. It changes where that work is performed, so the replacement architecture must make authority easier to inspect than it was in the hierarchy.

Start with a domain map. For each workflow, name the owner of the business outcome, the policy, the data, routine exceptions, material exceptions, and service restoration. Then define decision rights: who may propose an action, who may approve it, which actions software may execute, and who can override or stop the process. An agent may interpret an unstructured supplier document and propose the next action, but ordinary software should verify identity, permission, and the current case state before committing anything.

That separation needs a compact action record rather than a general promise of “human oversight.” Each proposed action should carry a case identifier, expected current state, requested action, required permission, a unique retry identifier, the status of any external effect, the intended next state, and the named exception owner. If the real state or permission differs, software rejects the action. If an external system times out after supplier creation, the effect is marked unknown and a reconciliation owner checks the system before any retry.

This is where idempotency matters. [RFC 9110, the 2022 standard defining core semantics for the web’s HTTP protocol](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-9.2.2), defines an idempotent request method as one where repeated identical requests have the same intended effect as one request. In business terms, the workflow needs safeguards against a retry creating two supplier records or issuing the same instruction twice. The agent should never infer that a timed-out action failed merely because it did not receive a response.

The same clarity should extend to suppliers. A data owner sets allowed data locations and uses; a contract owner covers service interruption and exit; an access owner can withdraw credentials; and the workflow owner can restore the safe process without waiting for the AI supplier. The audit trail, meaning the record of inputs, proposals, approvals, actions, and outcomes, must survive that exit. Stop authority identifies the person who can halt new actions, while rollback defines how the organization returns to a known safe state or procedure when an external action cannot simply be undone.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents can interpret and propose, while ordinary software enforces identity, permissions, case state, and valid transitions. Named humans retain exception, stop, restoration, and supplier-exit authority, with an audit trail that makes the whole path visible.</p></div>

## Redesign work before reducing roles

The people question cannot wait until the technical trial succeeds. Coordination-heavy roles often combine routine routing with expert judgment, coaching, conflict resolution, and accountability. If agents remove the routing, leaders must examine the remaining work rather than assume that the whole role has vanished. Some positions will become broader and more consequential; others may shrink or cease to be viable.

That change also weakens the old career pyramid. Many organizations have linked progression and pay to supervising more people, controlling more information, or approving more work. A flatter model needs credible alternatives based on domain mastery, decision quality, risk ownership, workflow improvement, and the ability to develop colleagues. Otherwise, the company asks people to accept fewer management roles while leaving ambition and recognition attached to the structure it is dismantling.

The transition therefore needs its own funded workstream before any structural approval. HR and the domain leader should co-own it, with protected learning time, published selection and progression criteria, employee representatives involved before decisions are fixed, and a date by which role and pay paths will be settled. It should state where redeployment is realistic and how people will be treated where it is not. Coaching capacity must also be assigned explicitly; deleting a layer does not make development, difficult conversations, or duty of care disappear.

This is a governance issue as much as a fairness issue. If a workflow only works because experienced employees quietly absorb exceptions or teach others outside the measured process, then the supposed coordination gain is incomplete. Those human contributions belong in both the operating evidence and the workforce decision.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Remove tasks before judging roles, and account for the expert judgment and coaching that remain. A flatter structure needs funded learning, new progression and pay paths, employee participation, and honest treatment of people whose work cannot be credibly redeployed.</p></div>

## Separate the three authorizations

A CIO can keep this decision defensible by refusing to bundle three different approvals into one transformation program.

**Authorize capability now.** Build technical judgment across business functions, map complete workflows, name domain owners, and document current decision rights. None of this depends on proving that a layer should disappear.

**Authorize operating proof next.** Fund a bounded trial with the evidence record, supplier controls, workforce capacity, independent verification, stop trigger, and safe fallback agreed in advance. The result should show whether coordination was removed, relocated, or merely hidden as repair work.

**Authorize structural change later.** Consider changing roles or layers only after repeated complete-workflow results meet the declared quality, control, cost, and human-judgment conditions. Require tested stop and restoration paths, a funded people plan, completed employee participation, and a clear limit on what the evidence permits the organization to change.

This preserves the ambition in the original AI-first thesis without pretending that agents make hierarchy obsolete on arrival. Technical judgment can move outward now. Structural authority should move only as fast as the organization can make ownership clearer, complexity controlled, and consequences reversible. Only then is a flatter company a defensible result of the operating evidence rather than an assumption built into the program.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Develop distributed technical judgment now, prove complete workflows under bounded conditions next, and authorize hierarchy changes only after the controls and people commitments hold. The org chart records the decision; it should never substitute for its evidence.</p></div>
