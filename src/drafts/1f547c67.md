On 24 February 2026, [Arvind KC became Chief People Officer at OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), the AI company behind the ChatGPT generative AI assistant. The appointment matters because the hardest AI-first decisions are no longer confined to model development. They now reach roles, authority, careers, and the shape of the company itself.

On 10 May 2026, Benjamin Simkin published *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, an independently published field manual. Simkin calls the design problem the “Retrofit Trap”: adding AI to work built for manual human execution while leaving the handoffs, approvals, and reporting structure intact. The process may become faster at one point without becoming a different system.

The question I keep getting from founders and executives is what an AI-first organization looks like in practice. Who does what, how are decisions made, and why should the chart change? I think the answer begins with two claims: every role becomes technical, and some hierarchies become flatter because software can perform coordination work that once required management layers.

The causal link is an operating model I call *agentic production*. An [AI agent](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/) is software that uses an AI model, instructions, data, and tools to carry out a multi-step task. Agentic production is a workflow in which agents execute routine work and coordinate handoffs while people set goals, own policy, and decide consequential exceptions.

That definition makes the claim testable. A management layer should disappear only when the workflow can produce the same or better outcome within declared limits for quality, cost, human review, permissions, an audit trail, and rollback to the human process. If it cannot, the hierarchy is still doing necessary work. If it can, the org chart is only half the redesign, because the company must also replace the authority and career paths that the old layer carried.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first design can remove routine relay work, but only after an agent workflow proves that it can carry the load within explicit operating limits. The real redesign covers technical literacy, human authority, and careers as well as reporting lines.</p></div>

## The two claims

KC's appointment and Simkin's book are useful signals, but neither proves that a company should flatten. The evidence has to come from the work itself. Simkin's Retrofit Trap helps locate the test: if the old process still depends on the same human routing and approvals, adding an AI tool has not changed the organizational architecture.

The first claim is that people throughout the company need enough technical understanding to judge how AI changes decisions in their domain. The second is narrower than “management disappears.” Fewer managers may be needed where their main function is collecting updates, translating between teams, and routing routine approvals after agents can perform those tasks reliably.

I call that part of the organization a *relay layer*. The term describes a function, not a job title. A manager who coaches people, allocates resources, resolves conflict, sets policy, or carries accountability is doing more than relaying work, and those responsibilities do not vanish because ticket routing has improved.

This distinction gives the argument a clear failure condition. If removing a layer increases unresolved exceptions, weakens decisions, raises the full cost of a completed task, or pushes hidden review work onto domain experts, the agent has not replaced coordination. The company has merely made coordination harder to see. I think flattening belongs after demonstrated reliability.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every role needs technical judgment, but hierarchy should shrink only where a relay function has been replaced and measured. Coaching, resource choices, conflict resolution, policy, and accountability remain human work.</p></div>

## Every role becomes technical

A technical role does not require everyone to code or put engineers in every seat. It means decisions in sales, operations, finance, marketing, and support now depend on what an AI system can do, where it fails, and how its output should be checked. The person responsible for the decision needs to understand those limits even when someone else builds the system.

I've watched this play out in organizations I advise on agentic production. A marketing team stalls when it cannot judge whether an AI-generated campaign brief reflects the strategy. An operations team cannot safely skip human review if it has no way to measure the accuracy of an agent's ticket classification. A finance team cannot choose well between a system that reduces repeated reconciliation and a tool that has not changed cycle time, error rates, or cost.

This is a literacy rather than a coding skill. I think AI systems act as amplifiers because clear instructions, sound source material, and a useful quality standard can spread through a workflow. Confused ownership and weak judgment spread just as easily, often behind a dashboard that looks like progress.

There is important prior art here. Workflow engines and *state machines*, software that moves work through predefined stages according to rules, already route cases whose paths can be fully specified. An agent changes a narrower part of the system: it can interpret material such as a customer email and choose among permitted next steps when every wording variation cannot be specified in advance. The surrounding permissions, records, and escalation rules remain ordinary workflow engineering, and they become more important when an AI model helps choose the path.

The old organization sends technical questions to a separate function. The AI-first organization still needs specialists, but technical reasoning becomes a shared language for decisions. That shared language allows authority to move closer to the work without turning local freedom into guesswork.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A technical role is one whose owner can judge how AI affects the decision in front of them. Agents add flexible interpretation to established workflow engineering; they do not remove the need for rules, permissions, or specialist knowledge.</p></div>

## When a relay layer can disappear

Consider a customer-support workflow. An agent can receive a ticket, classify the issue, retrieve the customer's account history and contract terms, draft a response, and check it against policy. A routine case can move forward only when the source systems agree, the proposed action stays within an approved limit, and the output passes the company's quality checks. An exception pauses the workflow and goes to a named support owner, who sees the evidence, makes the decision, and remains accountable.

The operating contract should be explicit. The agent may read the ticket, account record, contract, and current policy; it may draft a reply and issue only the actions the policy permits. Its states might be received, evidence gathered, draft prepared, approved for routine execution, escalated, and closed. The audit trail records the input, sources consulted, tools used, policy version, proposed action, final action, and any human decision. One transition is forbidden: an escalated case cannot return to automated execution without human approval.

In the old process, a supervisor might assign the ticket, request status, collect context, and review a standard answer. Once an agent performs those steps reliably and leaves a usable record, that part of the supervisor's role can shrink. The role should remain where the same person coaches the team, handles sensitive cases, sets service policy, or resolves conflict with another function.

“Reliable enough” needs a gate, not a feeling. For one support queue, a company could record a four-week human baseline and then run a six-week supervised trial on a comparable case mix. Before the trial begins, the owner sets the required resolution quality and customer outcome, the maximum review and exception rate, the response-time target, and the full cost per resolved case, including models, integration, supervision, and rework. The gate also requires a complete audit trail, enforced tool permissions, a named accountable owner, and a tested way to return the queue to human handling. A hard security or privacy breach should stop the trial; repeated misses against the declared quality threshold should trigger rollback before anyone debates whether the numbers are close enough.

The threshold values will differ by risk and customer promise, but declaring them in advance makes the organizational claim falsifiable. The layer can change only if the new workflow meets the same outcome at acceptable cost without moving an unreasonable review burden elsewhere. I think this is the difference between redesign and headcount theatre.

A public Swedish case shows both the opportunity and the boundary. In February 2024, Klarna, the Swedish payments company, [reported that its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month](https://www.klarna.com/international/press/klarna-ai-assistant-handles-two-thirds-of-customer-service-chats-in-its-first-month/), equal to two-thirds of its support chats. Klarna said customer satisfaction matched its human agents, repeat inquiries fell by 25 percent, and resolution time dropped from 11 minutes to under two. The results came from Klarna and were not independently audited, but they show the kind of baseline comparison an operating decision needs.

The case did not become a simple argument for removing people. In June 2025, [Klarna chief executive Sebastian Siemiatkowski said the company would keep using AI for manual support work while also promising customers a human connection](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/04/klarna-ceo-says-company-will-use-humans-to-offer-vip-customer-service/). He described human service as a premium experience. The lesson is narrower and more useful: high automated volume can justify changing the routine path, while customer trust and difficult cases can still justify a human path and the authority behind it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A relay layer becomes removable when a bounded trial beats a declared baseline for quality, economics, review burden, auditability, and reversibility. Klarna's support results show the potential scale, while its retained human option shows why automation volume alone cannot settle the org-chart decision.</p></div>

## The compounding advantage

Once a domain expert can supervise an agent workflow, the role gains a second capability. An *agent manager* is a person who can specify work for agents, check their results, and improve the workflow over time. The title matters less than the combination of technical literacy and real domain expertise.

An editor can use agents to prepare drafts, collect sources for claims, and flag repeated language before the final edit. A finance analyst can have agents pull and reconcile records, then surface anomalies for investigation. A customer-success lead can use them to triage cases and prepare account summaries, preserving attention for relationship risk and difficult conversations. In each example, the agent handles repeatable preparation while the specialist remains responsible for judgment.

I think the advantage compounds only when saved effort improves the system that produced it. Human corrections need to change the instructions, source material, policies, or tests used by the workflow. If learning stays in one person's head, the same errors recur at higher speed and the apparent gain remains fragile.

I don't know a defensible productivity multiplier that applies across companies. The result depends on task volume, baseline quality, the cost of mistakes, and how much human review remains. The strategic advantage comes from the direction of learning: a team that turns corrections into a better shared system can improve faster than one that keeps adding isolated tools to the old process.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agent management can multiply a domain expert's capacity, but the gain compounds only when corrections improve the shared workflow. The size of the gain belongs in the trial evidence, not in a universal productivity claim.</p></div>

## Distributed ownership needs governance

A flatter company will often look less tidy because more decisions happen close to the work. Leaders see more local movement and fewer requests for permission. That can be productive, but motion itself is not evidence that the design works.

The safeguard is explicit *decision rights*, meaning a written answer to who may decide what and when an issue must be escalated. Reversible choices that stay within one domain, budget, quality standard, and policy boundary can usually sit close to the work. Company strategy, major capital allocation, security and privacy rules, legal obligations, cross-domain conflicts, and high-impact people or customer decisions still need central authority.

The most useful frame from my own work is *domain-mapped ownership*. Each domain has one person who defines good output, sets operating limits, reviews exceptions, and can change the workflow. This does not replace established work on management spans or workflow governance. Its narrower purpose is to connect an agent's permission boundary and exceptions created by an AI model's choices to one human owner who can make the final call. It avoids the Everybody Reviews Everything Trap, where feedback conflicts and nobody owns the result.

For a European support workflow, the boundary also has to reflect the [EU General Data Protection Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng), the 2016 law governing personal-data processing. It must also account for any applicable duties under the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the 2024 risk-based regulation of AI systems. Legal scope depends on the use case, so the practical point is to involve privacy, security, legal, and supplier-risk owners before permissions are delegated. Data access, retention, human oversight, and escalation then become enforceable workflow terms with more weight than a policy promise.

The same principle applies to worker governance. Where law, collective agreements, or company commitments require employee consultation, that work belongs before the reporting line moves. I think leaders can tolerate distributed experimentation only when customers, employees, and accountable executives can still see who made a decision and how to challenge it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Distributed ownership works when local authority has a named owner, measurable limits, a decision record, and an escalation path. Privacy, security, legal, supplier, and worker-governance boundaries must be built into the workflow before hierarchy is removed.</p></div>

## The career ladder is part of the architecture

The harder question is what happens to the people whose coordination tasks shrink. People are not absorbed by agents; parts of their work are. Some managers can become domain owners or agent managers. Others may retrain, move elsewhere in the company, or leave. Organizations I have advised during AI-first reorganizations have handled this differently, but the human transition has been harder than the technical one.

A flatter organization also breaks a familiar promise about ambition. If there are fewer management layers, progression can no longer depend mainly on acquiring more direct reports. Companies need credible paths through deeper craft, larger domain ownership, technical leadership, or responsibility that crosses domains. Otherwise they remove a layer of coordination and accidentally remove the main language of status and reward.

That makes the employee compact part of the operating model. Before a workflow changes the chart, leadership should name who owns consultation, who funds and schedules reskilling, how redeployment decisions will be made, and which non-managerial paths carry greater pay and authority. These choices will vary, but leaving them implicit turns efficiency into a promise employees have no reason to trust.

My take is to begin with one workflow and one bounded decision. Draw the target process, record the baseline, name the human owner, limit the agent's tools, write the escalation and stop rules, fund the people transition, and measure the complete result before changing the reporting line. Then use what the team learns to redesign the next part of the company.

The architecture of an AI-first company is therefore more demanding than a flat chart. It asks leaders to prove which coordination work has become unnecessary, preserve authority where judgment still matters, and build a credible future for the people who used to carry the middle. If leadership cannot redesign ambition as carefully as it redesigns execution, the company will be efficient on paper and illegitimate in practice.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A durable AI-first company proves which layer became unnecessary and gives people a credible place to go when it does; removing layers without those conditions merely produces a smaller chart.</p></div>
