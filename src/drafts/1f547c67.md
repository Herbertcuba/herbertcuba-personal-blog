An AI-first company makes two bets at once. Every role acquires a technical dimension, and ownership moves closer to the people who understand the work as agents absorb more execution and coordination. Taken together, those bets point toward a flatter company with more initiative at its edges.

The first bet has a clear source. In a [recorded DECODE interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ), Arvind KC, chief people officer at [OpenAI](https://openai.com/about/), an AI research and deployment company, says that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role,” applied to a particular domain. He also describes the organization as “very bottom-up driven.” The first observation supports distributed technical judgment; the second points toward distributed ownership. Neither proves that another company can safely remove a management layer.

That final step is a design hypothesis. I think it is credible, but only when a CIO can show that agents have removed coordination from complete workflows, authority remains visible, failures are contained, and the people affected have a real transition path. Otherwise, a flatter chart simply hides work that someone still has to do.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Distributed technical judgment and bottom-up ownership create the possibility of a flatter AI-first company. They do not remove the need to prove the operating model before changing the hierarchy.</p></div>

## Technical judgment belongs in every function

Technical judgment does not mean that everyone must code. It means knowing enough about an AI system to decide where it can help, what evidence its output needs, when a result is outside its limits, and which decisions must stay with a person.

That capability looks different by domain. A salesperson should know when an agent can assemble an account brief but cannot commit the company to a price. Finance needs to separate a proposed classification from an authorized posting. Operations must understand when an unusual case requires escalation, while marketing and support need clear standards for claims, tone, and customer remedies. Domain expertise supplies the meaning; technical judgment supplies the boundary.

This changes where AI ownership sits. A central platform team can provide approved services, security controls, and shared records, but it cannot define every useful decision at the edge. Business teams need room to shape their own workflows inside common rules, with named owners for the domain outcome and the control risk. From the center, that can look less tidy than uniform process. In practice, bounded local ownership is what lets technical judgment turn into operating capacity.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to set useful boundaries for AI, while shared platforms provide the controls. The shift is toward domain ownership within common rules, not toward making every employee a programmer.</p></div>

## Measure the coordination that disappears

Benjamin Simkin, an entrepreneur and author, calls the central failure the “Retrofit Trap” in his 2026 book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5). The label describes bolting AI onto an old structure instead of redesigning work around the technology. It is a useful diagnosis, not evidence that any particular redesign will succeed.

Consider a maintenance work order. An agent may read a fault report and draft a repair plan in seconds, yet the case can still wait for a supervisor to identify the asset, another manager to approve a contractor, a coordinator to check the part, and an administrator to repair the record after completion. The first task became faster. The workflow did not.

That distinction matters because hierarchy is partly a coordination system. Managers collect information, route approvals, resolve exceptions, and make sure one step hands cleanly to the next. Agents can potentially absorb routine parts of that work by assembling context, checking known conditions, proposing a route, and keeping the case moving. They do not absorb the human responsibility to set risk appetite, change policy, resolve a value conflict, or accept an exception outside delegated limits.

So the unit of evidence should be a correctly closed case: a complete piece of work that ends with the right outcome, records, permissions, approvals, and communication. Compare it with a matched baseline that includes a similar mix of ordinary and difficult cases. Measure elapsed time, human routing touches, repair effort, outcome quality, control failures, total cost, and the judgment still required. A layer is genuinely removable only when its recurring work has disappeared or found an explicit new owner. Moving the same work into invisible overtime is not flattening.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Task speed cannot justify delayering. The evidence must cover correctly closed cases and show that routing, repair, and exception work has actually gone away rather than shifted to someone else.</p></div>

## Put authority in the architecture

A flatter company needs stronger control design because fewer coordination layers leave less room for informal repair. Start with a domain map: every workflow has a business owner for the outcome, a control owner for the rules, and named people who can approve exceptions. Decision rights should state who may propose, execute, override, pause, and change the policy.

The agent may interpret a fault report and propose the next action. Ordinary software should enforce identity, permission, spending limits, and authoritative state, meaning the company’s official record of where the case currently stands. This separation matters because a generative-AI model produces likely rather than guaranteed answers. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology’s [July 2024 generative-AI risk profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf) also describes indirect prompt injection, in which hostile instructions are placed in material an AI application later retrieves. A supplier document or email must therefore never be able to grant itself authority.

Delegation needs an expiry date as well as a limit. The record should identify the person or service acting, on whose behalf it acts, which resource and action are allowed, and when that permission ends. It must also say what revocation does to work already in progress. Stop authority is the named right to block new agent-triggered actions immediately; override and restart require their own owners and audit trail.

Repeated actions need equal care. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods), the Internet Engineering Task Force’s June 2022 standard for web communication, defines an idempotent request as one whose repeated identical requests have the same intended effect as a single request. In business terms, a retry should not create a second purchase order. When an external system may have acted but the internal record was not updated, the workflow should pause and reconcile with that system before retrying. A unique action key helps detect duplication, but a named person still owns any uncertain result and corrective action.

This architecture lets decisions move outward without making control disappear. The edge can act quickly because its boundaries, evidence, and route back to human authority are explicit.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents may interpret and propose, while ordinary software enforces current state and delegated permissions. Named humans own policy, exceptions, stops, uncertain external effects, and recovery.</p></div>

## Rebuild the career system with the operating model

The old hierarchy did more than route work. It also carried status, pay progression, coaching, and a path from junior contributor to senior leader. If agents remove coordination-heavy roles, those functions do not vanish with the boxes. A company that ignores them may gain a leaner chart while losing apprenticeship, ambition, and institutional memory.

The people decision must therefore begin while the design is still open. For EU employers using a high-risk AI system at work, [Article 26(7) of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, requires them to inform workers’ representatives and affected workers before putting that system into service or using it, subject to applicable rules. That requirement covers a defined legal category and is a floor, not a complete participation model.

A responsible redesign goes further. Give one executive ownership of the workforce transition and fund paid learning, role mapping, redeployment, and support for people who cannot move into the new structure. Keep task boundaries, exception ownership, sequencing, and training open to influence early enough for participation to change the plan. Then rebuild progression around deeper domain judgment, control ownership, coaching, and the ability to improve a whole workflow, with clear consequences for title and pay.

This will create friction because some roles will shrink and some careers will change direction. Naming that cost early is part of the operating case, not a message to prepare after the decision.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Delayering also dismantles parts of the career system. Participation, learning, progression, pay, redeployment, and support need funded owners before the organization changes, while employees can still influence the design.</p></div>

## Make the org-chart decision from a signed record

The external sources in this article support the originating observations, the Retrofit Trap label, and relevant control and participation concepts. They do not establish that management layers can be removed safely. The workflow test below is my proposed decision method, with no universal threshold and no Nordic production case presented as proof.

Before changing reporting lines, require a completed one-page record:

**AI-first operating-model decision record**

- **Workflow and accountable business owner:** `________`
- **Baseline and case mix:** `________`
- **Trial scope and operating limits:** `________`
- **Decision rights, exception owner, and stop owner:** `________`
- **Evidence sources and person validating them:** `________`
- **Complete-workflow results, including repair and control failures:** `________`
- **Workforce impact and participation owner:** `________`
- **Review date and local acceptance conditions:** `________`
- **Reversal path if those conditions are missed:** `________`

The executive decision should follow the record. Keep the structure if coordination remains, redesign the workflow if failures are poorly contained, extend the trial if the evidence is too thin, and remove a layer only when its work has been eliminated or reassigned with visible accountability. Reversibility matters because the organization is changing both technology and social structure at the same time.

A flatter AI-first company is therefore an earned operating condition. The CIO’s job is to make sure that technical judgment can spread and local ownership can grow without allowing authority, repair work, or human cost to disappear from view. Only then should the org chart catch up with the work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Use a signed, reversible decision record rather than an AI-first prediction. Change the hierarchy only when complete-workflow evidence, explicit authority, controlled failure, and a funded workforce transition make the new structure defensible.</p></div>
