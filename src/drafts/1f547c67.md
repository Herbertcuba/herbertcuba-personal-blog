Every role in an AI-first company acquires a technical dimension. At the same time, ownership can move closer to the people who understand the work, giving the company a path toward fewer coordination layers.

Arvind KC, [chief people officer at OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), an artificial-intelligence research and product company, connected these ideas in a [May 2026 interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ). He said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role,” then qualified it as a technical role applied to a particular area of business work. In the same conversation, he described innovation at OpenAI as bottom-up, with ideas able to originate across the organization rather than only at the top.

Those observations do not prove that another company can safely remove management layers. They do point to a different architecture: people with deep business knowledge direct AI agents, meaning software that can interpret a goal, use approved tools, and perform several steps within set boundaries. If those agents absorb enough execution and coordination, some hierarchy may no longer be necessary. The proof has to come from the work before it appears in the org chart.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI-first organization design links distributed technical judgment with bottom-up ownership, but a flatter structure remains a hypothesis until workflow evidence, governance, and workforce commitments support it.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment belongs in the domain

A technical role does not mean that every salesperson, finance partner, marketer, operations lead, or support specialist becomes a programmer. It means that each person can exercise technical judgment inside a domain, a part of the business with its own rules, data, and outcomes. They can break work into steps, judge what an AI system can handle reliably, recognize weak evidence, and decide when a human must take over.

That local context matters. In a disputed supplier invoice, for example, an agent might compare the invoice with a purchase order, delivery record, and contract. A finance expert still has to know whether those sources are sufficient, which mismatch is routine, and which one may signal fraud or a broken commercial agreement. Technical judgment joins domain judgment; it does not replace it.

This is where many AI programs go wrong. Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls the act of bolting AI onto an old structure the “Retrofit Trap.” An agent may extract invoice fields in seconds, yet the case can still pass through the same inboxes, approval meetings, manual checks, and repair queues. One faster task has left the operating model intact.

The right unit of change is a complete workflow: the full path from a business request to a resolved and recorded outcome. That forces leaders to look beyond a polished AI interaction and ask where the case waits, who reroutes it, and what people quietly repair after the agent finishes.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Technical judgment is the ability to apply AI responsibly inside a business domain. It becomes organizationally meaningful only when the company redesigns the complete workflow instead of accelerating an isolated task.</p>
</div>

## A hierarchy is also a routing system

Hierarchy does more than assign status. It carries information upward, sends decisions downward, allocates exceptions, settles conflicts, and checks whether work has actually finished. Some management roles combine this routing function with coaching, professional standards, resource choices, and accountability. Agents may absorb the routing; the other duties do not vanish with it.

In the invoice workflow, software can gather evidence, compare records, request missing material, recommend a permitted action, and update the case after approval. People still own policy, risk tolerance, genuinely new exceptions, competing business priorities, and the consequences of a decision. This division can move routine ownership toward the edge because domain teams no longer need a manager to carry every piece of information between systems and functions.

Whether that change supports a flatter structure depends on a correctly closed case. I use that term for a case that reaches the intended business outcome, meets quality and control requirements, and leaves a usable record. The measure should include human routing touches, waiting time, repair effort, control failures, total operating cost, and the judgment still required from people. A task-level speed measure cannot answer the organizational question.

The baseline and acceptance criteria must be set before the result is known. So must the review period, chosen locally to cover the workflow’s normal peaks and exception mix. The evidence base for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a universal numerical threshold for safely removing a management layer. A CIO should therefore treat any proposed threshold as a company decision, not an industry benchmark.

The review must also look for displaced coordination. A lower manager count means little if senior specialists inherit a growing repair queue, technology teams become permanent case handlers, coaching declines, or cross-domain exceptions wait longer. Those are restoration signals: evidence that the organization must restore capacity or change the workflow because the work moved rather than disappeared.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A flatter company is justified by sustained evidence that complete cases close with less routing and repair while quality, control, cost, and human judgment remain acceptable. Headcount reduction alone proves nothing.</p>
</div>

## Put authority outside the model

Distributed ownership can look chaotic from the center. The answer is not to pull every decision back upward. It is to make domains, boundaries, and authority easier to see than they were in the old hierarchy.

Each workflow needs a business owner for its outcome, a policy owner for its rules, an exception owner for unusual cases, a technology owner for system behavior, and a named person with stop authority, meaning the right to suspend the workflow when its controls or outcomes become unsafe. These roles may be combined, but none should be left implicit.

The AI model should interpret documents, weigh evidence, and propose or execute actions within those boundaries. Ordinary software should enforce permissions and authoritative state, the trusted record of where a case currently stands. If an invoice is marked “disputed,” the system should reject an unapproved jump to “paid” even when the model proposes it. Free-form reasoning is useful for ambiguity; it is a poor place to hide a binding permission.

The same clarity applies after something goes wrong. An override is an authorized departure from the normal case path or policy, and its owner, reason, and outcome should be recorded. A rollback restores an earlier system state when that is possible. If an external action cannot simply be undone, the recovery plan needs a compensating action, such as stopping a payment before settlement or correcting the ledger afterward.

Retries deserve special attention because an uncertain response can prompt the workflow to issue the same instruction twice. Idempotency is the property that makes a repeated request have the same intended effect as one request. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-9.2.2), the 2022 standard for the behavior of web requests, defines an HTTP request method as idempotent on that basis. A provider’s duplicate-request control can reduce risk, but it does not prove that the business outcome happened exactly once across every system. Reconciliation, the comparison of workflow, provider, bank, and ledger records, is still needed.

Auditability follows the decision, not the chat transcript. The record should show who or what acted, the permission and policy used, the model and tool versions, the state before and after, any external action, any override, and the final outcome. Supplier dependence belongs in the same design: name the contract owner, preserve usable case data and instructions, maintain a fallback path, and agree the condition that would trigger a provider change.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Agents may interpret and act, while ordinary software enforces permissions and case state. Named owners, recoverable actions, reconciliation, audit records, and a supplier exit path turn bottom-up autonomy into governed authority.</p>
</div>

## The people architecture has to change too

Removing a coordination layer also removes part of the traditional career ladder. That affects where people learn management, how specialists gain status and pay, who coaches new colleagues, and where ambitious employees go next. A company that redraws reporting lines without rebuilding those paths has not completed the design.

The replacement should reward deeper domain judgment, ownership of business outcomes, workflow stewardship, coaching, and the ability to resolve cross-domain questions. Pay and progression cannot depend mainly on team size if the intended organization has smaller teams and fewer managers. Learning also needs funded time and a clear destination; telling people to become “more strategic” is not a transition plan.

For coordination-heavy roles, leaders owe an explicit account of which duties disappear, which remain valuable, and which new roles are real and funded. Some people can move into domain ownership, control design, service improvement, or coaching. Others will need substantial training, redeployment, or a transparent exit process. There is no responsible version of this change that treats every transition as frictionless.

In Sweden, the [Co-Determination in the Workplace Act (1976:580)](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-1976580-om-medbestammande-i_sfs-1976-580/) is a national labor law governing employer-union participation. Its exact duties depend on the situation, but it gives Swedish leaders a concrete reason to involve labor relations, counsel, and employee representatives before significant decisions are fixed. Other Nordic jurisdictions have their own requirements. Participation is part of the operating architecture, not a message prepared after approval.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>When routing roles shrink, the company must rebuild coaching, progression, pay, learning, and participation around domain judgment and outcome ownership, while dealing honestly with redeployment and exits.</p>
</div>

## Make the org chart the last approval

A CIO should require one decision record that follows the workflow from trial to production and, only then, to organization redesign. The purpose is to stop three different decisions from collapsing into one enthusiastic pilot review.

| Decision | Named forum and owner | Evidence required | Precommitted response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bounded trial | Business workflow owner with technology and control owners | Current baseline, intended outcome, permitted data and actions, acceptance criteria, review period, and full delivery cost | Stop or redesign the trial if cases cannot be evaluated safely |
| Limited production | Business owner with CIO, risk, finance, and supplier-contract owners | Correctly closed cases, human routing and repair, control results, audit record, tested stop and recovery paths, supplier fallback, and residual human judgment | Restrict or suspend production when agreed quality, cost, control, or recovery conditions fail |
| Organization redesign | Accountable executive with CIO and people leadership, plus required employee-participation bodies | Sustained workflow results across normal conditions, a role and capability map, funded transition commitments, and named restoration signals | Change reporting lines only while the evidence holds; restore capacity or authority when coordination, coaching, or risk load returns |

For the supplier-invoice example, the business owner should bring the same case record to every forum. A successful extraction test cannot authorize payment actions. A stable production workflow cannot by itself authorize removal of a role. Each step has a different owner, proof burden, review date, and reversal condition.

The post-change review matters as much as the approval. Before changing the org chart, name the executive who will monitor returning repair work, exception queues, control failures, coaching gaps, and cross-domain delay. Put those signals on an agreed review agenda and preserve the authority to restore people or decision capacity. Reversal then becomes an operating commitment rather than an embarrassed admission.

I think the architecture of an AI-first company can be materially flatter and more bottom-up. But the leader’s job is not to announce that destination. It is to prove that coordination has left the workflow, make power visible in the systems that replace it, and carry the human consequences into the decision. Only then has the org chart earned the right to change.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Separate trial, production, and organization decisions. Fix the evidence, owners, review period, workforce commitments, supplier exit, restoration signals, and reversal authority before results arrive, then change the org chart only if the complete workflow continues to justify it.</p>
</div>
