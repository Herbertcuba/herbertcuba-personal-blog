An AI-first company changes the architecture of work before it changes the org chart. Two linked claims drive that change: every business role gains a technical dimension, and organizations can become flatter as AI agents absorb meaningful execution and coordination. The first spreads judgment toward the edges of the business; the second removes some of the work that made hierarchy necessary.

Here, AI-first means designing complete operating flows around what people and AI can each do well, rather than adding a model to isolated tasks. An AI agent is software that uses an AI model to interpret a goal and take a sequence of permitted actions in business systems. If agents can handle routine work and keep cases moving, ownership can sit closer to customers, operations, and other business domains.

Arvind KC, an executive in the people function at OpenAI, the artificial-intelligence company that makes the ChatGPT assistant, put the first claim plainly in a recorded interview: “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He also described innovation at OpenAI as bottom-up. That observation is a useful starting point, but it is not evidence that companies can safely remove management layers today. A flatter structure becomes defensible only when the work, controls, and people system have changed with it.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>An AI-first operating model links distributed technical judgment with less routine coordination, but flatter ownership must be earned through evidence, governance, and a responsible workforce transition.</p>

## Technical judgment belongs in every function

Technical judgment is broader than coding. It means knowing what an AI system can do reliably in a specific business domain, what evidence its output requires, how it tends to fail, and when a person must decide. Domain knowledge becomes more important because the person using the system needs to recognize plausible mistakes, not merely produce an answer.

In sales, that may mean distinguishing a useful account summary from a commitment the company is allowed to make. Finance needs to know when a model can prepare an analysis but cannot approve a payment. Support and operations teams must separate common cases from exceptions with legal, safety, or customer consequences. Marketing needs to judge whether AI-generated creative content is accurate, permitted, and appropriate for the brand.

Researchers describe the uneven line between tasks a model can handle reliably and tasks it cannot as its capability frontier. A 2023 field experiment reported in *Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier* studied 758 consultants at Boston Consulting Group, a global management consultancy, using GPT-4, OpenAI’s large language model released that year. Participants performed better on tasks within the frontier, but correctness fell on a task outside it. The organizational lesson is that expertise needs to sit close to use: bottom-up ownership works only when people can recognize the boundary and escalate what crosses it.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to use AI within its real capability boundary, test its output against domain evidence, and keep consequential decisions with accountable people.</p>
</div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the hierarchy

Benjamin Simkin, author of the business book *The AI First Company*, calls a common failure the “Retrofit Trap”: adding AI to an existing process while leaving the surrounding design intact. Imagine an AI system that extracts data from a disputed supplier invoice in seconds. If finance still emails procurement, waits for a standard approval, copies the answer into another system, and repairs duplicate actions by hand, one task became faster while the operating model stayed the same.

This matters because a traditional hierarchy is also a coordination system. Managers gather information, allocate work, chase handoffs, approve standard cases, and route exceptions, alongside the human work of setting priorities, coaching, resolving conflict, and accepting responsibility. An agent-supported workflow may absorb the repetitive parts by collecting evidence, checking a case against policy, prompting the next owner, and updating the record.

Some authority remains human. A person still needs to own policy, settle ambiguous exceptions, accept material risk, and stop the process when conditions become unsafe. If routine coordination genuinely declines, broader roles and fewer handoffs may support a flatter organization. Flattening first would merely move the same burden into hidden queues and overloaded teams.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI retrofitted onto one task leaves the surrounding hierarchy intact; structural change becomes plausible only when complete workflows need less human routing, repair, and routine approval.</p>
</div>

## Prove the whole workflow before flattening

The right unit of evidence is a complete workflow, from a business request to an accepted outcome recorded in the relevant systems. The useful result is a correctly closed case: the outcome meets its quality and control conditions even if the system can mark an earlier step “complete.” For the supplier dispute, extraction speed matters far less than whether the invoice is resolved once, under the right authority, with a traceable reason.

I would ask the workflow owner to count every human routing touch, the effort spent checking and repairing AI output, reopened cases, quality problems, control failures, and total cost including model use and oversight. The owner should also identify the judgment left at the end and separate common cases from rare, high-stakes exceptions. This is a proposed decision scorecard, not an established industry benchmark.

I would not give a Nordic board a universal cut-off. The evidence behind this article contains no verified Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes one, and the acceptable result will differ between customer support and a regulated payment. Set acceptance conditions before the pilot, compare the complete flow with current performance, and let the accountable risk owner define which failures are unacceptable. Then you can see whether coordination disappeared or was merely shifted elsewhere.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Use correctly closed cases, human routing and repair, quality, control failures, total cost, and residual judgment to test the operating model. No universal Nordic threshold makes that decision for you.</p>
</div>

## Put authority where everyone can see it

A flatter company still needs explicit ownership. Map each workflow to a domain, meaning a bounded area of business responsibility such as supplier-payment exceptions, and name one owner for its outcome and controls. Write the decision rights in plain language: what the agent may gather, recommend, or execute; what requires approval; and what it must never do.

Hard controls belong in ordinary rule-based software. Permissions determine which systems and actions are available, while state is the authoritative record of where a case stands, such as disputed, approved, or paid. Idempotency means a retried request cannot trigger the same payment twice. An AI model may interpret an email or propose the next step, while ordinary software enforces value limits, required approvals, and the current case state.

Every path also needs an exception owner and stop authority: a named person who can pause the workflow and is accountable for doing so. An override changes a decision with a recorded reason, while rollback returns the case or system to its last safe state. An audit trail should show the input, proposed action, permission used, approval, outcome, and later correction. This architecture gives teams room to act at the edges without concealing who holds authority.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Give each domain a named owner, keep permissions and case state in rule-based software, limit what agents may do, and make every exception, override, stop, and rollback visible in the audit trail.</p>
</div>

The mapping is organizational: when an AI agent can approve, route, or stop work but no accountable person can inspect its boundaries or halt it, authority has been hidden inside the system. An AI-first company needs visible owners, limited permissions, and a stop path that people have tested before a crisis.

## Redesign the career system with the operating model

When agents absorb status collection, approval chasing, and exception routing, some coordination-heavy roles will change and some may disappear. The remaining human work still includes coaching, priority setting, conflict resolution, domain improvement, and accountable judgment. Leaders should examine those activities separately instead of treating “manager” as one indivisible job.

The career architecture must change too. Where progression, status, and pay are tied to team size, a flatter structure removes more than reporting lines. Companies need credible paths for deep domain expertise, control stewardship, coaching, and workflow ownership, with levels and compensation that do not depend on accumulating direct reports. Otherwise the organization asks people to accept flatter structures while preserving a pyramid as the only route to ambition.

Start the transition with a task map before declaring that a layer is redundant. Show which work is expected to shrink, which work becomes more valuable, and which decisions remain human. Give people time and support to learn new work, assess redeployment before separation, and involve employees and their representatives early. If positions may still disappear, say so plainly and fund that consequence as part of the operating-model decision.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Flattening changes careers as well as coordination. Leaders need new routes to progression and pay, investment in learning and redeployment, and an honest, funded process for roles that may disappear.</p>
</div>

## Change the chart last

Before changing reporting lines, I would take four owner-specific questions into the executive or board discussion:

- **Workflow owner:** Do complete cases close correctly with less human routing and repair, at acceptable quality and total cost, without unacceptable control failures?
- **Business and risk owners:** Which decisions may the agent make, which may it only propose, and who owns exceptions and the authority to stop the flow?
- **Technology and security owners:** Are permissions, state, duplicate-action protection, audit, override, and rollback enforced and tested outside the model?
- **People leader:** Which tasks and roles change, how will careers and pay adapt, and what commitments cover learning, redeployment, and possible separation?

The executive sponsor then has one final question: which coordination layer exists mainly to carry work that has demonstrably gone away? If the answer remains mixed, redesign the workflow and gather better evidence before redesigning the hierarchy.

An AI-first company can become materially flatter because technical judgment sits across the business and routine coordination moves into governed systems. That possibility becomes a responsible decision only when operating evidence, visible authority, controlled complexity, and the workforce transition support the same conclusion. The org chart should record a proven change in work, not serve as the experiment.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Change the org chart only when named owners can defend the workflow evidence, decision rights, technical controls, and people commitments. A flatter structure should formalize work that has already changed.</p>
</div>
