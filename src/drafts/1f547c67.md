Arvind KC became Chief People Officer at OpenAI, the AI company behind ChatGPT, in February 2026. A few months later, he posted ten reflections on LinkedIn, the professional network, about how the company works. I read them twice: first as workplace observations, then as a blueprint for organizational design.

They mapped closely to Benjamin Simkin's *The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*, a self-published field manual released in May 2026. Simkin, an engineer turned business coach, describes the “Retrofit Trap”: adding AI to processes built for manual human execution and expecting the structure itself to improve. KC is writing from inside the company building ChatGPT and other general-purpose AI models; Simkin is writing for owners rebuilding more conventional businesses. They reach the same structural argument from different directions.

The question I keep getting from founders and executives is what an AI-first organization looks like in practice. Who does what, how are decisions made, and why should the chart change? I think two of KC's reflections carry most of the answer: every role becomes technical, and bottom-up innovation makes the company flatter and less tidy.

The causal link is an operating model I will call *agentic production*. An AI agent is software that uses an AI model, instructions, data, and tools to carry out a multi-step task. Agentic production is a workflow in which those agents execute routine work and coordinate handoffs while people set goals, own policy, and handle consequential exceptions. I think this can remove layers built mainly to relay information, but it does not make management disappear. It works only where the workflow is reliable enough to deserve that trust.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first design changes the org chart when agents can reliably perform routine work and route its exceptions. The resulting company needs broad technical literacy and fewer relay layers, while human authority remains essential.</p></div>

## The two claims

KC's seventh reflection says, *“Every role is a technical role.”* His third says OpenAI is *“designed for bottom-up innovation”* and may seem chaotic because the model promotes individual empowerment. Simkin reaches the same place through workflow design. His AI-first owner shifts from operator to architect, and his workflows are built around AI capabilities with human oversight where judgment matters.

Together, the claims describe a conditional structure. People throughout the company must understand the technology well enough to make decisions in their domain. At the same time, fewer managers need to move updates and approvals between teams once software can do that work reliably. I think the condition matters: a company with unstable workflows, poor data, or unclear authority will lose coordination if it removes a management layer before replacing the work that layer performs.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Shared technical reasoning and automated coordination can support a flatter organization, but only after the old layer's real responsibilities have been identified and reassigned.</p></div>

## Every role becomes technical

“Every role is technical” does not mean everyone has to code or that engineers should occupy every seat. It means decisions in sales, operations, finance, marketing and support now depend on what an AI system can do, where it fails, and how its output should be checked. The person responsible for the decision needs to understand those limits, even when someone else builds the system.

The practical test is whether a domain leader can evaluate the work. I've watched this play out in organizations I advise on agentic production. A marketing team stalls when it cannot judge whether an AI-generated campaign brief reflects the strategy. An operations team cannot safely skip human review if it has no way to measure the accuracy of an agent's ticket classification. A finance team cannot choose well between an AI system that removes repeated work and another subscription that merely consumes budget.

This is a literacy rather than a coding skill. Simkin makes the point with his amplifier idea: *“AI is an amplifier. It takes whatever you give it and produces more of it, faster.”* Clear instructions, sound source material, and a useful standard for quality improve the result. Confused ownership and weak judgment spread through the workflow just as quickly, often behind a dashboard that looks like progress.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A technical role is one whose owner can judge how AI affects the decision in front of them. Coding is optional; judging capability, failure modes and evidence of quality is part of the job.</p></div>

## Why some hierarchies flatten

One important function of middle management is coordination, but it is not the only one. Managers also coach people, allocate resources, resolve conflict, make personnel decisions, and carry accountability. The flattening argument applies most directly to layers whose main work is collecting status, translating between teams, and routing routine approvals.

Consider a customer-support workflow. An agent can receive a ticket, classify the issue, retrieve the customer's account history and contract terms, draft a response, and check it against policy. When a request stays within an approved refund limit and passes the company's quality checks, the agent can move it through the routine path. If the customer requests an exception, the source systems disagree, or the case raises a legal risk, the agent pauses and presents the evidence to the named support owner. That person still decides and remains accountable for the policy.

In the old process, a supervisor might assign the ticket, ask for status, collect the relevant context, and review a standard answer. Once an agent can perform those steps reliably and leave a record of what it did, that part of the supervisor's role can shrink. The role still matters if the same person coaches the team, handles sensitive cases, sets service policy, or resolves a conflict between support and product.

This boundary is what the original claim leaves implicit. Before removing a layer, a leader needs a repeatable workflow with clear inputs, tested agent performance, access to the right systems, a record that can be audited, and a person who owns the exceptions. I think flattening should follow demonstrated reliability rather than serve as a starting principle.

The most useful frame from my own work is *domain-mapped ownership*. Each domain has one person who defines what good output looks like, sets operating limits, reviews exceptions, and can change the workflow. That avoids the failure mode where everybody reviews everything, feedback conflicts, and no one can make the final call. Agents can absorb much of the routing, but they cannot absorb accountability.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents reduce hierarchy when they take over proven coordination work, such as gathering context and routing standard cases. Coaching, policy, conflict resolution, and accountability still need human owners.</p></div>

## The compounding advantage

KC's eighth reflection extends the argument to every job: *“Every role has a suffix: ‘agent manager.’ It is not enough to just do your role; you need to be able to wield agents and use them in a way that significantly amplifies you or your team.”* An agent manager is a domain expert who can specify work for agents, check their results, and improve the workflow over time.

That changes a role without erasing its craft. An editor can use agents to prepare drafts, collect sources for claims, and flag repeated language before the final edit. A finance analyst can have agents pull and reconcile records, then surface anomalies for investigation. A customer-success lead can triage cases and prepare account summaries, preserving more attention for relationship risk and difficult conversations.

Simkin argues that the advantage compounds because saved time and higher margins can be reinvested, while systems improve through more data and human corrections. The mechanism depends on those corrections changing the instructions, source material, policies, or tests used by the workflow. Otherwise the same errors simply recur at higher speed.

I don't know a defensible productivity multiplier that applies across companies. The gain depends on task volume, the cost of mistakes, baseline quality, and how much human review remains. The strategic point is about the direction of learning: a team that reinvests saved effort into a better system can improve faster than one that keeps adding isolated tools to the old process.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agent management can multiply a domain expert's capacity, and the gain compounds when human corrections improve the underlying workflow. The size of that gain must be measured in the work itself.</p></div>

## Distributed ownership needs governance

KC's warning that things may seem chaotic describes the surface of bottom-up innovation. More decisions happen close to the work, so leaders see more local movement and fewer requests for permission. That can be productive, but chaos itself is not evidence that the design works.

The safeguard is explicit *decision rights*, meaning a written answer to who may decide what and when an issue must be escalated. Reversible decisions that stay within one domain, budget, quality standard, and policy boundary can usually sit close to the work. Company strategy, major capital allocation, security and privacy rules, legal obligations, cross-domain conflicts, and high-impact people or customer decisions still need central authority.

A leader can distinguish distributed ownership from confusion by looking for a named owner, a shared measure of success, a record of what the agents and people did, and an escalation path. If two teams change the same workflow in incompatible ways, agents follow conflicting policies, or exceptions bounce between people, ownership is missing. The response is to clarify the boundary and the final decision owner instead of pulling every routine choice back to the top.

Simkin's design question remains useful: *“If I were building this business today, from scratch, knowing what AI can do, would it look like this?”* Applied honestly, it can remove handoffs and work that survives mainly because it is familiar. The same exercise should preserve controls that still protect customers, employees and the company. I think this is the discipline the chaos argument needs: leaders can tolerate local experimentation only when its risks remain visible.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Distributed ownership works when local authority has a named owner, measurable limits, a decision record, and an escalation path. Leaders should centralize shared risks and cross-company choices, not every routine action.</p></div>

## The architecture is the strategy

KC and Simkin are restating an old idea: the structure of an organization shapes what it can produce. What has changed is that an AI agent can now participate in a workflow instead of merely making one task faster. For repeatable digital work, the cost of maintaining several relay layers may eventually exceed the cost of redesigning the process. Ambiguous, regulated, or deeply human work will often keep more human coordination.

The harder question is what happens to the people whose coordination tasks shrink. People are not absorbed by agents; parts of their work are. Some managers can become domain owners or agent managers. Others may retrain, move elsewhere in the company, or leave. Organizations I have advised during AI-first reorganizations have handled this differently, but the human transition has been harder than the technical one.

A flatter organization also needs a different account of ambition. If there are fewer management layers, progression can no longer depend mainly on acquiring more direct reports. Companies will need credible paths through deeper craft, larger domain ownership, technical leadership, or responsibility that crosses domains. I don't have a clean answer for how many people can make that transition, and pretending otherwise would turn an organizational argument into a headcount slogan.

My take is to begin with one workflow. Draw the target process, name the human owner, automate the routine path, write the escalation rules, and measure quality before changing the reporting line. Then use what the team learns to redesign the next part of the company. The real test of AI-first leadership is whether you can change the chart while remaining accountable for the people and decisions it used to hold.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An AI-first redesign replaces routine relay work with tested agent workflows and redraws careers around ownership rather than layers. The structure is credible only when its authority and escalation paths account for the human consequences as carefully as the automation.</p></div>
