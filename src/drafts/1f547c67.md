An AI-first company starts with two linked claims. Every business role gains a technical dimension, and organizations can move more ownership toward the people closest to the work as AI agents absorb part of execution and coordination.

An AI agent is software that uses a generative AI model to interpret a goal, choose steps, and act through approved tools. Its arrival does not make coding a universal job requirement, and one successful agent does not justify removing managers. The real question is whether the operating model around the agent has changed enough to support a different organization.

I think that distinction matters. A company becomes AI-first when it redesigns complete workflows, decision rights, controls, roles, and careers around the new capability. If it merely accelerates isolated tasks, the old hierarchy still has to carry the work between them.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Distributed technical judgment and a flatter organization are connected possibilities, but the second must be earned through workflow evidence, visible authority, and a responsible workforce transition.</p></div>

## Technical judgment belongs in every function

In a recorded DECODE interview about people and organization, Arvind KC, a people leader at OpenAI, the AI company behind ChatGPT, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” His qualification is essential: the technical dimension is applied to a domain.

A finance leader does not need to become a software engineer. That leader does need to know which source data an agent may use, where a generated explanation can be trusted, which controls must pass before a payment, and when a person must decide. The same logic applies in sales, operations, marketing, and support. Technical judgment means understanding what AI can do reliably, where it fails, and what evidence is needed before its output affects a customer or the company.

KC’s linked observation about bottom-up innovation follows from this distribution of judgment. People inside each domain see its weak signals, awkward exceptions, and useful opportunities before a central programme can. The central AI team still supplies shared platforms and boundaries, but it cannot remain the sole source of ideas or approval for every experiment.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to evaluate AI inside its own domain; central teams provide common capabilities and guardrails, while useful discovery moves closer to the work.</p></div>

## The Retrofit Trap begins between tasks

Benjamin Simkin, author of the business book *The AI First Company*, calls a familiar failure mode the “Retrofit Trap”: adding AI to an existing process while leaving the surrounding operating design intact. The tool may improve one task, yet manual routing, approvals, duplicate entry, and repair work continue to determine the performance of the whole workflow.

Consider a hypothetical customer refund. An AI assistant might draft an excellent reply, but a person still has to find the order, check eligibility, request approval, enter the refund, update the case, and correct any mismatch. The language task became faster. The refund workflow did not.

This is where organizational architecture enters the argument. A traditional hierarchy does more than supervise people. It moves information upward, sends approvals downward, and routes exceptions to someone with enough authority to resolve them. An agent-supported workflow may take over information retrieval, status updates, routine checks, and low-risk routing. People still have to set policy, own consequential exceptions, resolve conflicts between domains, and carry accountability for the outcome.

A flatter structure becomes plausible only when the coordination work has actually disappeared. Moving it into an invisible repair queue, or asking fewer managers to absorb more exceptions, is not organizational redesign.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Retrofit Trap hides in the handoffs around an AI-assisted task. Flattening becomes credible only when the complete workflow removes routine routing and repair rather than relocating them.</p></div>

## A flatter structure has to earn its case

The unit of evidence should be a correctly closed case, not a model response. A correctly closed refund means the customer received the right outcome, the money and records agree, required controls passed, and no hidden clean-up remained for another team.

For any candidate workflow, I would put six decision questions in front of the executive team. How often does the case close correctly from beginning to end? How many human routing touches remain? How much repair effort appears later? What happens to service quality and control failures? What is the total cost, including review, incidents, and platform operation? Which decisions still require human judgment, and why?

These are proposed operating questions, not an established industry scorecard. The sources behind this argument do not establish a Swedish or Norwegian numerical benchmark for when a management layer is safe to remove. A defensible threshold has to reflect the organization’s own risk, case mix, obligations, and tolerance for failure.

Evidence also has to include ordinary pressure: unusual cases, policy changes, staff absence, and a failed tool call. If performance depends on a few experts quietly rescuing the workflow, the coordination layer still exists even if the dashboard cannot see it. Once repeated evidence shows that routine routing has gone, managers may be able to support broader domains and more decisions can remain at the edge. Coaching, conflict resolution, and accountability do not vanish with the handoffs.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Measure complete, correctly closed work across human touches, repair, quality, control, cost, and residual judgment. Delayering is an organization-specific conclusion from that evidence, not a standard automation threshold.</p></div>

## Put authority where everyone can see it

Removing a coordination layer does not remove the need for authority. It makes the replacement architecture more important. Start with a domain map: a plain record of which business area owns each outcome, policy, data set, exception class, and risk. Then define decision rights, meaning who may decide what, within which limit, and where the case goes when that limit is reached.

The generative AI model can interpret a request and propose the next action. Ordinary rules-based software should enforce identity, permissions, and current case state, which is the authoritative record of what has already happened. It should also enforce idempotency, the technical property that lets a failed step be retried without issuing a second refund. An audit log must record who or what acted, on which information, under which policy, and with what result.

Ownership should be just as concrete. A business domain owner is accountable for the outcome and exception policy. A platform owner controls agent identities, tool access, model changes, and technical rollback, which restores the last safe configuration. A named operational owner can override a proposed action, stop the workflow, and take responsibility for unresolved cases. Risk, security, and audit functions test whether those controls work rather than relying on a model’s explanation of its own behaviour.

This separation keeps complexity under control. The agent handles interpretation within a boundary; the control system enforces the boundary; people own its purpose and consequences. Bottom-up ownership then has room to grow without turning permission into guesswork.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A governed AI-first architecture combines domain ownership and explicit decision rights with software-enforced permissions, state, safe retries, audit, override, stop, and rollback. Agents interpret inside those boundaries; accountable people set and own them.</p></div>

The mapping is about organizational authority, not machine intent. When an enterprise agent can approve, route, or execute work but nobody can name its policy owner, permission boundary, current state, or stop authority, consequential power has moved while accountability has stayed hidden.

## The old pyramid carried more than approvals

A hierarchy is also a career system. It supplies coaching, status, pay progression, access to larger decisions, and a visible path for ambition. If an AI-first design reduces coordination-heavy roles, leaders cannot treat those functions as incidental losses.

Some roles will become broader domain roles, some will focus more on coaching or complex judgment, and some may disappear. That transition creates real questions about who gets training, how people qualify for the new work, whether expert progression pays as well as managing a large team, and where junior employees learn when fewer coordination roles remain.

A credible plan therefore maps the human transition with the same care as the workflow. For every affected role, leaders should state which tasks are expected to go, which judgment remains valuable, what new roles are available, how selection will work, and what time and support people receive to learn. Employees and their representatives should enter that design before outcomes are fixed. Where no realistic transition exists, the company should say so early and provide fair support rather than hiding a workforce reduction inside an automation programme.

The replacement career architecture must reward expertise, domain ownership, teaching, and sound decisions without making headcount the main route to status. Otherwise the company asks people to accept a flatter organization while preserving a pyramid-shaped definition of success.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Delayering also removes parts of the career system. A responsible redesign must replace its coaching, learning, pay, progression, and status functions while dealing honestly with roles that change or disappear.</p></div>

## Put the organization decision after the operating decision

Before changing the org chart, a CIO should require four things. First, complete-workflow evidence must show correctly closed cases under normal and difficult conditions. Second, an authority map must name domain owners, decision limits, exceptions, overrides, stop authority, audit, and rollback. Third, the cost case must include human review, repair, controls, incidents, and the complexity of running the agent system. Fourth, a funded people plan must cover changed roles, learning, career progression, pay, consultation, redeployment, and fair exits where necessary.

The sequence matters because an organizational layer should be removed only after its routine coordination work is gone. If the agent is stopped tomorrow, leaders should already know where open cases go, who can act, and how the organization returns to a safe mode. An answer that depends on an unnamed team repairing the queue is evidence that the redesign is unfinished.

An AI-first company can become materially flatter, with more ownership at its edges. That future is defensible when authority becomes more visible, complete workflows perform without concealed labour, and people know what will replace the old pyramid. The CIO should change the hierarchy because the operating model has changed, not use the hierarchy as a bet that it might.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Take the org-chart decision only after workflow proof, explicit authority, full-cost control, safe fallback, and a funded workforce transition are in place. Flatter is an earned operating result, not an AI ambition.</p></div>
