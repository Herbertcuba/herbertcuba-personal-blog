Two claims sit at the heart of an AI-first company. Every business role acquires a technical dimension, and organizations can become flatter as AI agents absorb routine execution and coordination. Here, an AI agent means software that uses a generative model and approved tools to pursue a goal with bounded autonomy.

The claims are connected, but they do not carry the same level of proof. Distributed technical judgment is a capability leaders can develop now. A flatter structure is an operating-model choice that must be earned in real workflows. I think that distinction is the leadership issue: the org chart should move only after the work, authority, controls, and career system have been redesigned together.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>AI can support a flatter company, but the capability comes before the structural claim and workflow evidence comes before a change to the hierarchy.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment moves into every function

[Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), appointed in 2026 as chief people officer of OpenAI, an AI research and deployment company, described the capability shift in a [DECODE interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ): “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role,” with that role “applied to a particular domain.” The useful word here is *applied*. His point does not require every salesperson, controller, marketer, or support specialist to become a software engineer.

It does require technical judgment: the ability to decide what AI can handle reliably, what evidence would reveal a failure, and where accountable human judgment must remain. A sales leader needs to understand when an agent may prepare an offer but not change a commercial term. Finance must distinguish document matching from approval of an unusual liability. Operations, marketing, and support need equivalent boundaries for their own work.

I take the structural implication one step further. When that judgment sits inside each domain, ideas can originate closer to the work instead of waiting for a central AI team. This is bottom-up innovation in a practical sense: domain experts identify a problem, direct a bounded experiment, and own the result. From the centre it may look untidy, but clear boundaries can make the edges empowered without making them unmanaged. None of this proves that a management layer is redundant.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to direct and challenge AI in its own domain; that enables bottom-up ownership without turning everyone into a coder or making managers automatically obsolete.</p>
</div>

## A faster task can preserve the old company

Benjamin Simkin’s 2026 business book [*The AI First Company*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5) calls the practice of adding AI to an unchanged organization the *Retrofit Trap*. The model may accelerate one activity while the surrounding handoffs, approvals, and repairs continue exactly as before.

Consider supplier onboarding. An agent can read an application and draft a risk summary in seconds, yet an employee may still chase missing documents, email the summary to three approvers, copy data into a procurement system, and repair exceptions in a spreadsheet. The visible task is faster. The coordination system is untouched, so the hierarchy still has to route information and resolve every break.

An AI-first redesign starts with the complete flow. Approved tools may collect documents, check completeness, propose a risk category, update the business system after approval, and notify the right people. Humans still set policy, decide material exceptions, and accept business risk. What changes is the volume of routine work that must travel upward merely to be translated, queued, or chased.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up an isolated activity while preserving manual routing and repair. A flatter structure becomes plausible only when the complete workflow needs less coordination.</p>
</div>

## Flattening has to be earned in the workflow

A polished agent response is weak evidence for an organizational redesign. The useful unit is a *correctly closed case*: the intended business outcome was reached, policy was followed, official records were updated, and no hidden human repair was needed to make the result look complete.

A trial should therefore cover a representative mix of routine cases, missing information, conflicting information, and genuine exceptions. Its record should show correctly closed cases, human routing touches, repair effort, quality and control failures, total end-to-end cost, and residual judgment. Residual judgment means the decisions that still require a person to interpret ambiguity, weigh competing duties, or accept risk.

These measures are an internal decision contract, not an industry benchmark. No Swedish or Norwegian production benchmark in the evidence for this article establishes a safe point for removing a management layer. Each company has to set its own acceptance conditions against the existing workflow and its risk appetite before the trial begins. Otherwise success can be declared after seeing the result.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Judge complete and correctly closed cases, including hidden repair and residual judgment. Set company-specific acceptance conditions before the trial rather than borrowing or inventing a universal threshold.</p>
</div>

## The replacement architecture makes authority visible

Removing a coordination layer does not remove the decisions that layer carried. The replacement begins with a domain map that names the business owner for each outcome and the people who may grant permissions, approve exceptions, override a proposal, stop the workflow, and review its audit trail. A model can interpret a document or propose an action, but it should not invent its own authority.

Rules-based software should enforce identity, permissions, limits, and authoritative state, meaning the current approved status held in the company’s official system. A simple state path might be **proposed → approved → executed → reconciled**. Reconciliation is the check that the requested action, the external result, and the official record agree. A stale request or an over-limit action should be rejected without changing that record.

Retries need duplicate protection as well. The Internet Engineering Task Force, the organization that develops internet standards, defines *idempotency* in its [2022 HTTP standard](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods) as repeated identical requests having the same intended effect as one request. In business terms, a timeout must not create a second supplier record when the agent tries again.

Ownership completes the control design. The domain executive holds stop authority, the explicit right to suspend the workflow when a limit or control fails. The platform owner is accountable for technical rollback, returning the workflow to its last approved version, while the domain owner decides how to handle cases already affected. These are established permission, workflow, and record controls applied to agents. They should remain inspectable even when the model’s reasoning is uncertain.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Agents may interpret and propose, while ordinary software enforces permissions and official state. Named humans must own exceptions, stop decisions, affected cases, audit, and technical rollback.</p>
</div>

## A flatter company needs a new career system

The traditional pyramid is also a people system. It distributes coaching, status, pay, progression, and access to consequential work. If agents reduce roles built around collecting updates, chasing approvals, and routing exceptions, leaders cannot treat the affected people as an implementation detail.

A credible transition starts by mapping tasks rather than assuming whole jobs disappear. It names the work likely to shrink, the judgment that remains, and the new responsibilities created around domain ownership, control review, coaching, and workflow improvement. It also commits learning time, redeployment options, fair selection principles, and a route to comparable pay and status for experts who progress without becoming managers.

The entry path needs attention too. Routine coordination has often given junior employees context and supervised practice. When agents take more of it, the company must create deliberate ways to learn the domain, observe exceptions, and earn wider authority. In a Nordic organization, HR and employee representatives belong in this design from the start, before structural savings are presented as a finished decision.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Flattening changes the career and learning system as well as the workflow. Leadership must fund progression, coaching, participation, and transition before claiming the old pyramid is no longer needed.</p>
</div>

## Change the chart only after the decision record holds

The CIO should require a signed operating record before proposing a structural change. The domain executive convenes the review. The process owner prepares the baseline and representative case set; an evidence owner independent of daily delivery verifies outcomes and hidden human effort; finance validates end-to-end cost; the risk owner signs the limits and exception path; the technology owner signs the controls and owns rollback; HR and employee representatives record the workforce commitments.

The group should agree its review cadence and acceptance conditions before launch, then reconvene after any material control failure. Its choices are explicit: continue, narrow the scope, stop and reverse the workflow, or recommend a structural change. The domain executive exercises stop authority, while the executive forum approves any change to roles or reporting lines and records who owns the consequences.

That sequence matters. Complete-workflow evidence shows whether coordination has actually disappeared. The control map shows where authority went. The transition plan shows whether leadership is prepared to carry the human cost and opportunity. Until all three hold, changing the org chart is speculation. Once they do, a flatter company becomes a defensible operating decision rather than a reflexive response to impressive technology.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Before changing reporting lines, require a signed record of workflow outcomes, control ownership, review decisions, rollback, and workforce commitments. The org chart changes last.</p>
</div>
