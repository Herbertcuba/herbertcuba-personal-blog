Before removing a management layer, ask which decisions that layer makes and why they have to travel through it. Faster software may reduce the effort inside a task while leaving every approval, exception, and accountability question exactly where it was. In that case, the work moves faster but the organization has not changed.

Here, an [AI agent](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/) means software that gives an AI model instructions and access to specific tools, then lets it continue until the task is complete or human help is required. Its practical value is continuity: the same system can read an input, update a record, and pause when a case falls outside its permission. That wider span is what makes agents relevant to organization design.

*[The AI First Company](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)* appeared on 10 May 2026 as an independently published book by [Benjamin Simkin](https://bensimkin.com/), who describes his work as business architecture and scaling. The book uses “AI-first” for a company that begins process design with what AI can demonstrably do. Simkin calls the easier alternative the “Retrofit Trap”: adding AI to work designed for manual human operation while leaving the structure around it intact.

I think the practical dividing line is whether the company redesigns tasks or complete operating loops. An operating loop is the full path from an input to an action, including the exceptions, decisions, and feedback that keep the work moving. Once agents carry more of that path, fewer organizational layers may be useful. But the chart should change last, after authority and career progression have somewhere credible to go.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents matter to organization design when they carry work across a complete operating loop. A flatter chart becomes credible only after the workflow, human authority, and path for employee growth have all been redesigned.</p></div>

## Build one operating loop

The Retrofit Trap is easiest to see in the gap between a polished AI output and the work surrounding it. A company may use an agent to read a document or draft a recommendation, then rely on people to find the owner, request missing information, chase approval, update the system, and tell everyone what happened. The automated task looks impressive, but the old coordination burden remains.

Consider a hypothetical employee-expense process. An agent could read the receipt, compare the claim with current policy, request missing evidence, approve a routine claim within a defined limit, post it to the finance system, and route an unusual case to a named finance owner. That is more than document analysis. The agent is carrying the work between stages while preserving a human path for cases that require judgment.

The loop also has to survive contact with reality. Leaders need to know where it waits, how often people repair the agent’s work, which exceptions escape its rules, and whether coordination quietly returns through email or chat. I think those observations are a better basis for redesign than a target number of managers. If the relay keeps reappearing, the workflow is unfinished.

This does not mean every complete loop removes a role. It means leaders can finally separate coordination that exists because information is hard to move from work that remains valuable because people need coaching, priorities conflict, or consequences require human accountability. Only the first category is a candidate for absorption by software.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Automating the visible task is insufficient if people still carry every handoff around it. Map the complete loop, including repairs and exceptions, before deciding that an organizational layer has lost its purpose.</p></div>

## Put authority where context lives

Even after the loop is complete, a second question remains: who has the right to decide? Decision rights are the explicit rules for who may approve an action, override the agent, stop the process, and answer for the result. If those rights stay vague, the company replaces a visible chain of command with an invisible one.

In the expense example, a finance owner should set the approval limit, define acceptable evidence, choose which policy source is authoritative, and receive serious exceptions. The people using the process should be able to see why a claim was approved or escalated. The technical team may build and maintain the agent, but it should not quietly inherit authority over financial policy merely because it understands the software.

This is why technical literacy has to spread beyond a technical department, without turning every employee into a programmer. A person who owns an agent-supported decision must understand what information the system used, where its limits sit, how failure becomes visible, and when human review is required. Otherwise authority moves away from the people who know the business consequence.

I think a flatter company is earned when context, authority, and accountability meet close to the work. Some managers will still be needed because they resolve conflict, allocate scarce resources, or develop people. Others may discover that most of their role consisted of moving information between people who can now act directly. The distinction has to come from the work, not from enthusiasm for a thinner chart.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Software can carry a workflow, but a named person must still own its limits and consequences. Fewer layers work only when people near the decision have enough technical understanding to exercise real authority and remain accountable.</p></div>

## Change the career bargain before the chart

Once routine coordination shrinks, the traditional route to seniority becomes less reliable. Many companies have rewarded growth by giving someone more people to manage and more information to control. If agents now carry much of that information and routine follow-up, removing layers also removes rungs from the career ladder.

The replacement cannot be an inspirational promise that everyone will become more strategic. People need to see how responsibility expands in the new structure. Progress might come through ownership of a more consequential operating loop, deeper expertise, responsibility for higher-risk decisions, or the ability to improve other people’s judgment. Those paths need clear expectations, recognition, and pay, or management will remain the only credible form of advancement even after the management work has changed.

I don’t know how many people whose roles center on coordination will want, or be able, to make that transition. A serious redesign should say which activities are ending, which human responsibilities remain, what support is available, and how people can build evidence that they are ready for broader scope. That uncertainty makes a direct explanation more important.

My take is that a flatter chart without a new career bargain is simply a reduction in roles. I think an AI-first company becomes a real organizational model only when it can show three things in operation: software carries a complete loop, human authority is easier to locate, and people can gain scope without recreating the old relay. The chart should record those choices once the work makes them real.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>When agents absorb routine coordination, career growth must shift toward wider ownership, deeper expertise, harder decisions, and the development of others. Redraw reporting lines only after the company can explain how responsibility and opportunity will grow in the structure that replaces them.</p></div>
