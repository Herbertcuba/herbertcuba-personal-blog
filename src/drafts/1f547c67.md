An AI-first company changes who can act and how work moves. It begins with two linked propositions. Technical judgment, the ability to decide where AI is useful, reliable, and bounded, becomes part of every business role. As more routine execution and coordination move into software, decisions and improvements can originate closer to the work, making a flatter organization possible.

Here, an [AI agent](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/) means software that uses a generative model, a system that interprets and produces language or other content, to pursue a goal through approved tools with limited autonomy. An agent may gather information, propose an action, update a record, or route an exception. None of that proves that a management layer is ready to disappear.

I think this distinction is the leadership issue. Distributed technical judgment is a capability to build now. A flatter structure is an operating hypothesis that must earn its way into the org chart through evidence, explicit authority, controlled complexity, and a credible transition for the people whose work changes.

  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>AI-first design links broader technical judgment with more ownership at the edges, but organizational flattening remains conditional until the company proves that whole chains of coordination have actually been removed.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment moves into the domain

In [a recorded interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ), Arvind KC, then Chief People Officer at OpenAI, an AI research and deployment company, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately placed that technical dimension inside a person’s domain. He also described innovation at OpenAI as bottom-up, with ideas emerging throughout the organization rather than only from a central function.

That observation does not mean everyone must code. A sales leader needs to know when an AI-produced account summary is grounded in current customer data. A finance specialist needs to recognize when a variance explanation rests on a weak assumption. A support lead needs to decide which customer situations can follow policy and which require human discretion. In each case, technical judgment combines domain knowledge with an understanding of the system’s limits.

KC’s account of one AI company is not proof that a large Nordic enterprise can safely remove layers. It does explain why bottom-up ownership becomes more plausible. When people near the work can frame a problem for AI, test the result, and recognize failure, they do not need to send every improvement through a central technology queue.

From the centre, that distribution of initiative may look untidy. The answer is domain ownership: a named business owner remains accountable for outcomes, boundaries, and exceptions in a defined area, even when many people within it can improve the work. Local initiative becomes useful when accountability stays visible.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to use AI within its own expertise and recognize its limits. That capability supports bottom-up improvement, while named domain owners keep the resulting freedom accountable.</p>
</div>

## A task demo cannot justify a new org chart

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, calls the practice of adding AI to an unchanged process the “Retrofit Trap.” The name is useful because a faster task can leave the operating model almost untouched.

Consider a hypothetical supplier invoice that does not match its purchase order. An agent produces a clear summary in seconds, but an accounts-payable specialist still emails the buyer, a manager chooses the route, a controller approves the exception, and another employee updates the finance record. The summary improved. The chain of routing, approval, and repair did not.

An AI-first trial would test the full path instead. The agent could gather the invoice, purchase order, delivery record, and policy; identify the discrepancy; and propose a permitted resolution. Routine cases might proceed within approved limits, while novel, ambiguous, or material exceptions go to a named human owner. The case is finished only when the decision is executed, the official finance record is current, the supplier receives the right response, and the evidence is available for review.

This exposes what hierarchy often does. It moves information, grants permission, routes exceptions, resolves conflict, coaches people, and carries accountability. Agents may absorb part of the information and routing load. Human authority remains necessary for material trade-offs, novel exceptions, resource choices, coaching, and accountable decisions. A layer can shrink only when the coordination work attached to it has genuinely declined, rather than being pushed into hidden manual repair.

For that reason, I would evaluate a **correctly closed case**: a case with the right business outcome, updated official records, an inspectable decision trail, no unresolved control failure, and no unrecorded human repair. The trial should cover a representative mix of routine work, known exceptions, and cases that require judgment. It should record human routing touches, repair effort, quality, control failures, total cost, and residual judgment, meaning the decisions people still have to make after routine steps are handled.

This is a proposed decision test, not an industry benchmark. The evidence assembled for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production benchmark for safely removing management layers, so a CIO should resist borrowed percentages and universal thresholds. Each organization must define its case mix, baseline, success conditions, and stop conditions before the trial begins.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up one task while preserving its surrounding handoffs. Flattening becomes defensible only when representative cases close correctly with less routing, repair, cost, and unresolved human work across the full workflow.</p>
</div>

## Put decision rights before model discretion

Removing visible hierarchy without replacing its control function can make authority harder to find. The safer architecture separates interpretation from permission. A generative model may read an invoice and propose a resolution; rule-based transaction software, which applies explicit conditions rather than free-form language, enforces identity, permissions, amount limits, and the current case state.

A controlled case path might be `proposed → approved → executed → reconciled`. **Reconciliation** means checking that the official financial record matches what the workflow believes happened. If another actor has changed the invoice, a version check rejects the stale proposal without changing the official state. If the amount exceeds the agent’s permission, the action waits for the designated approver. These are established workflow and ledger controls applied to agents, not new powers granted to a model.

Retries need the same discipline. **Idempotency** means that repeated identical requests have the same intended effect as one request, a principle formalized in the Internet Engineering Task Force’s [2022 HTTP standard](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods). The Internet Engineering Task Force is the standards body responsible for many core internet protocols. Duplicate-safe requests reduce the risk of executing a payment twice, while reconciliation checks what the external finance system actually recorded.

The ownership model should be equally plain. The domain owner defines valid outcomes and exception classes. A risk or process owner sets limits and approval policy. The technology owner controls tool access, the **system of record**, meaning the official source of current case state, and the audit log. A named operational owner handles overrides and holds **stop authority**, the right to pause the automated path. The same map assigns rollback, which is responsibility for returning work to the last approved process or state when a control fails.

Auditability then has a practical meaning: the organization can reconstruct which person or agent proposed an action, which rule permitted it, what changed, who overrode it, and how the case ended. Authority remains human and inspectable even when execution becomes more distributed.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Agents can interpret and propose, while explicit software rules enforce permissions and state. Named owners must remain visible for policy, exceptions, overrides, stopping, audit, and reversal.</p>
</div>

## A flatter company needs a different career system

Coordination-heavy roles will change if agents take over status collection, handoff chasing, routine approval checks, and first-line exception routing. Some roles will broaden around judgment and ownership. Others may narrow or end. Leaders should say that plainly, because a workforce transition cannot be made credible through a promise that everyone will simply move to “higher-value work.”

The old pyramid is also a career system. It links progression and pay to team size, creates coaching relationships, and gives ambitious employees a visible next step. Remove layers without replacing those functions and the company may offer more responsibility at the edges while weakening recognition, learning, and support.

A new design therefore needs paths based on domain depth, technical judgment, coaching, control stewardship, and responsibility for cross-functional outcomes. Pay bands must recognize the scope and consequences of those roles. Managers whose routing work declines may become stronger coaches, domain leaders, or control owners, but those are real jobs with defined expectations, not reassuring labels applied after a restructuring decision.

Before a structural proposal advances, the business and people leaders should map the work likely to disappear, the people affected, the skills required in the new model, the funded learning time, available redeployment, changes to pay and progression, and fair terms where no credible role exists. In Nordic organizations, collective agreements and employee-participation processes belong in that design from the start, with the relevant employee representatives involved at the proper stage.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Flattening alters jobs, coaching, status, pay, and progression as well as reporting lines. The transition is credible only when new career paths and support are designed early, and when leaders are honest about roles that may shrink or end.</p>
</div>

## Decide through one signed operating record

A structural redesign should enter executive governance as a bounded decision, rather than as a reward for an impressive demonstration. Begin with one workflow whose business owner, case boundary, risks, and affected roles can be named. Then create a single operating record that follows the trial from baseline to decision.

The responsibilities should be set before live work begins:

1. The domain executive and trial lead prepare the workflow boundary, representative case mix, current baseline, and role-change hypothesis.
2. An evidence owner who is independent of the agent build team verifies outcomes, human effort, failures, total cost, and remaining judgment.
3. Technology, security, and risk owners sign the permission model, state controls, audit path, stop conditions, and rollback plan.
4. The people executive records workforce commitments and confirms that the relevant employee-relations and participation processes have begun.
5. A CIO-led operating-model forum reviews the same record at a cadence fixed in advance according to case volume and risk.

The forum should see results by case type, including stopped, repaired, and escalated cases, rather than one blended average. Its available decisions are to continue the trial, narrow its scope, stop and roll back, or advance a structural proposal. The record names the person who can order each action and the owner responsible for restoring the previous workflow if the trial is reversed.

Before a proposal reaches the board, the domain executive, CIO, risk or control owner, and people executive should sign the evidence and commitments within their responsibility. Success thresholds are local decisions, but they must be written before results arrive. That prevents a polished demo or a favourable average from quietly becoming a mandate to remove roles.

If the record shows that coordination still survives as manual routing, invisible repair, or unresolved judgment, redesign the workflow and keep the necessary layer. If the complete path works within its controls, authority remains inspectable, costs hold, and the people commitments are funded, then a flatter structure becomes a defensible operating choice. The board can see both what the organization is ready to remove and what it has chosen to preserve.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>The CIO should bring one signed record to the structural decision: verified workflow evidence, explicit controls, named signatories, workforce commitments, pre-agreed review criteria, and owners for stopping and reversal. The org chart changes only when that record supports it.</p>
</div>
