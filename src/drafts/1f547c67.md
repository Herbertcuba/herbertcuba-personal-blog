An AI-first company starts from two linked propositions. Technical judgment spreads into every role, and the organization can become flatter when AI agents absorb meaningful execution and coordination. An AI agent here means a system that can interpret a goal, use approved tools, and take several steps toward an outcome rather than merely produce text.

Arvind KC, speaking about people leadership at OpenAI, the company that develops ChatGPT, argued that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He explained that the technical dimension is applied to a domain, meaning a business area such as finance, sales, or operations. He also described innovation as increasingly bottom-up because people close to the work can see where the technology helps.

That does not turn every employee into a programmer. It gives every function a responsibility to understand what AI can do reliably, what data and permissions it needs, how failure appears, and where a person must decide. I think flatter structures become possible when that distributed judgment is combined with agents that remove real coordination work. Until both conditions exist, changing the org chart is speculation.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first means distributing technical judgment and redesigning work around agents. A flatter company is a possible result, but only after execution and coordination have changed in practice.</p></div>

## Technical judgment belongs in the business

Technical judgment is now part of ordinary business judgment. A salesperson needs to know whether an agent may prepare an account brief from approved records and when uncertain customer data makes that brief unsafe. A finance lead needs to distinguish reliable invoice matching from an exception that changes tax, cash, or fraud risk. A support manager needs to know which cases can close automatically and which require discretion.

This is where many transformations stall. Benjamin Simkin, author of the management book *The AI First Company*, calls it the **Retrofit Trap**: adding AI to an isolated task while leaving the surrounding operating model intact. A support agent may draft a reply in seconds, yet an employee still looks up the order, routes an exception to a manager, copies the decision into another system, triggers the refund, and repairs mismatched records. The sentence became faster; the case did not.

An AI-first operating model redesigns that complete flow, including its handoffs, approvals, exceptions, and records. Bottom-up ownership matters because the people inside a business domain understand those details. Their freedom still needs boundaries, however, because local speed becomes enterprise risk when nobody owns the policy or the consequences.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to design safe uses of AI in its own work. Isolated task acceleration falls into the Retrofit Trap when manual routing, approval, and repair remain untouched.</p></div>

## The workflow earns the flatter structure

In practical terms, a hierarchy does more than supervise people. It gathers information, assigns approvals, resolves exceptions, tracks current status, and confirms that work is complete. An agent-supported workflow may absorb routine parts of that coordination by classifying requests, retrieving policy, asking for missing information, proposing an action, updating a queue, and executing actions that fall within an approved limit.

The human authority left behind is smaller in volume but heavier in consequence. People still set policy and risk appetite, resolve contested exceptions, weigh goals that cannot be reduced to a rule, and remain accountable for stopping the system. Removing a layer before its coordination work has disappeared simply pushes that work onto employees, senior leaders, or an invisible repair team.

For that reason, the useful unit of evidence is a **complete workflow**, from intake to recorded outcome. A **correctly closed case** reaches the right business result, preserves required controls, communicates the outcome, and leaves an accurate record. Leaders should examine the share of correctly closed cases alongside human routing touches, repair effort, quality, control failures, total cost, and **residual judgment**: the decisions that still require a person after routine steps are automated.

A 2023 field experiment involving 758 consultants at Boston Consulting Group, a global management consultancy, illustrates why task averages are dangerous. Harvard Business School researchers found gains on tasks inside the **AI capability frontier**, the boundary of work that OpenAI’s GPT-4 language model could perform reliably, but lower correctness on a task outside that frontier. An impressive drafting result can therefore coexist with a weak end-to-end outcome.

These measures are an evidence agenda, not established benchmarks. I am proposing no Swedish or Norwegian benchmark for how many layers to remove or what success rate makes removal safe. Each organization has to set thresholds from the risk of the workflow and prove them under its own operating conditions.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents justify fewer coordination layers only when complete workflows close correctly with acceptable human effort, quality, control performance, and cost. Task-level speed cannot carry that decision.</p></div>

## Make authority more visible as the company flattens

A flatter organization needs a clearer authority map. **Domain-mapped ownership** assigns each business area a named owner for its outcomes, policies, and exceptions. **Decision rights** then state who may propose, approve, execute, override, or stop each class of action. Without that map, bottom-up initiative can conceal who is actually in charge.

The technical boundary matters as much as the management boundary. Agents are useful for interpreting unstructured requests and proposing actions under uncertainty. Conventional, rules-based software should enforce permissions, approved limits, and **workflow state**, the verified stage a case has reached. It should also provide **idempotency**, which makes a retry safe instead of issuing a second refund, and an audit record showing what happened, under which rule, and with whose authority.

Consider the refund flow. The service-domain owner can define the customer outcome and normal policy; finance can own payment controls; security and risk can define access and escalation limits. The agent may interpret the complaint and recommend or execute a permitted refund, but it should not grant itself access, rewrite the limit, or erase the record. A named leader needs **stop authority**, the power to pause the flow, while an override changes a proposed decision and a **rollback** restores a prior safe state when the action can be reversed.

This model can look untidy from the centre because more decisions originate near customers and operations. Yet the boundaries are stronger: central leaders set enterprise controls, domain owners improve their workflows, and every consequential path remains visible. That is governed distribution, not unmanaged autonomy.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Flatter structures require named domain owners, explicit decision rights, and visible paths for override, stop, and rollback. Agents may interpret and propose, while rules-based software enforces permissions, state, safe retries, and audit records.</p></div>

The organizational mapping is direct: an agent becomes dangerous when its operating instructions are concealed, its authority reaches critical systems, and the humans accountable for the outcome lack a dependable way to inspect or stop it. Intelligence does not settle ownership; the design of authority does.

## Redesign careers with the coordination work

The people consequence cannot wait until the technology case is finished. Coordination-heavy roles often combine routine routing and status collection with judgment, conflict resolution, coaching, and accountability. Agents may absorb much of the first category while leaving the second more important. Leaders therefore need to examine the work inside each role before deciding whether that role disappears, narrows, or becomes a different job.

The evidence does not make that decision for them. The 2023 US National Bureau of Economic Research working paper [*Generative AI at Work*](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161) studied 5,179 customer-support agents and found average productivity gains, with larger gains among novice and lower-skilled workers. Its authors explicitly warned that the study did not establish effects on aggregate employment or wages. A productivity finding is not a workforce plan.

A flatter company also removes familiar promotion steps. If management has been the main route to higher pay, status, and influence, fewer coordination roles can turn a structural improvement into a career dead end. The replacement needs credible expert and domain-owner paths, equal recognition for judgment without direct reports, deliberate coaching, and learning time for employees taking on technical responsibility.

Some transitions will still be painful. A responsible plan names the work expected to decline, the roles likely to change, the options for learning or reassignment, and the owner of each decision. For a Nordic executive, dialogue with employees and their representatives belongs in the design process before choices harden, rather than in a communication package after the fact.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Coordination-heavy jobs must be broken into routine routing and genuinely human responsibility before roles are removed. A flatter company also needs new paths for pay, progression, coaching, learning, and a candid transition process.</p></div>

## Put the evidence before the org chart

Before asking a board to approve a flatter operating model, a CIO should be able to answer four questions with operating evidence and named owners:

1. **Does the complete workflow work?** Show case volume and mix, correctly closed outcomes, human routing touches, repair effort, quality, control failures, total cost, and the judgment that remains with people.
2. **Who holds each decision right?** Name the domain owner, the actions an agent may propose or execute, the owner of exceptions, and the people with override and stop authority. Show how permissions, state, audit, and rollback are enforced.
3. **Which coordination work has actually gone away?** Tie any proposed layer change to proven removal of information gathering, approval routing, status work, or routine exception handling. Define the trial scope and a reversible route if the new model fails.
4. **What is the workforce commitment?** Identify affected roles, future career paths, pay and progression principles, learning support, employee dialogue, and the executive accountable for the transition.

An AI-first company can become materially flatter and more bottom-up. But the org chart should record an operating reality rather than declare a technological ambition. Change the work first, make decision rights explicit, rebuild the career model alongside them, and alter reporting lines only when complete-workflow evidence makes the old coordination layer genuinely redundant.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A board-defensible redesign requires proof that complete workflows perform, authority remains visible, specific coordination work has disappeared, and the workforce transition is owned. Reporting lines should change only after those conditions are met.</p></div>
