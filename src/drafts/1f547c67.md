Two claims define the architecture of an AI-first company. Every business role gains a technical dimension. At the same time, ownership can move closer to the work when AI agents carry routine execution and coordination across functional boundaries.

In [a video interview published on 1 June 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ), Arvind KC, chief people officer at OpenAI, the company behind the ChatGPT generative AI assistant, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately explained that the technical role is applied to a particular domain. In the same interview, he described innovation inside OpenAI as bottom-up.

That is a useful design signal from one unusual company, not evidence that other enterprises can safely remove management layers. The CIO-level question is more demanding: has a complete workflow shed enough routine coordination for authority, roles, and career structures to change without losing control? My answer is yes in principle, but only after the operation earns the redesign.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every role needs stronger technical judgment now. A flatter organization remains a conditional design choice until complete workflows show that routine coordination has actually disappeared.</p></div>

## Technical judgment belongs in every domain

Technical judgment does not mean that everyone must code. It means that people in sales, operations, finance, marketing, and support can judge what an AI system can do reliably, which data and tools it may use, how failure appears in their domain, and when a human must decide. The domain expert still owns the meaning of a good outcome.

This changes where improvement begins. A finance team should not wait for a central AI group to discover every useful control check, just as support leaders should not outsource the definition of a properly resolved case. Central teams can provide shared platforms and limits, while people closest to the work shape the use case and expose its edge conditions. From the centre, that distribution of initiative may look untidy. In practice, visible boundaries can turn apparent disorder into accountable ownership at the edges.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical capability becomes part of each business role when domain experts can shape, test, and supervise AI-supported work within shared enterprise boundaries.</p></div>

## Find the coordination before removing the layer

Benjamin Simkin’s 2026 book, [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls it the “Retrofit Trap” when a company adds AI inside an existing structure instead of rebuilding around what the technology makes possible. The trap is easy to enter because a faster task produces an impressive demonstration even when the surrounding operation stays the same.

Consider a request to increase a customer’s credit limit. An agent could gather account history, check the request against policy, ask for missing evidence, draft a recommendation, and send genuine exceptions to the right risk owner. If managers still copy information between systems, chase approvals, correct malformed requests, and route every case by hand, the organization has accelerated analysis without changing its operating model.

Traditional hierarchy does more than supervise people. It also moves information, grants approvals, resolves exceptions, and repairs work that crosses departmental lines. Agents may absorb parts of that coordination, but executives must still set risk appetite, allocate decision rights, and own consequential exceptions. A layer becomes removable only when its recurring work has gone elsewhere by design, rather than being hidden in review queues or informal support.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up a task while preserving its handoffs. Delayering becomes plausible only when the workflow removes routine routing, approval chasing, and repair work as well.</p></div>

## Demand evidence from the whole workflow

The unit of evidence should be a correctly closed case, measured from request to final outcome. In the credit-limit example, that means an increase approved within policy, a justified rejection, or an exception delivered to the authorized human with the required evidence. A quick recommendation that later creates rework, a control breach, or a duplicate account change is not a successful case.

Before changing the organization, create a decision record that covers four things:

- **Outcome and case mix:** compare the complete workflow with its baseline across ordinary cases and difficult exceptions, using independent quality review.
- **Hidden human work:** count routing touches, review time, repairs, overrides, retries, and repeated runs; report the range of failures and acknowledge when the sample is too small to support a broad conclusion.
- **Full economics and dependency:** include model use, integration, monitoring, incident handling, human review, where data is stored, and whether a supplier can be replaced without rebuilding the workflow.
- **Residual judgment:** identify the decisions that still require human authority, the conditions for stopping or rolling back the workflow, and the roles that gain or lose work.

Set local acceptance criteria and a review date before the trial begins. These categories are a proposed operating test, not an established benchmark. The verified material available for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer. I think the honest conclusion is to treat flattening as a local hypothesis until the full workflow proves it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Measure quality, cost, control, and human effort per correctly closed case. A task-level speed gain cannot justify an organizational change, and no universal Nordic delayering threshold is available here.</p></div>

## Give the new architecture hard boundaries

A flatter company still needs an explicit control structure. Map each workflow to a named business outcome owner, a control owner, and the people responsible for its systems and data. Then state what the agent may gather, propose, approve, or execute; which exceptions require a person; and who can override, stop, restart, and roll back the operation.

Ordinary software should enforce identities, permissions, and authoritative state, meaning the official record of where a case currently stands. An agent may interpret documents and recommend a credit increase, but the workflow system should prevent a case marked “risk review required” from becoming “approved” without the risk owner’s decision. The same principle applies to idempotency, defined in [RFC 9110, the standard for web request semantics](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-9.2.2): retrying the same authorized operation should not create a second intended business effect. A repeated tool call must not raise the credit limit twice.

These software controls are established practice. Their organizational importance lies in making the agent’s mandate enforceable rather than leaving it in a policy document. The audit record should show the case, evidence, requested action, permission used, state before and after, and any human override. Stop authority is effective only when it is formally delegated to a named person, with clear conditions for restart and a tested route back to a safe state.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Domain ownership can move outward only when permissions, case state, audit, overrides, and stop authority remain visible and enforceable.</p></div>

The mapping is about concealed authority. When an AI system can act but no one can inspect its mandate, current state, or conflicting instructions, apparent autonomy hides an ownership failure. A sound operating model makes those boundaries visible and keeps a named human able to stop the workflow.

## Rebuild the human system

Removing coordination work changes more than headcount. Many management and specialist roles combine routing with judgment, coaching, conflict resolution, and accountability. The operating evidence must separate those activities so that routine forwarding is not mistaken for leadership, and valuable human work is not discarded with the queue it once sat beside.

The career architecture must change at the same time. Progression can rest more on domain depth, exception handling, control improvement, coaching, and cross-functional outcomes, rather than depending mainly on team size. Pay and status need credible paths for expert contributors. Paid learning, redeployment capacity, transparent selection criteria, and timely consultation with employee representatives belong in the design before irreversible role decisions are made.

Some roles will still shrink or disappear, and leaders should say so plainly. A responsible transition identifies affected work early, names who funds learning and redeployment, explains how decisions will be made, and provides a fair route for people who cannot move into the new structure. Trust is an operating requirement here because hidden repair work and unreported failures return quickly when people believe the evidence will be used against them.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A flatter structure needs new ways to coach, progress, pay, and develop people, plus an explicit and fair transition for roles built around coordination work.</p></div>

## Authorize the workflow before the org chart

The next executive decision should be bounded. Choose one meaningful workflow, assign its outcome and control owners, approve the decision record, and schedule a scale, extend, or stop review in the executive operating forum. Include workforce representatives and affected domain leaders early enough to shape the trial, not merely react to its result.

Return to the organization design only when correctly closed cases improve, total cost is understood, control failures remain within the predeclared mandate, and the evidence shows that coordination work has truly declined. At that point, leadership can decide whether to widen spans, remove a layer, redefine roles, or keep the existing structure. If the evidence is weak, stop or extend the test without forcing an organizational story onto it.

An AI-first company can distribute technical judgment and become materially flatter. The responsible sequence is to prove where the work moved, make authority inspectable, and commit to the people carrying the transition before asking the org chart to follow.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Authorize a bounded workflow test first. Change the hierarchy only after complete operating evidence, enforceable authority, full economics, and workforce commitments make the decision defensible.</p></div>
