Two claims define the architecture of an AI-first company. Every business role acquires a technical dimension, and the organization can become flatter as AI agents absorb more execution and coordination. The first claim changes who can exercise technical judgment. The second changes where authority can sit.

The word *can* matters. An AI agent is software that can interpret a goal, use approved tools, and propose or carry out steps within set boundaries. Its arrival does not make hierarchy redundant by itself, because hierarchy also routes information, approvals, exceptions, and accountability. A CIO should consider removing a coordination layer only when the underlying work no longer needs that layer and the replacement makes ownership clearer rather than harder to see.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>An AI-first operating model links distributed technical judgment with a potentially flatter organization, but the flatter shape has to be earned through evidence, controls, and accountable ownership.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment belongs in every function

Arvind KC, an OpenAI people leader speaking in a DECODE business interview about the company’s way of working, argued that every future role is fundamentally technical because technical capability is applied within a domain. He also described innovation as bottom-up: people close to the work identify and build better ways of doing it rather than waiting for every idea to come from a central technology function.

That does not mean everyone must code. Technical judgment is the ability to judge what AI can do reliably, what information it may use, how failure would appear, and when a person must decide. In sales and marketing, that includes knowing whether source data is sufficient for a recommendation or an AI-generated claim. In operations, finance, and support, it means distinguishing a routine pattern from an unusual case whose financial, legal, or customer consequence needs an accountable person.

This capability moves design authority closer to each business domain, meaning the part of the company with its own customers, rules, data, and outcomes. Bottom-up ownership still needs boundaries. Domain experts should be able to improve their workflows, while named leaders remain responsible for policy, risk, and results.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Every role gains a technical dimension when people must judge AI’s reliability and limits inside their own domain. That spreads design capability through the business without turning every employee into a programmer.</p>
</div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the hierarchy

Benjamin Simkin, author of the management book *The AI First Company*, calls one common failure the Retrofit Trap: adding AI to an isolated task while leaving the surrounding operating model intact. A supplier-onboarding agent might read documents and draft a risk assessment, for example, while employees still copy data between systems, chase approvals by email, check which version is current, and repair cases the agent handled badly. The visible task becomes faster, but the workflow still depends on the same routing machinery.

This matters because a conventional hierarchy is partly a coordination system. Managers gather local information, reconcile competing priorities, approve deviations, and move exceptions to someone with greater authority. An agent-supported workflow may gather context, compare a case with policy, keep a queue moving, and route a genuine exception directly to its owner. Yet people must still set the policy, resolve conflicting goals, approve consequential exceptions, and own the outcome.

A company has escaped the Retrofit Trap only when those surrounding handoffs change. If AI produces more output while people spend their time checking, forwarding, and repairing it, the organization has accelerated a task without creating an AI-first operating model.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up one activity but preserves manual routing, approvals, and repair. Organizational flattening becomes plausible only when complete workflows need less coordination, not when one task produces output faster.</p>
</div>

## A flatter structure has to be earned

The evidence bar should be higher than a successful demonstration. A field experiment summarized by Harvard Business School, the US management school, involved 758 consultants and found better performance on tasks inside AI’s capability frontier, the shifting boundary of work a model can handle well. On a task outside that boundary, access to AI reduced correctness. The study is useful because it shows why leaders must test the actual work, but it does not establish that a company can safely remove management layers.

I think the right unit of evidence is the correctly closed case across a complete workflow. “Correctly closed” means that the intended outcome was reached, required controls passed, and no hidden rework or reopening was needed. For each representative case type, leaders should compare elapsed time, human routing touches, repair effort, quality, control failures, and total cost, including supervision and new software dependencies. They should also record the residual judgment: the decisions that still require a person to weigh ambiguity, competing goals, or consequences.

These measures are a proposed operating test, not an established benchmark. The evidence behind this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that sets a numerical threshold for safe removal of management layers. Each company therefore has to establish a baseline, test enough normal and difficult cases to expose failure patterns, and agree in advance what evidence would pause or reverse the redesign.

Flattening is justified where coordination work has demonstrably disappeared. Where it has merely moved into exception queues, manual repair, or an overstretched domain owner, the layer is still doing work even if its title has been removed.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Use complete, correctly closed cases to test whether agents remove coordination. Count handoffs, repair, quality, control failures, full cost, and remaining human judgment; task-level gains alone cannot defend a flatter structure.</p>
</div>

## Put authority into the architecture

A flatter company needs more visible control, not less. Start with a domain map that names the owner of each workflow and case type. Then write decision rights in operational terms: what an agent may do, what it may only propose, what requires approval, who owns an exception, and who can stop the workflow.

Ordinary software should enforce the hard boundaries. It should verify identity and permissions, hold the current workflow state, and make sensitive actions idempotent, meaning that retrying the same instruction cannot create a duplicate payment, order, or case. The agent can interpret unstructured information and propose the next step, but it should not invent its own permissions or decide which system record is current.

The same design needs an audit trail showing what happened, which data and rule informed the action, and who approved an override. Stop authority must belong to a named role with the practical ability to pause the process. Rollback should restore a known safe state or reverse an action where reversal is possible; an irreversible action needs approval before execution, not a comforting rollback label afterwards.

This architecture also exposes complexity that a polished interface can hide. Every model, tool connection, exception path, and monitoring duty creates an operational dependency. A coordination layer should not be removed by replacing it with a system that only a few specialists can understand and nobody clearly owns.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Place policy and accountability with named domain owners, enforce permissions and workflow state in ordinary software, and give agents bounded room to interpret and propose. Override, stop, audit, and rollback paths must remain visible and usable.</p>
</div>

The organizational mapping is narrow: an agent may deliver a clean recommendation while people still decide which evidence counts, who may override the system, and whether contrary signals remain visible. Governance becomes real when those choices are explicit, auditable, and held by someone who can stop the process.

## Redesign the career system with the org chart

A flatter structure can be economically coherent and still be badly led. Coordination-heavy roles contain more than status reporting and approval routing. Managers also coach, resolve conflict, transfer context, assess performance, and help people grow. If agents absorb the routine coordination, leaders must decide where those human duties go before declaring the layer unnecessary.

The old pyramid also served as a career system. More responsibility often meant managing more people, gaining a larger title, and moving upward through layers. A flatter company needs another answer for progression, pay, learning, and ambition. Senior domain ownership, expert paths, coaching responsibility, and authority over higher-risk decisions can provide meaningful growth, but only when the criteria and rewards are as clear as the management ladder they replace.

Some people in changed roles will make that transition, and some will not. Renaming a coordinator as an “orchestrator” does not create the necessary judgment or a viable job. The workforce plan should identify which work disappears, which skills transfer, what learning leads to a real destination, how selection will be made, and what support is available when redeployment is not credible. For a Nordic CIO, affected employees and workforce representatives should be involved early enough to shape the transition rather than receiving a finished design.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Removing coordination layers also removes parts of the career and coaching system. A defensible transition reallocates human duties, creates credible expert progression, and states plainly how learning, selection, redeployment, and support will work.</p>
</div>

## Take an evidence contract to the board

The board paper should begin with workflows, not a target number of layers. It should show which complete case types now close correctly with fewer human handoffs, where the capability boundary was tested, and which failure patterns remain. It should also disclose the full operating cost and the dependencies introduced by agents, tools, monitoring, and specialist support.

The second commitment is authority. Every material permission, exception, override, and stop decision needs an owner, while the system must preserve current state and an audit trail. The third is the people transition: which work changes, which human responsibilities remain, what credible paths exist, and what the organization will do for people who cannot move into the new roles.

With those commitments in place, a CIO can make a bounded decision. Flatten the parts of the organization where operating evidence shows that routine execution and coordination have genuinely been absorbed. Keep or redesign the layers where judgment, coaching, conflict resolution, or repair still requires them. The org chart should record an operating reality already demonstrated, rather than advertise one that leadership hopes AI will create.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Ask the board to approve an evidence-backed operating change: proven complete workflows, explicit authority and controls, controlled complexity, and a funded workforce transition. Change the hierarchy only where those commitments make the new shape defensible.</p>
</div>
