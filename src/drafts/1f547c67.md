Two claims define the architecture of an AI-first company. Every role acquires a technical dimension. And when agents absorb meaningful execution and coordination, ownership can move closer to the work, creating the possibility of a flatter organization with more bottom-up initiative.

Here, an agent means AI software that interprets a goal and can act through approved tools. That capability changes more than task speed because a useful agent may gather information, apply policy, prepare a decision, update systems, and route an exception. The organizational question is whether enough of that work has genuinely moved for roles, decision rights, and career structures to move with it.

The answer cannot come from an impressive demonstration or a cost target. A responsible CIO needs evidence from complete workflows, visible human authority, controlled technical complexity, and a credible plan for people whose work changes. The org chart comes later.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>AI-first combines distributed technical judgment with the possibility of flatter ownership. That possibility becomes an organizational decision only after the work, controls, and human transition have been proved together.</p>
</div>

## The technical dimension belongs in every role

In a [2026 interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s), Arvind KC, chief people officer at the AI company OpenAI, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately qualified the point: the technical role is applied to a particular domain. That distinction matters because technical judgment is broader than coding.

Technical judgment means understanding what an AI system can do reliably, what evidence it needs, how failure becomes visible, and where a person must decide. A salesperson needs it when an agent proposes contract terms. A finance leader needs it when a model classifies an unusual expense. Marketing, operations, and support need the same capability in their own domains. Their expertise remains the starting point; they now also need to shape and supervise the system acting on it.

KC’s statement supports the capability claim. It does not prove that management layers should disappear. I treat bottom-up ownership as an organizational hypothesis: if domain experts can improve and govern the workflows closest to them, fewer routine decisions may need to travel upward. The test is whether authority and accountability can move with the work rather than merely creating more local activity.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to set an agent’s boundaries and recognize failure, but nobody needs to become a software engineer. Distributed capability makes bottom-up ownership possible; operating evidence must show whether it makes flatter structure sensible.</p>
</div>

## Remove coordination before removing layers

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls the act of bolting AI onto an old structure the “Retrofit Trap.” The term captures a common design failure: one activity becomes faster while intake, routing, approvals, correction, and record-keeping continue as before.

Consider an illustrative contract-renewal workflow. An agent that drafts the customer email may save time, yet the account manager still gathers the existing terms, finance checks the price, legal reviews deviations, operations updates the service record, and somebody repairs mismatched data. The draft is a better task. The workflow is still organized around the old handovers.

A traditional hierarchy does several kinds of coordination work. It compresses information for senior attention, routes approvals, resolves exceptions, allocates resources, and settles conflicts between domains. An agent-supported workflow may absorb the routine parts by collecting the case, checking standard policy, proposing an allowed next step, updating records, and sending only genuine exceptions to the right owner.

Some authority remains human. People set policy, accept business risk, resolve novel trade-offs, change employment conditions, and answer for consequential outcomes. A layer can therefore be reconsidered only when its routine coordination has disappeared or moved into a governed workflow. If the same work is pushed onto already busy specialists, the company has hidden the hierarchy rather than flattened it.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>The Retrofit Trap improves an isolated task while preserving the surrounding organization. Flatter structure is justified only when a complete workflow removes recurring routing and repair without concealing the human authority that still matters.</p>
</div>

## Prove the whole workflow

Task speed is too narrow a measure for an organizational decision. The useful unit is a correctly closed case: the outcome is right, required permissions were respected, records agree, exceptions followed the approved path, and no uncounted repair remains. The trial must declare which cases qualify and which are excluded, or an apparently strong result can simply omit the difficult work.

For every eligible case, measure outcome quality, elapsed time, human routing touches, repair effort, exception volume, control failures, and total cost. Total cost includes model use, integration, human review, incident handling, and the manual fallback kept available. Record residual judgment as well: which decisions still required a person, why, and at what level of the organization.

These measures form a local evidence contract, not an industry benchmark. The material available for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer. Each company must set its own limits from risk appetite, service obligations, workforce context, and the consequences of a wrong decision.

A filled illustrative record for the renewal workflow could set the scope to standard renewals for existing customers in one market, excluding custom pricing, new data clauses, and regulated accounts. It could run for two renewal cycles, require a case-level audit trail, and stop if the agent attempts an excluded change or the team cannot restore the manual route during a drill. Its recorded recommendation might be “limited production; retain current coordination capacity until post-production evidence is reviewed.” Those are design choices for the example, not claimed performance results or universal thresholds.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Grade complete and correctly closed cases, including hidden human work and full cost. Set local decision limits, publish exclusions, and allow production approval without pretending that the same evidence already supports a change to the hierarchy.</p>
</div>

## Put authority where people can see it

Bottom-up ownership needs a visible constitution. Start with decision rights, meaning who may decide what, within which boundary, and who handles an exception. Then map each business domain to an accountable owner before choosing where an agent may act.

For the illustrative renewal flow, the commercial director could be accountable for customer outcomes and routine exceptions. General counsel could own the permitted contract clauses, the CIO could own tool permissions, model and supplier changes, audit retention, and service recovery, while the COO holds stop authority and the power to restore manual routing. The people leader owns the role-transition plan. The exact titles will vary, but each responsibility needs one named holder.

The control boundary should also be visible in software. The agent can interpret an email, retrieve relevant facts, and propose a next step. Rules-based workflow software should enforce permissions and the authoritative state, which is the record the company accepts as the current truth. In the renewal example, the agent may move a case from `received` to `proposed`; it may not move an excluded case from `proposed` to `executed`. A rejected transition is recorded and routed rather than reasoned away.

Retries need the same discipline. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-9.2.2), a standard defining the behavior of web requests, describes idempotency as repeated identical requests having the same intended effect as one request. In practice, a request key and its outcome must be stored and checked as part of the same protected operation as the external action. A key by itself does not prevent a duplicate renewal, payment, or message.

Before production, the team should exercise the stop and recovery path and retain the trace. It should prove who can suspend the agent, restore the manual route, replace a supplier, respond to a model change, and recover the last approved state. Rollback means restoring configuration and routing where possible; it cannot erase a commitment already sent to a customer, so the plan must also name the owner of remediation.

## The pyramid carried people, too

Coordination-heavy roles often carry more than routing. Managers coach, resolve conflict, preserve context, judge potential, and help people progress. Removing a layer without replacing those functions will make the chart flatter while leaving the company weaker.

The workforce review should therefore separate work into four practical categories: activity the workflow removes, judgment that stays with a person, new stewardship created by the system, and work that returns when exceptions or failures rise. That map shows which roles genuinely change and prevents routine administration from being confused with the whole value of a manager.

Career architecture must change at the same time. A flatter company needs credible expert paths, paid learning, recognized responsibility for domain and workflow stewardship, and promotion and pay criteria that do not depend on accumulating direct reports. People whose roles shrink need a funded route into new work, redeployment, or a transparent exit. Ambition does not disappear when a reporting layer does.

For a Nordic CIO, employee participation should begin while the operating rules and evidence plan can still change. Business, risk, finance, technology, people leadership, affected employees, and their representatives need to understand the proposed scope before production, not after a structural decision has effectively been made. That is how the human transition becomes part of the architecture rather than a communication exercise attached to it.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>A hierarchy also provides coaching, context, progression, and conflict resolution. Any flatter model must fund those functions through expert careers, learning, fair transition paths, and early participation by the people who will carry the change.</p>
</div>

## Make the organizational decision last

A board-defensible change should move through a deliberate sequence. Before the trial, the business owner, risk, finance, and technology leaders fix the scope, evidence, cost envelope, permissions, supplier-continuity plan, and stop rules. Before production, the people leader and affected employee representatives review role impacts, learning, redeployment, and the manual fallback.

The operating group can then decide whether the workflow deserves limited production. Only after sustained production evidence should the executive team or board consider changing roles or removing coordination capacity. That structural proposal should state what work has disappeared, what judgment remains, who now owns it, and what commitments the company is making to affected people.

Every structural approval also needs a post-change review date. A named operating executive, such as the COO, should examine routing, repair, exception, service, and coaching load at that date and hold the authority and budget to restore coordination capacity if the work returns. Without that recovery power, the organization can recognize a bad change yet remain unable to correct it.

This keeps the central leadership decision honest. An AI-first workflow may justify flatter ownership, but the chart should record a proven operating model rather than anticipate one. Until the evidence, authority, and people system support the change together, the responsible decision is to keep proving the work.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Approve the workflow and the organizational redesign through separate gates. Fix the coalition and controls before the trial, involve affected people before production, and give a named executive a stated review date plus the authority and budget to restore capacity after any structural change.</p>
</div>
