Two linked claims define the architecture of an AI-first company. Every role acquires a technical dimension, and ownership can move closer to the people who understand the work. If AI agents, software systems that use a generative model to interpret a goal and call approved tools, also absorb enough execution and coordination, the company can become materially flatter.

That possibility comes with a demanding condition. Technical judgment means knowing what AI can do reliably, what evidence it needs, where it fails, and when a person must decide. It asks a salesperson, controller, or operations lead to reason about an AI system rather than become a programmer. A single fast task also says little about whether a management layer is still needed.

The CIO's decision is therefore architectural: change the organization only after whole-workflow evidence shows that the coordination work has genuinely disappeared, and only when authority, system complexity, and the workforce transition have named owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An AI-first company can distribute technical judgment and flatten ownership, but the org chart should move only after agents remove real coordination work under visible controls and a credible people plan.</p></div>

## Technical judgment belongs in the business

In a [2026 video interview on the DECODE YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s), [Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), chief people officer at OpenAI, the AI research and deployment company that develops ChatGPT, said that every role is becoming “fundamentally a technical role” and that the technical role is applied to a domain. His wording matters because the useful capability is not abstract fluency with AI. It is judgment inside sales, finance, operations, marketing, support, and the other places where the business actually makes decisions.

A sales lead needs to know which account information an AI-generated recommendation can safely use. Finance has to distinguish a routine classification from an exception that changes reported numbers. Marketing must judge whether generated material is accurate and appropriate, while support and operations must know when an automated action can proceed and when the surrounding context demands escalation. These are business responsibilities with a new technical dimension.

KC's domain framing supports that first claim. The bottom-up implication is my argument, rather than a documented outcome attributed to him: if the people closest to a domain are best placed to find useful applications and judge their limits, a central AI team cannot own every improvement. The center should provide common platforms, security, and control boundaries; domain teams should own the work and its results.

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls the alternative the “Retrofit Trap.” This is what happens when AI accelerates a task but leaves the surrounding routing, approvals, data entry, and repair work intact. A faster draft inside an unchanged process may be useful, but it has not produced an AI-first operating model.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical judgment should sit inside each business domain, with shared boundaries from the center. Faster isolated tasks are still a retrofit when the surrounding coordination remains manual.</p></div>

## A flatter structure has to be earned

Hierarchy does more than supervise people. In his 1974 paper [“Organization Design: An Information Processing View”](https://doi.org/10.1287/inte.4.3.28), organization-design scholar Jay R. Galbraith explained that rules can coordinate predictable work, while hierarchy handles exceptions when those rules are insufficient. Managers gather information, resolve conflicts, approve deviations, and carry unusual cases across functional boundaries.

An agent-supported workflow may absorb part of that load. It can collect records, check known conditions, propose a next action, update status, and route only the exceptions that need human judgment. Yet accountability for risk, employment decisions, policy limits, and contested outcomes remains human. The useful measure is how much coordination the complete workflow still requires; a fluent exchange with an agent is weak operating evidence.

Consider a disputed supplier invoice. An agent could compare the purchase order, delivery record, invoice, and contract terms, then propose an action. Ordinary software should still verify the actor's permission, the amount limit, and the current official case status before anything is posted or paid. If another action has already moved the case, a stale or over-limit approval is rejected, leaves the official record unchanged, and is recorded for review.

I would evaluate this through a **correctly closed case**: a case that reaches the intended business outcome, follows the required controls, and leaves no hidden cleanup behind. The evidence should include human routing touches, repair effort, outcome quality, control failures, total cost, and the judgment that still could not be delegated. Representative routine cases and difficult exceptions both belong in the sample, because an average can conceal the work that keeps returning to managers.

No universal removal threshold follows from that test. The evidence available for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that supplies a safe numerical benchmark for deleting a management layer. Each executive team must set its own evidence period and acceptance conditions according to the workflow's risk, then disclose those conditions as a company decision rather than presenting them as an industry standard.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Flattening is justified only when complete cases close with less routing and repair, acceptable quality and control, lower total effort, and a clearly bounded residue of human judgment.</p></div>

## Replace routing with visible authority

A flatter company still needs an operating structure. For every domain, leaders should map who owns the outcome and who may propose, approve, execute, override, stop, restart, and reverse an action. This decision-rights map replaces informal escalation with explicit authority; it does not hand authority to a model.

The control boundary should be equally plain. A state machine, ordinary software that permits only defined moves between case statuses, enforces valid transitions, while a system of record holds the **authoritative state**, meaning the official record of where the case stands. The agent interprets messy input and proposes a permitted move. Identity systems, permission rules, amount limits, and the state machine decide whether that move may occur.

An audit trail records the actor, evidence, rule, tool result, and any override so a decision can be reconstructed later. **Stop authority** is the named right to pause live actions immediately when a boundary is breached. Both controls need human owners outside the agent's own reasoning.

For the supplier invoice, a valid path might be proposed → approved → posted → reconciled. Reconciliation means comparing the workflow record with the financial ledger to establish what actually happened. Each attempted payment also needs an identity that lets the system recognize a retry instead of paying twice, a standard control called **idempotency**. If the outcome of a payment request is uncertain, reconciliation comes before another attempt.

The following governance rhythm should be designed before the trial starts. It is a proposed decision model rather than an external benchmark:

| Role | Accountable work |
| --- | --- |
| Workflow owner | Convenes the cross-functional trial, defines the representative case mix, and prepares the decision record. |
| Evidence owner | Validates outcomes, hidden human effort, control failures, and full cost independently of the team rewarded for delivery. |
| Domain, technology, risk, and people signatories | Sign the claims within their authority: business outcome, system controls, risk acceptance, and workforce commitments. |
| Executive review forum | Sets a review cadence suited to the workflow before launch, then decides whether to continue, narrow, stop, or consider structural change. |
| Stop and reversal owners | Pause live actions when limits are breached and restore the previous working path through a tested rollback, meaning a controlled return to the last safe process. |

This architecture can support fewer coordination layers without creating concealed complexity. It also makes a board discussion concrete: the company can show who decides, what the software enforces, how exceptions surface, and who can stop the system when the evidence turns.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A flatter model needs explicit decision rights, software-enforced permissions and state, auditable actions, named signatories, and separate owners for stopping and reversing the workflow.</p></div>

## Change the career system before the chart

Coordination-heavy roles may shrink when routine routing disappears, but management also carries coaching, conflict resolution, progression, and institutional memory. Removing a layer without reallocating those responsibilities leaves a thinner chart and a weaker organization. The human transition is part of the architecture, not cleanup after the technology decision.

Leaders therefore need to redesign career paths alongside workflows. Domain expertise, workflow ownership, control stewardship, and coaching can become routes to progression. Pay and recognition should follow the accountability people carry, rather than assuming that ambition must always mean supervising a larger team. Learning time, role conversion, and redeployment need funding, and employees and their representatives should enter the design early enough to influence it.

Some roles will change substantially, and some may not survive in their current form. A responsible plan says this plainly, identifies the affected work, and defines what the company will offer before asking people to support the redesign. There is no credible flatter-company thesis without a position on careers, pay, learning, and those who bear the transition cost.

Before changing the org chart, I would ask the executive team to approve four linked commitments:

1. **Workflow evidence:** a baseline, representative case mix, correctly closed outcomes, human repair, quality, incidents, and full cost.
2. **Visible authority:** named owners for permissions, exceptions, overrides, stop, restart, audit, and rollback.
3. **Controlled complexity:** known dependencies, tested failure recovery, and an explicit account of the judgment that remains human.
4. **Workforce transition:** affected roles, employee participation, coaching capacity, new progression paths, learning investment, pay consequences, and redeployment commitments.

Each commitment needs an accountable preparer, evidence owner, signatory, and review forum. Only when the operating model has earned confidence through those records should the org chart reflect it. Until then, the right decision is to keep improving the workflow without pretending that a faster task has already created a flatter company.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Change the chart last. First prove the workflow, expose authority, control the new complexity, and commit to how careers and affected roles will be handled.</p></div>
