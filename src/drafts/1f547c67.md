An AI agent can automate a queue long before it can justify changing the org chart. The distance between those events is where many claims about flat, AI-first companies outrun their evidence. I call the decision rule that closes the gap the *relay-layer test*.

A relay layer collects updates, translates between teams, routes routine approvals, and makes work visible to people above it. The term describes a function rather than a title. A manager who coaches people, allocates resources, resolves conflict, sets policy, or carries accountability is doing work that an automated handoff does not replace.

Workflow automation and organizational redesign are therefore different claims. A company can automate most cases in a queue and still need human authority for the few cases carrying most of the risk. None of the evidence used here demonstrates that a company has safely removed a management layer, and I think that boundary belongs in the thesis rather than a footnote.

Benjamin Simkin gives the old approach a useful name in *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, his independently published field manual from 10 May 2026. He calls it the “Retrofit Trap”: adding AI to work designed for manual human execution while leaving the old handoffs, approvals, and reporting structure intact. On 24 February 2026, [Arvind KC became Chief People Officer at OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), the AI company behind the ChatGPT generative AI assistant. KC's role is relevant because the argument concerns jobs and reporting lines, but the appointment supplies context rather than evidence that a layer can disappear.

The question I keep getting from founders and executives is what an AI-first organization looks like in practice. My answer begins with *agentic production*. An [AI agent](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/) is software that uses an AI model, instructions, data, and tools to carry out a multi-step task. Agentic production is an operating model in which agents execute routine work and coordinate handoffs while people set goals, own policy, and decide consequential exceptions. The chart should follow the work only after that model performs within declared limits and can return safely to people when it fails.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The relay-layer test separates a busy AI workflow from a defensible organizational change. Routine coordination can shrink only after the company proves quality, full cost, review burden, authority, reversibility, and a credible path for the people who carried the work.</p></div>

## The relay-layer test

The argument rests on two claims. People throughout the business need enough technical understanding to judge how AI changes decisions in their domain. Some hierarchies can then become flatter where agents reliably take over execution and routine coordination. The second claim depends on the first and remains conditional.

A support supervisor who mainly assigns cases, requests status, gathers context, and checks standard answers may see much of that relay work shrink. The same person may still be needed to coach the team, handle sensitive cases, settle conflict between functions, or remain accountable for the service. The relay-layer test asks which work disappeared and where the remaining authority moved.

That wording creates a clear failure condition. If removing a layer increases unresolved exceptions, slows important decisions, raises the full cost of a completed task, or pushes hidden review onto domain experts, the agent has not replaced coordination. The company has merely made coordination harder to see. I think flattening belongs after demonstrated reliability, never inside the business case used to justify a pilot.

Simkin's Retrofit Trap helps locate the test. When a process still depends on the same human routing and approvals after an AI tool arrives, the company has improved a step without changing its architecture. When routine relay work genuinely disappears, the chart may be able to follow, provided the old layer's authority, operating capacity, and career functions have been replaced deliberately.

The empirical challenge is still open. The sources in this article show workflow redesign and large-scale automation, but none reports a comparable company removing a management layer and then publishing decision latency, exception load, complete cost, accountability failures, and career outcomes. A serious claim of safe flattening should produce those measures during a supervised trial and through a defined period after the reporting line changes. Without that second observation window, the company has tested the tool rather than the organization.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A relay layer is removable only when its coordination function has disappeared without weakening decisions or shifting invisible work elsewhere. The claim remains falsifiable after the chart changes, when exception load, cost, accountability, and career outcomes reveal whether the new structure can hold.</p></div>

## Every role becomes technical

A technical role does not require everyone to code or engineers to occupy every seat. It means decisions in sales, operations, finance, marketing, and support now depend on what an AI system can do, where it fails, and how its output should be checked. The person responsible for the decision needs to understand those limits even when someone else builds the system.

I've watched this play out in organizations I advise on agentic production. A marketing team stalls when it cannot judge whether an AI-generated campaign brief reflects the strategy. An operations team cannot safely skip human review if it has no way to measure the accuracy of an agent's ticket classification. A finance team cannot choose well between a system that reduces repeated reconciliation and a tool that has not changed cycle time, error rates, or cost.

This is a literacy rather than a coding skill. I think AI systems act as amplifiers because clear instructions, sound source material, and a useful quality standard can spread through a workflow. Confused ownership and weak judgment spread just as easily, often behind a dashboard that looks like progress.

Established engineering sits underneath the newer agent language. Workflow engines and [state machines](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/step-functions/latest/dg/concepts-statemachines.html), software that move work through predefined stages according to rules, already route cases whose paths can be specified in advance. An agent changes a narrower part of the system: it can interpret material such as a customer email and propose one of the permitted next steps when every wording variation cannot be written as a rule. Permissions, records, and escalation paths remain ordinary workflow engineering, and they matter even more when an AI model helps choose the path.

The old organization sends technical questions to a separate function. An AI-first organization still needs specialists, but technical reasoning becomes a shared language for decisions. That shared language lets authority move closer to the work without turning local freedom into guesswork.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A role becomes technical when its owner can judge how AI affects the decision in front of them. Agents add flexible interpretation to established workflow engineering; rules, permissions, records, and specialist knowledge still carry the system.</p></div>

## Prove the workflow before changing the chart

Consider a customer-support workflow. An agent can receive a ticket, classify the issue, retrieve the customer's account history and contract terms, draft a response, and check it against policy. A routine case moves forward only when the source systems agree, the proposed action stays within an approved limit, and the answer passes the company's quality checks. An exception pauses the workflow and goes to a named support owner, who sees the evidence, makes the decision, and remains accountable.

The operating contract should be explicit. The normal path is received, evidence gathered, proposal prepared, authorized, executed, and closed. An exception moves from proposal prepared to escalated, then waits for a human decision. An *audit trail*, the record of what the system used and did, stores the inputs, sources, model and instruction versions, tools, policy version, proposed action, authorization result, final action, and any human decision. That record needs enough detail to replay a disputed case and understand why it took its path.

The model proposes; a *deterministic authorization service*, a rule-based component that does not rely on the model's judgment, enforces permissions. A named person approves exceptions. Direct movement from escalated to automated execution is forbidden and should fail in testing. Each approved action also receives a unique transaction key, so a retry cannot issue the same refund or account change twice.

“Reliable enough” needs an *acceptance gate*, a set of conditions declared before the trial starts. Count every attempted case: rejected proposals, retries, escalations, failed actions, and human rework cannot disappear from the result. The illustrative commitments below describe one low- to medium-risk support queue, not a universal benchmark. The percentages show what a real ceiling looks like, while the budget blank forces leadership to supply the number before approval.

A representative failure shows why these conditions cannot collapse into one success rate. Imagine that a weekly blind review scores 91 percent against a 94 percent human baseline. The audit trail shows that the model proposed a refund under an expired policy, while the authorization service rejected the action and sent the case to a person. The guardrail prevented customer harm, but the quality gate still failed. If the next weekly sample also misses, the rollback rule fires. Safe containment is evidence that control works; it is not evidence that the workflow is ready to replace a layer.

A public Swedish case illustrates both the opportunity and the limit. In February 2024, Klarna, the Swedish payments company, [reported that its AI assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month](https://www.klarna.com/international/press/klarna-ai-assistant-handles-two-thirds-of-customer-service-chats-in-its-first-month/), equal to two-thirds of its support chats. Klarna said customer satisfaction matched its human agents, repeat inquiries fell by 25 percent, and resolution time dropped from 11 minutes to under two. These are company-reported figures rather than an independent assessment, but they show the kind of baseline comparison an operating decision needs.

The case did not become a simple argument for removing the human path. In June 2025, [Klarna chief executive Sebastian Siemiatkowski said Klarna could use AI to take away manual work while promising customers a human connection](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/04/klarna-ceo-says-company-will-use-humans-to-offer-vip-customer-service/). He described human service as a premium experience. Klarna's figures support redesigning the routine path; they do not establish that a management layer was safely removed, complete economics improved, or a replacement career system worked.

Passing the trial authorizes a reversible structural experiment. Before any reporting line changes, the executive sponsor names an operating owner and a restoration owner, then reserves the people and authority required to run the old path for 90 days. Temporary dual-running does not mean duplicating every case. It means trained people remain available, access stays active, and the company can restore supervisory capacity without improvising during a failure.

The post-change reviews make the organizational claim testable. At day 30, the operating owner examines hidden expert review, unresolved exceptions, quality, cost per accepted resolution, decision time, and customer complaints. At day 60, the people lead and employee representative examine workload, redeployment, training time, pay and authority in new roles, while security and supplier-risk owners confirm data location, model changes, and the exit path. At day 90, the full decision forum chooses to retain, adjust, or reverse the new structure and records the evidence behind that choice.

Restoration cannot depend on executive mood. A critical privacy, security, or policy failure returns the affected work to people immediately. Two consecutive weekly misses on the declared quality, service, review, cost, or unresolved-exception ceiling restore the named supervisory capacity. The company may try again after correcting the cause and rerunning the gate, but the reporting line is never allowed to make the old process unrecoverable.

Automation volume can earn a closer look at the chart; it cannot settle the decision. I think the relay-layer test becomes organizational proof only when the workflow passes before the change and the new structure holds through the observation window. Until a company can show both, flattening remains a proposal rather than a result.

## The advantage compounds through correction

Once a domain expert can supervise an agent workflow, the role gains a second capability. An *agent manager* is a person who can specify work for agents, check their results, and improve the workflow over time. The title matters less than the combination of technical literacy and real domain expertise.

An editor can use agents to prepare drafts, collect sources for claims, and flag repeated language before the final edit. A finance analyst can have agents pull and reconcile records, then surface anomalies for investigation. A customer-success lead can use them to triage cases and prepare account summaries, preserving attention for relationship risk and difficult conversations. In each example, the agent handles repeatable preparation while the specialist remains responsible for judgment.

I think the advantage compounds only when saved effort improves the system that produced it. Human corrections need to change the instructions, source material, policies, or tests used by the workflow. When learning stays in one person's head, the same errors recur at higher speed and the apparent gain remains fragile.

I don't know a defensible productivity multiplier that applies across companies. The result depends on task volume, baseline quality, the cost of mistakes, and how much human review remains. The strategic advantage comes from the direction of learning: a team that turns corrections into a better shared system can improve faster than one that keeps adding isolated tools to the old process.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agent management can multiply a domain expert's capacity, but the gain compounds only when corrections improve the shared workflow. The size of the gain belongs in operating evidence rather than a universal productivity claim.</p></div>

## Distributed ownership needs governance

Shared technical literacy cannot carry a distributed organization by itself. When more decisions happen close to the work, leaders see more local movement and fewer requests for permission. That can be productive, but motion does not prove that the design works.

The safeguard is explicit *decision rights*, meaning a written answer to who may decide what and when an issue must be escalated. Reversible choices that stay within one domain, budget, quality standard, and policy boundary can usually sit close to the work. Company strategy, major capital allocation, security and privacy rules, legal obligations, conflicts between domains, and high-impact people or customer decisions still need central authority.

The most useful frame from my own work is *domain-mapped ownership*. Each domain has one person who defines good output, sets operating limits, reviews exceptions, and can change the workflow. The frame connects an agent's permissions and model-proposed choices to a human owner with the authority to make the final call. It also avoids the Everybody Reviews Everything Trap, where feedback conflicts and nobody owns the result.

For a European support workflow, the boundary must reflect the [EU General Data Protection Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng), the European Union's 2016 regulation governing personal-data processing. It must also account for any applicable duties under the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the European Union's 2024 risk-based regulation of AI systems. Legal scope depends on the use case, so privacy, security, legal, and supplier-risk owners need to shape permissions before they are delegated. Data access, retention, human oversight, and escalation then become enforceable workflow terms rather than a policy promise.

Supplier governance belongs in the same operating boundary. Leaders need to know where data goes, what changes when a provider updates a model, how old decisions can be replayed, and whether the company can move the workflow elsewhere without losing records or control. The exit test in the charter turns those questions into demonstrated capabilities before the organization depends on the supplier.

Worker governance follows the same logic. Where law, collective agreements, or company commitments require employee consultation, that work belongs before a reporting line moves. I think distributed experimentation is defensible only when customers, employees, and accountable executives can still see who made a decision, which rule applied, and how to challenge the result.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Distributed ownership works when local authority has a named owner, measurable limits, a decision record, and an escalation path. Privacy, security, legal, supplier, and worker-governance boundaries must be built into the workflow before hierarchy is removed.</p></div>

## The career ladder is part of the architecture

The harder question is what happens to the people whose coordination tasks shrink. People are not absorbed by agents; parts of their work are. Some managers can become domain owners or agent managers. Others may retrain, move elsewhere in the company, or leave. Organizations I have advised during AI-first reorganizations have handled this differently, but the human transition has been harder than the technical one.

A flatter organization also breaks a familiar promise about ambition. If there are fewer management layers, progression can no longer depend mainly on acquiring more direct reports. Companies need credible paths through deeper craft, larger domain ownership, technical leadership, or responsibility across domains. Otherwise they remove a layer of coordination and accidentally remove the main language of status and reward.

Consider a support supervisor whose relay work has largely disappeared. A credible next role could be service-domain owner, responsible for quality thresholds, policy changes, exception patterns, and improvements to the agent workflow. The company would fund training and scheduled learning time before the role changes, place the new position in a pay band with authority comparable to the former management role, and judge progression by the performance and safety of the domain rather than the number of direct reports. This is a structural example rather than a promise that every supervisor will make the transition, but it shows what an alternative career path has to contain.

That makes the *employee compact*, the set of promises about transition and progression, part of the operating model. Before a workflow changes the chart, leadership should name who owns consultation, who pays for communication and scheduled reskilling, how redeployment decisions will be made, what temporary dual-running requires, and which non-managerial paths carry greater pay and authority. Leaving these choices implicit turns efficiency into a promise employees have no reason to trust.

My take is to begin with one workflow and one bounded decision. Complete the charter, fund the people transition, prove the routine and exception paths, and change one reporting line only after the evidence passes. Keep the old capacity recoverable through the 90-day structural test, then use the result to decide whether the next part of the company is ready.

The architecture of an AI-first company asks more of leaders than a flat chart. They have to prove which coordination work has become unnecessary, preserve authority where judgment still matters, and build a credible future for the people who carried the middle. The company earns a flatter structure only when the work, authority, and path forward still hold after the layer is gone.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first redesign joins operating proof to a human transition: relay work must disappear, authority must remain visible, and progression must carry real pay and decision rights. A smaller chart counts only when the company can restore what it removed and the people affected can see a credible path forward.</p></div>
