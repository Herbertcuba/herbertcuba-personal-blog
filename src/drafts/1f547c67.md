Two claims sit at the center of an AI-first company. Every business role gains a technical dimension, and ownership can move closer to the work as AI agents absorb more execution and coordination. If both claims hold in daily operations, parts of the company may become materially flatter.

That possibility is easy to overstate. An AI-first operating model redesigns a complete business workflow around AI capabilities. In this article, an agent means software that uses a generative model to interpret a goal and act through approved tools. Adding one to an existing task can improve that task while leaving the surrounding approvals, handovers, and repairs untouched.

For a CIO, this creates two decisions rather than one. The first is whether an agent-supported workflow deserves to run in production. The second is whether the evidence is strong enough to redesign roles, reporting lines, and careers around it. Combining those decisions turns an operating hypothesis into an organizational gamble.

  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>AI-first design begins with distributed technical judgment and the possibility of flatter ownership, but workflow approval and organizational redesign require separate evidence.</p>
</div>

## Separate the two decisions

A production decision asks whether a workflow delivers the intended outcome at acceptable cost and risk. An organization decision asks something harder: has enough coordination work actually disappeared to justify changing the hierarchy? Faster output from one activity cannot answer the second question.

Consider a supplier bank-account change. An agent could read the request, gather documents, compare records, and prepare a recommendation. Yet the complete workflow also includes independent verification, approval, the update to the supplier record, notification, and monitoring before money is sent. If employees still chase missing evidence, route every exception, or repair records afterwards, the company has automated an activity without removing the work that supports it.

The useful unit of evidence is a correctly closed case: a request inside the pre-agreed scope reaches the right business outcome, follows required controls, leaves a usable record, and creates no hidden repair downstream. The trial should include routine cases and declared exclusions such as conflicting records or suspected fraud. It should record human routing touches, repair effort, quality failures, control breaches, elapsed time, total operating cost, and the judgment that still belongs to people. The team should also exercise its stop procedure and manual recovery path, preserving the record of what happened.

Those measures are a local evidence contract, not an industry benchmark. No Swedish or Norwegian production case in the material available for this article establishes a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer. Each leadership team must set its decision criteria in advance, according to the workflow's risk and economics, and refuse to move the criteria after seeing the result.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Judge complete, correctly closed cases rather than isolated task speed, then keep production authorization separate from any decision to remove coordination capacity.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment moves into the domain

In a May 2026 interview published by the business channel DECODE, [Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), chief people officer at the artificial-intelligence company OpenAI, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately qualified the statement by tying the technical role to a particular domain, meaning an area of business expertise and accountability ([interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=344s)).

That does not mean everyone must code. Technical judgment is the ability to reason about what an AI system can do reliably, where its evidence is weak, which actions are safe to automate, and when a person must decide. A salesperson needs that judgment before making an AI-supported commitment to a customer. Finance needs it when routine document matching turns into a disputed amount or possible fraud. Operations, marketing, and support face their own boundaries between repeatable work and consequential judgment.

The interview supports the claim about technical roles. I treat bottom-up innovation as the organizational hypothesis that follows from it, not as a finding proved by that recording. When domain teams can shape and evaluate their own workflows, useful changes can originate closer to the work. A central AI function still has an important role in shared platforms, security, supplier management, and common controls, but it should not become the only place where the company understands AI.

Distributed technical judgment therefore comes with local accountability. The people nearest the workflow should help define success and failure, while named owners retain authority over risk, spend, and exceptions. Without that pairing, bottom-up ownership becomes hidden experimentation rather than an operating model.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to shape and challenge AI-supported work; local initiative becomes useful only when domain expertise is paired with visible accountability.</p>
</div>

## Coordination is the unit of redesign

A conventional hierarchy does more than allocate status. It carries information upward, translates goals downward, routes approvals, resolves exceptions, allocates resources, and provides coaching. Some of that work is repeatable coordination. Some requires human authority because it involves conflict, fairness, ambiguous trade-offs, or accountability to employees and customers.

Agents may absorb parts of the repeatable work by gathering context, checking routine conditions, following up on missing inputs, preparing a standard decision, and sending unusual cases to the right owner. This matters because a flatter structure becomes plausible only when those duties leave the management layer across the whole workflow. Moving unresolved cases to already busy specialists changes the queue, not the organization.

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls the act of bolting AI onto an old structure the “Retrofit Trap.” The term fits an organization that accelerates document creation while preserving manual intake, routing, approval, and correction around it. The tool looks new, but the coordination burden remains.

The practical test is to map each recurring coordination duty before discussing headcount or reporting lines. If an agent-supported workflow removes status collection but increases exception review, that trade belongs in the decision. If it removes routing while managers still carry coaching and resource conflicts, those responsibilities need a deliberate home. Flattening is defensible only when the remaining work has owners, capacity, and authority.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>A flatter structure becomes credible when complete workflows shed repeatable coordination and the judgment, coaching, and conflict work that remains has been explicitly reassigned.</p>
</div>

## Put authority into the operating design

Decision rights are the named permissions to propose, approve, execute, override, or stop an action. They should be visible before an agent receives access to business systems. In the supplier example, the agent may interpret the request and propose a next step. Ordinary software should enforce who may verify it, which approvals are required, and whether the case is in a valid state before the supplier record changes.

Authoritative state means the system record that determines what has happened and what may happen next. A simple contract might allow a case to move from submitted to verified, then approved, then applied. It should reject a jump from submitted directly to applied. Idempotency means that retrying a request produces no additional effect; this requires the workflow to store the request identifier and outcome and enforce the duplicate check around the action. A unique key by itself is only a label.

The same design needs an audit trail, a human override path, stop authority, and rollback where reversal is technically possible. Rollback cannot recover money already sent to the wrong account, which is why prevention and stop controls matter before an irreversible action. Durable workflow software, meaning software that preserves a process through interruptions, is already designed to manage [state, checkpoints, retries, and recovery](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-functions/durable/durable-functions-overview). Free-form model reasoning should operate inside those controls rather than replace them.

Ownership should follow the same clarity. The business owner signs for outcomes and routine exceptions. The risk owner sets prohibited actions and approval rules. The technology owner maintains data access, logs, model and supplier change controls, and the manual recovery route. The people owner carries role changes, learning, and consultation. An executive sponsor decides whether production evidence supports deployment and, later, whether organizational evidence supports redesign.

The sequence matters. Business and risk leaders first agree the scope, cases, and decision rights. Technology and finance then define the approved systems, data, actions, operating cost, and continuity plan, including what happens if a model or supplier changes. People leaders and employee representatives examine role consequences before commitments become irreversible. Only then should the executive group open the production gate; structural change belongs at a later gate with its own record.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Agents may interpret and propose, while named people own outcomes and ordinary software enforces permissions, valid state changes, duplicate prevention, audit, override, stop, and recovery.</p>
</div>

## Rebuild the people model with the structure

Removing routine coordination changes more than a job description. Management roles often combine routing work with coaching, pay decisions, conflict resolution, professional development, and a visible route to greater responsibility. If the routing work shrinks, the company still needs to decide where every other function goes.

A flatter company therefore needs credible expert careers with status and pay that do not depend on acquiring direct reports. It needs protected time for learning, clear access to coaching, and ways for early-career employees to build judgment when they can no longer learn by processing every routine case. Managers whose roles change need honest options: preparation for a different role, movement into domain expertise or people leadership, redeployment where work exists, and a fair process when it does not.

For a Nordic CIO, employee participation should begin while the workflow and role consequences can still change, not after the decision has hardened. That is both a people commitment and an operating advantage: employees doing the work can identify concealed repairs, informal approvals, and exception handling that a process map misses. Their input improves the evidence while making the transition more credible.

The structural decision should also name an executive operating owner, fix a post-change review date, and grant that owner authority to restore coordination capacity or the manual route. If exception, repair, coaching, or control work returns after a layer changes, the company should respond to the evidence rather than defend the redesign for appearances.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Flatter organizations must replace the career, coaching, learning, and transition functions carried by the old pyramid, with employee participation and authority to restore capacity when work returns.</p>
</div>

## Put the final choice in front of the board

The board paper should carry two recommendation lines. The production line covers workflow scope, correctly closed cases, exception and repair load, quality, control failures, full cost, residual human judgment, supplier continuity, and the result of a stop-and-recovery exercise. The organization line covers which coordination duties disappeared, where the remaining duties moved, how decision rights changed, and how people will progress and learn.

It should also name the owner for every consequential boundary: business outcome, risk rule, system access, exception decision, override, stop, recovery, workforce transition, and post-change review. The review date and the authority to restore capacity belong in the original decision, not in a contingency discussion after trouble appears.

I would make the structural choice reversible and evidence-led. Let the workflow earn production on its own case. Let any change to the hierarchy earn a second approval after repeated operation shows that coordination has genuinely left the layer, controls remain effective, and the new people model can carry what the old structure provided. A flatter AI-first company may be possible, but responsible leadership has to build the conditions that make it defensible.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Ask the board to approve deployment and structural redesign separately, with complete-workflow evidence, named authority, a funded people plan, and a dated power to restore capacity.</p>
</div>
