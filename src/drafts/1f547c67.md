An AI-first company begins with two linked propositions. Every business role acquires a technical dimension, and the organization can become flatter as AI agents absorb part of the execution and coordination once carried by managers and specialist teams. An AI agent here means software that uses a generative AI model to interpret a goal, choose a next step, and call approved tools within a workflow.

The first proposition changes who can shape the work. The second changes the structure around it, which makes it much more consequential. Distributed technical judgment can start now, while structural flattening has to be earned through evidence that whole workflows close correctly, authority remains visible, and the people affected have a credible path forward.

That is the architecture question for a CIO. The opportunity is real, but the org chart should move only after the operating model can carry the weight.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI can move technical judgment toward domain experts and coordination into software, creating the possibility of a flatter company. The possibility becomes an organizational decision only after workflow evidence, governance, and workforce commitments make it defensible.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment moves into every role

During a recorded DECODE interview about people leadership at OpenAI, the AI research and product company, people executive Arvind KC said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” His follow-up gives the claim its useful meaning: the technical role is applied to a domain. It does not turn every employee into a software engineer.

I think the practical capability is **technical judgment**: knowing what an AI-supported workflow can do reliably, where it is likely to fail, what context it needs, and which decision a person must still own. A salesperson needs that judgment when a generated proposal touches pricing policy. A finance owner needs it when an invoice is unusual. Operations and support teams need it when an apparently routine case contains a safety, contractual, or customer consequence.

This capability also changes where improvement originates. A domain expert who understands both the work and the limits of the system can identify a useful automation, test its output, and own the result without waiting for a central technology team to discover the problem. Bottom-up ownership means giving that expert room to improve the workflow within clear boundaries, with technical specialists still responsible for shared platforms and controls.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Technical judgment is the ability to assess AI reliability, failure, context, and residual human responsibility inside a business domain. Spreading that capability lets domain experts shape workflows without pretending that everyone must code or that central controls no longer matter.</p>
</div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the old organization

In *The AI First Company*, author Benjamin Simkin calls a common failure the **Retrofit Trap**: adding AI to an existing task while leaving the surrounding operating model intact. The task looks faster, but its approvals, handoffs, queues, and repair work continue much as before.

Consider a customer complaint. An AI agent may draft a response in seconds, yet a person still copies it into the service system, finds the order, checks the policy, asks a manager to approve a refund, and sends an unusual case to another team. The drafting metric improves while the time, cost, and accountability of resolving the complaint barely move. That is task acceleration, not an AI-first workflow.

A hierarchy does more than allocate status. It carries information, grants approval, translates policy, and routes exceptions when the normal process breaks. An agent-supported workflow may absorb some of that coordination by gathering records, comparing the case with policy, requesting a decision from the right owner, and maintaining a usable record of what happened. Human authority remains necessary where the organization changes policy, accepts material risk, makes employment decisions, or resolves a trade-off the rules were never designed to settle.

The useful design question is therefore precise: which coordination obligations have actually left the workflow, and which accountable decisions remain?

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Retrofit Trap makes one task faster while manual routing and repair survive around it. An AI-first operating model changes the complete flow of work and removes only the coordination that software can perform without hiding accountable human decisions.</p>
</div>

## Flattening has to be earned by complete cases

Once the coordination mechanism is visible, the case for a flatter structure becomes testable. The unit of evidence should be a **complete workflow**, from the event that starts the work to a correctly closed case. “Correctly closed” means the business outcome is valid, required controls were followed, exceptions reached an accountable owner, and the organization can reconstruct the decision afterward.

A pilot should compare representative cases with the current process and show at least four kinds of evidence:

- **Flow:** elapsed time, waiting time, and the number of human touches used only to route or chase the work.
- **Outcome and control:** correct resolutions, rework, customer or operational harm, and failures of policy or access controls.
- **Economics:** model, software, oversight, and infrastructure cost together with the human effort spent reviewing and repairing outputs.
- **Residual judgment:** the decisions people still make, why those decisions require a person, and whether the right owner receives them in time.

These are decision categories, not an established industry scorecard. I would not present a Swedish or Norwegian numerical threshold because no production case in the available evidence supports a universal one. Each company needs to set its acceptance level from the workflow’s consequence, its baseline, and its own risk appetite.

A fast average is also insufficient if rare failures create material damage. Test ordinary work, ambiguous inputs, unavailable systems, repeated requests, and cases that must stop. Only then can a leader see whether a management layer has lost a real coordination function or whether the work has merely become less visible.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Judge the operating model by complete, correctly closed cases rather than isolated AI speed. Flow, outcome, control, total cost, and residual judgment reveal whether coordination disappeared, moved elsewhere, or became hidden repair work.</p>
</div>

## Replace routing with visible control

Removing a coordination layer creates an obligation to install a clearer architecture in its place. Start by mapping each **domain**, meaning a bounded area such as customer remediation, supplier payment, or pricing, to a named human owner. That owner is accountable for policy and outcomes even when an agent performs much of the work.

Decision rights then need to be explicit. The design should state who may change policy, approve an exception, override a recommendation, stop the workflow, restart it, and accept the consequences of a failure. An override is a recorded human decision to depart from the normal path. Stop authority is the independent power to halt further action; it cannot depend on the same agent recognizing its own failure.

Core controls belong in ordinary rules-based software. Identity, permissions, spending limits, and **case state**, the authoritative record of where a piece of work currently stands, should not depend on free-form model reasoning. Retries should be **idempotent**, which means that repeating the same request cannot create a second refund, payment, or order. The agent can interpret an email, assemble evidence, or propose a next action, but software should enforce whether that action is allowed.

The same separation creates **auditability**, a record that shows the inputs, actions, approvals, and outcomes of a case. It also supports rollback, where a reversible change is restored, or a compensating action when true reversal is impossible. From the center, more local ownership may look untidy because fewer decisions travel through one chain. With named owners, hard boundaries, and inspectable records, that apparent disorder can be an empowered edge rather than concealed authority.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A flatter model needs named domain owners, explicit decision rights, independent stop authority, and inspectable records. Rules-based software should enforce permissions and state while agents interpret and propose within those boundaries.</p>
</div>

## The old pyramid was also a people system

Hierarchy coordinates work, but it also carries coaching, apprenticeship, pay progression, recognition, and ambition. If AI agents absorb status collection, routine approval, scheduling, and exception routing, coordination-heavy roles will change. That does not tell us whether the people in those roles should leave; it tells us that leaders must redesign the useful work and the career system together.

Before widening a manager’s span, decide who will coach employees, evaluate performance, resolve conflict, and develop domain judgment. If there are fewer management titles, create credible expert paths with comparable status and pay. If entry-level tasks are automated, preserve supervised work through which less experienced employees can learn how good decisions are made rather than only how to monitor a machine.

Some roles may still lose enough durable work that reassignment is not credible. Leaders should name that possibility early and bring HR, affected employees, and employee representatives into the design. The people plan should show which tasks change, what learning time and support are funded, how selection decisions will be made, and what happens when no suitable role remains. Otherwise, the redesign risks preserving manual work in the shadows while presenting the transition as frictionless.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Removing coordination work also removes parts of the career and learning system. A responsible redesign provides new coaching capacity, expert progression, funded transition paths, and fair treatment where a durable role cannot be found.</p>
</div>

## Put the evidence before the org chart

A CIO should take an **evidence contract** into the executive or board discussion: an agreement on what must be proven and which commitments must be in place before structure changes. It should answer six practical questions:

1. Which complete workflow is changing, and which named domain owner is accountable for its outcome?
2. What baseline and representative cases show that routing, waiting, and repair work were removed rather than shifted?
3. Which decisions remain human, and who owns exceptions, overrides, stop authority, and restart approval?
4. Which permissions, limits, case states, retry protections, and audit records are enforced outside the AI model?
5. What happens when the agent, a connected system, or the policy fails, and how will the organization contain and recover from that failure?
6. Which roles and career paths change, what transition support is funded, and what commitments have been made to the people affected?

Weak answers are a reason to keep the coordination layer while improving the workflow. Strong answers across representative cases may justify flattening where the evidence applies, without turning one successful pilot into a company-wide claim.

An AI-first architecture distributes technical judgment and can move meaningful ownership toward the edges of the company. Its legitimacy comes from making work, authority, failure, and human consequences more visible than they were before. A flatter organization should emerge as the measured consequence of that better operating model. It should never be the opening promise.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Before changing the org chart, require complete-workflow proof, named authority, enforced controls, recovery paths, and a funded people plan. Flatten only where that combined evidence shows that the old coordination function has genuinely been replaced.</p>
</div>
