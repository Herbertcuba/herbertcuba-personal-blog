*AI changes the org chart only after it changes the work. This is a practical architecture for distributing technical judgment now, testing whether AI systems truly remove coordination, and protecting authority and people before a hierarchy is redesigned.*

An AI-first company begins with two linked claims. Every business role acquires a technical dimension, and the organization can become flatter as more execution and coordination move to AI agents. An AI agent here means model-based software that can interpret a case, choose a next step, and use approved tools within a defined scope.

In a video published in June 2026, [Arvind KC, chief people officer at OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), the company behind the ChatGPT assistant, said that [“every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ). He immediately connected that technical role to a business domain, and his wider point was that innovation should also move from the edges upward.

Those claims do not have the same burden of proof. Distributed technical judgment is a capability decision that leaders can make now. A flatter hierarchy is an organizational bet: it becomes defensible only when complete-workflow evidence shows that routine coordination has actually disappeared, and when authority, controls, and the human transition have somewhere credible to go.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Build technical judgment across the business now. Treat a flatter structure as a conditional result of proven workflow change, visible authority, and a credible people plan.</p></div>

## Technical judgment belongs inside the business

Technical judgment is the ability to reason about work performed with AI: what can be delegated, what evidence is needed, where the system is likely to fail, and when a person must decide. Coding can help, but it is not the definition.

A salesperson needs to know when an AI-produced recommendation may inform a conversation and when it could become a binding offer. Finance needs to distinguish a plausible classification from a reconciled transaction. Operations must recognize exceptions the workflow cannot safely resolve, while support teams need clear escalation conditions. The technical dimension is the judgment required to shape, test, and govern these boundaries inside the work itself.

That knowledge cannot live only in a central AI team. A domain, such as procurement or customer support, is a part of the business with its own outcomes, data, rules, and trade-offs. The people closest to it should own how AI is applied there, while central technology, security, legal, and risk functions provide shared infrastructure and constraints.

From the centre, this distribution of ownership may look less orderly than one programme controlling every use case. Its discipline comes from a domain map, common controls, and evidence that can be compared. That is the difference between empowered edges and unmanaged experimentation.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical judgment means knowing how to delegate, test, and bound AI-supported work inside a business domain. It spreads decision quality across the company without requiring everyone to become a programmer.</p></div>

## Redesign the workflow before the hierarchy

In his 2026 book *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, business author Benjamin Simkin calls the use of AI inside an unchanged operating structure the “Retrofit Trap.” The label matters because a faster task can leave the surrounding organization untouched.

Consider supplier onboarding. An agent may read registration documents and draft a risk summary in minutes. If procurement still emails finance for a check, waits for legal approval, chases missing information, repairs inconsistent records, and asks a manager where an exception belongs, the company has accelerated document review while preserving the coordination system around it.

That coordination system is one reason hierarchies exist. In his 1974 paper [“Organization Design: An Information Processing View”](https://doi.org/10.1287/inte.4.3.28), organizational-design scholar Jay R. Galbraith described recurring situations being handled through rules while unusual situations moved upward to a level with a broader view. A management layer may therefore carry information, approvals, and exceptions as well as coaching and accountability.

An agent-supported workflow can maintain the case, collect missing evidence, apply approved routine rules, update the record, and route only genuine exceptions. Human authority still sets policy, resolves contested trade-offs, changes permissions, and decides when work should stop. Flattening becomes possible when the first category shrinks in production; the second does not disappear with better automation.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up an isolated task while leaving handoffs, approvals, repair work, and exception routing in place. A flatter company is plausible only when the workflow absorbs that coordination rather than moving it to another inbox.</p></div>

## Make the evidence unit a correctly closed case

A task demo is too small to support an organization decision. The useful unit is a complete workflow, from the initial request to an outcome recorded in the company’s system. A correctly closed case is one that reached the right outcome, followed the required controls, left an accurate record, and required no hidden repair after the dashboard declared success.

I would assess that workflow through six local measures: outcome quality across a representative case mix; human routing touches; review and repair effort; control failures and near misses; total cost, including integration, operation, oversight, and incidents; and residual judgment, meaning the decisions that still need accountable human interpretation after routine work has been removed.

These measures are a proposed decision method, not an established benchmark. The verified material available for this article provides no Swedish or Norwegian production threshold for removing a management layer safely. Each company must therefore publish its own baseline, pass values, and stop values before the trial begins rather than selecting them after seeing the result.

The trial record should state a fixed window, expected case volume, and a case mix that includes normal, ambiguous, high-impact, and failed inputs. It should also name an independent reviewer, the sample that person will inspect, the review capacity available, every spend category, the budget owner, and the date on which leaders will scale, extend, or stop the trial.

If the workflow closes cases well but still needs frequent routing or repair, the evidence supports better workflow design rather than fewer layers. If coordination falls across a representative mix and the remaining judgment has clear owners, the organization has earned the right to test a structural change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Measure complete, correctly closed cases rather than task speed. Set local trial terms in advance and count hidden human work, controls, full cost, and residual judgment before drawing any conclusion about hierarchy.</p></div>

## Put authority where everyone can see it

Removing a coordination layer also removes an authority channel. The replacement needs to be more explicit than the hierarchy it supersedes. A domain map should name who owns the business outcome, the workflow boundary, permissions, routine policy, exceptional decisions, independent review, and the power to override, stop, restart, or roll back the process to its last safe state.

The operating boundary is straightforward: the agent interprets evidence and proposes or performs allowed steps; ordinary software enforces what it may do. That software holds the authoritative state, the official record of where the case currently stands, and rejects actions that are not permitted from that state. An instruction found inside a supplier document cannot expand the agent’s authority.

Established software engineering already supplies the control pattern. A policy engine applies permission rules. A durable workflow preserves progress across outages. Transactional messaging records an intended external action with the related business update, and reconciliation compares the company record with the external system before another attempt. The organizational choice is to keep those controls outside the model’s free-form reasoning.

[Idempotency](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-9.2.2) means that repeating an identical request has the same intended effect as sending it once, rather than creating a second consequential action. An operation identifier must be unique, and reuse with changed details must be rejected. The request, effect, and saved result also need to stay linked so the company can establish whether one authorized payment was issued. If a payment outcome is unknown, the workflow rejects a second payment attempt until reconciliation establishes the first result.

For every consequential action, the audit record should contain the case and operation identifiers, human or agent actor, model and policy versions, evidence used, previous and next state, reason, timestamps, external response, overrides, and final reconciliation. A reviewer can then replay what happened. A rejected illegal transition, such as moving from “payment outcome unknown” directly to “pay again,” should remain in that record with the rule that blocked it.

This design controls complexity without pretending that uncertainty can be eliminated. Named people still own policy exceptions, stop and restart decisions, supplier failure, and the final business outcome. The model has room to reason, but it does not get to invent its own mandate.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Let agents interpret and act only inside explicit boundaries. Named owners set authority, ordinary software enforces permissions and case state, and a replayable audit path supports override, stop, reconciliation, and rollback.</p></div>

## Redesign careers with the operating model

The old pyramid carries more than approval traffic. It also carries coaching, performance assessment, pay progression, sponsorship, and the promise that ambition will eventually be rewarded with a larger team. Removing levels without replacing those functions turns an operating-model change into a career crisis.

Start by mapping work rather than deleting titles. Separate routine routing, status collection, and standard approval from judgment, coaching, conflict resolution, domain stewardship, and accountability. Some managers may become domain owners, coaches, or exception authorities. Some coordination-heavy roles will shrink, and leaders should say so early enough for people to make real choices.

A flatter company also needs a career structure that does not use headcount as its main signal of seniority. Progression and pay can recognize domain mastery, decision quality, development of others, control stewardship, and sustained workflow improvement. Paid learning time, access to representative cases, mentoring, and funded redeployment make that structure credible; a catalogue of optional courses does not.

For a Nordic enterprise, I would make employee-representative consultation part of the authorization record before the trial design hardens and again before any structural decision. The same record should include an affected-work map, published selection criteria, learning capacity, redeployment routes, operational cover during transition, and honest support where a role cannot continue. Workforce readiness is part of the evidence for scale.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Flattening removes career and leadership infrastructure as well as routing work. Map affected work, rebuild progression and coaching, fund learning and redeployment, and make employee consultation a condition of structural change.</p></div>

## Make the org chart the last approval

The org chart should be the final artifact because every line removed transfers work, authority, or development responsibility somewhere else. Before approving that transfer, the CIO should bring a one-page authorization record to the executive team or board:

| Record | What must be stated before approval |
|---|---|
| Workflow scope | Start and end points, baseline, fixed trial window, expected volume, and representative case mix |
| Decision values | Local pass and stop values for quality, human routing, repair, controls, full cost, and residual judgment |
| Independent evidence | Reviewer, sampling plan, review capacity, findings, and unresolved uncertainty |
| Authority and controls | Domain owner, permissions, exceptions, audit record, override, stop, restart, reconciliation, rollback, and supplier exit |
| People readiness | Affected-work map, employee consultation, selection criteria, paid learning, redeployment, transition budget, and continuity cover |
| Decision | Accountable executive, budget owner, decision date, and a recorded choice to scale, extend, or stop |

This record allows leaders to authorize distributed technical judgment and workflow redesign without promising that a layer will disappear. On the decision date, structural change proceeds only if representative cases close within the company’s declared values, the control system works under failure, residual judgment has named owners, and the workforce commitments are ready to fund.

The architecture is therefore conditional but still consequential. An AI-first company can move ownership downward and remove coordination layers where coordination has genuinely ceased to be a management job. The CIO’s responsibility is to prove that change and account for its human cost before the hierarchy is asked to reflect it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Authorize organizational redesign with one record that binds workflow evidence, independent review, authority, controls, budget, consultation, and workforce readiness. Change the hierarchy only when the work and its ownership have already changed safely.</p></div>
