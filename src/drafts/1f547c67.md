The org chart is a lagging indicator. When leaders start an AI transformation by erasing boxes, they redesign the visible company before proving that the work underneath it has changed.

In a [June 2026 interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=342s), Arvind KC—chief people officer at [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), the AI research and product company behind ChatGPT—said that future roles would be “fundamentally a technical role” applied to a particular domain. The legacy argument linked that observation to a second proposition: as AI absorbs more execution and coordination, organizations can become flatter and more bottom-up, with decisions and improvement ideas originating closer to the work.

An AI agent is software built around a model that pursues a defined goal through approved tools and steps. Its arrival does not make flattening inevitable. It makes a different architecture possible: technical judgment moves into business roles, routine dependencies can move into software, and human authority becomes both more distributed and more explicit.

The responsible sequence is role, workflow, evidence, authority, and people. The org chart comes last.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI can support a flatter company, but only after the underlying work and authority have genuinely changed.</p></div>

## The role changes before the reporting line

Technical judgment is the business ability to decide what an AI system may do, what evidence it needs, where its capability ends, and when a person must take over. Coding can help, but it is not the requirement.

A salesperson needs to recognize which contract terms an agent may extract and which concessions require commercial authority. Finance must distinguish routine reconciliation from a decision that changes risk exposure. Operations defines acceptable process states and recovery paths. Marketing judges whether product evidence supports a claim. Support decides when a familiar policy applies and when ambiguity, vulnerability, or relationship consequences demand human care.

That judgment matters because AI capability is uneven. In a [2026 experiment involving 758 consultants](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838), participants using OpenAI’s GPT-4 model were 19 percent less likely to reach the correct answer on one selected task outside the tested capability frontier—the set of tasks the model handled reliably. Fluent output did not reveal that boundary. The worker had to understand it.

Article 4 of the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:02024R1689-20260727), the European Union’s 2024 risk-based AI regulation, similarly requires providers and deployers to support an appropriate level of AI literacy among relevant staff while considering their knowledge, training, experience, and use context. AI literacy means enough contextual understanding to use and oversee the system responsibly, not an engineering credential for every employee.

Distributed technical judgment therefore changes the center’s job. A central AI function can provide platforms, assurance, and shared standards. Domain leaders still own the capability boundary inside their work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to recognize reliable AI work, unsuitable cases, and the right escalation point.</p></div>

## One clean output can conceal the old company

Consider a hypothetical regional marketing team preparing a new subscription offer. An agent can read approved product information, draft channel copy, adapt it for several audiences, and propose a launch package in minutes.

The surrounding company may remain untouched. People still chase the current price, verify product claims, request privacy and legal review, confirm the budget, correct an obsolete disclaimer, approve the audience, release the campaign, update the official record, and withdraw assets when a product condition changes. The draft became faster. The dependency chain did not.

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book [*The AI First Company*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls this the Retrofit Trap: accelerating AI-assisted tasks while preserving the old structure around them. The term is useful because it directs attention away from generated output and toward operating design.

In 1994, coordination researchers Thomas Malone and Kevin Crowston defined [coordination](https://doi.org/10.1145/174666.174668) as “managing dependencies between activities.” Jay Galbraith, an organization-design scholar, had explained in [1974](https://doi.org/10.1287/inte.4.3.28) why hierarchy often receives exceptions that established rules cannot settle. A hierarchy does more than route information, but information assembly, approval routing, status chasing, and known-exception handling are among the functions software may absorb.

A complete workflow runs from the initial trigger through the business outcome, official records, exceptions, and closure. For this article, a correctly closed case is the proposed evidence unit: the full outcome meets declared quality and policy conditions, the authoritative records are updated, every human intervention and repair is counted, and no hidden cleanup remains.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A fast AI output is not an AI-first operating model when people still carry the surrounding dependencies and cleanup.</p></div>

## Flatten only what the workflow no longer needs

A management layer becomes a candidate for redesign when its routine coordination has durably left the workflow. That is an operating hypothesis to test, not a forecast to announce.

The trial must measure correctly closed cases by predeclared case type, elapsed time from request to closure, human routing and approval touches, repair and reopening, outcome quality, policy and control failures, and total cost. Total cost includes models, integration, monitoring, human review, exception handling, and supplier fees. It must also expose residual judgment and any work that resurfaces as informal supervision, frontline cleanup, or extra risk review.

The agent-supported design needs two comparators: the current process and the strongest reasonable conventional alternative using fixed workflow rules or other established automation. If ordinary software removes the same coordination more cheaply and reliably, the agent has not earned organizational credit for it.

No validated Swedish or Norwegian production case identified in the current evidence supplies a numerical threshold for removing a management layer. Magnus needs locally declared conditions, observed over a company-set period, with adverse cases kept in the result rather than relabeled after the fact.

Even strong workflow evidence will leave human responsibilities. Novel trade-offs, changes to risk appetite, employment decisions, conflict resolution, coaching, and final accountability should not disappear into a model. Flattening is defensible only when leaders can name which responsibility vanished, which moved toward the domain, and which remained human.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Structural change is earned by complete-workflow evidence showing that coordination disappeared instead of moving somewhere less visible.</p></div>

## Move authority toward the work without hiding it

A flatter organization needs clearer authority because it has fewer places for ambiguity to hide. Economists Philippe Aghion and Jean Tirole distinguished [formal authority from real authority](https://doi.org/10.1086/262063) in 1997: the assigned right to decide can differ from the effective control over what happens.

That distinction matters when a domain team appears empowered but a vendor setting, inaccessible model instruction, central data owner, or concealed approval rule still determines the outcome. A decision right is explicit authority to propose, approve, reject, override, stop, or restart. Domain-mapped ownership assigns those rights to named business and control roles around a bounded area of work.

The domain owner defines the acceptable outcome, case classes, limits, and escalation rules. The agent interprets the campaign request and proposes a next step. Company-controlled software checks identity, permission, current case state, policy limits, and duplicate protection before anything consequential is released. A named human owns exceptions, overrides, stop and restart decisions, and the final accountability. The system records the actor, action, source, state, time, outcome, and available recovery path.

This division gives the edge room to act without asking a model to invent its own permissions. It also makes bottom-up ownership inspectable: the domain can improve the workflow inside declared boundaries, while changes to those boundaries follow visible authority.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Real empowerment combines domain ownership with explicit decision rights and company-controlled enforcement.</p></div>

## Design for the bad afternoon

The architecture becomes real when the campaign changes halfway through release. Authoritative state means the current approved status and version of the case. If the product owner changes the price after the agent prepares its package, the release software should reject the stale proposal and require a fresh one.

Retries need equal care. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-9.2.2), a 2022 internet standard from the Internet Engineering Task Force, defines idempotency narrowly: repeated identical requests have the same intended server effect as one request. A business workflow needs a stronger contract. One intended launch should have a stable identity, concurrent duplicates should be rejected, and a retry should return or reconcile with the stored outcome rather than buy the media twice.

External effects cannot always be reversed with an internal database change. If the campaign goes live but the official record fails to update, the workflow should enter a visible pending state while the systems are reconciled. Rollback restores software, configuration, or workflow rules to an earlier safe version. A compensating action is a new action—such as pausing the campaign and issuing a correction—that addresses an external effect that cannot simply be erased.

Every consequential action needs an audit trail, and a named human needs stop authority: both the right and the practical mechanism to halt new actions. The audit trail records who or what acted, the source and case state used, the time, the outcome, and any override. Restart belongs to a named role after investigation, not to whoever notices that the queue is growing.

The same principle applies to suppliers. Supplier exit means a tested ability to replace the provider or run the workflow manually without losing control of policy, state, records, and recovery. Otherwise, apparent decentralization can leave real authority outside the company.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Permissions, current state, duplicate protection, audit, recovery, and stop authority belong in enforceable controls around the agent.</p></div>

## Replace the ladder before removing rungs

The hardest organizational question is what happens to people when a large share of their coordinating work disappears. A role may contain routing and status collection alongside coaching, conflict resolution, institutional memory, judgment, and accountability. Treating the whole role as one automatable block hides both the human value and the transition cost.

The International Labour Organization, the United Nations agency for work and labor standards, reached a similar boundary in its [2025 analysis of generative AI and jobs](https://doi.org/10.54394/HETP0387): exposure to AI-capable tasks does not imply immediate automation of an entire occupation, and transformation is more likely because occupations still contain work requiring human input.

A credible redesign names the activities expected to shrink, the judgment that remains, and the path available to the people affected. That path needs funded learning time, transparent selection and redeployment rules, expert progression without headcount ownership, clear coaching responsibility, and pay bands that do not make management the only route to status. Ambition needs somewhere to go when the old pyramid narrows.

Employee participation also belongs inside the operating design. Social dialogue means structured discussion and negotiation among employers, workers, and their representatives. In Sweden, Section 11 of the [Co-Determination in the Workplace Act of 1976](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-1976580-om-medbestammande-i-arbetslivet_sfs-1976-580/) requires negotiation before qualifying significant changes where the statutory and collective-agreement conditions apply. Local legal and collective arrangements determine the exact obligation.

The workflow should therefore have two workforce checkpoints: one before the trial changes how work is performed, and another before evidence is used to change roles, pay, or reporting lines. A named owner must be able to pause the organizational decision when those commitments are incomplete.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A flatter structure needs new routes for learning, coaching, pay, progression, participation, and human judgment before old roles are removed.</p></div>

## Ask for two decisions, not one

The executive paper should be short enough to argue about and complete enough to reject. For one bounded workflow, it should state:

- the business outcome, case classes, company-set limits, and evidence period;
- the domain owner, permanent service owner, control owner, independent evidence owner, and executive sponsor;
- the current-process and conventional-automation comparators, with correctly closed cases, coordination, repair, quality, failures, cost, and residual judgment measured on the same basis;
- permissions, exception owners, stop and restart conditions, audit requirements, recovery tests, and supplier exit; and
- workforce checkpoints, learning commitments, selection principles, and the authority to pause structural change.

That paper supports two separate decisions. The first authorizes a reversible workflow trial and allows the company to expand it, narrow it, move it into normal operations, or stop. The second considers the organization only after the evidence holds, permanent ownership exists, controls have survived failure testing, and the people commitments are funded.

Combining those decisions turns an experiment into a restructuring promise before the facts exist. Separating them preserves the legacy possibility: agents may absorb enough execution and coordination for a materially flatter, more bottom-up company. It also gives Magnus the grounds to say no when the apparent gain is really hidden labor, weak control, supplier dependence, or an unfunded human transition.

The responsible CIO does not promise flatness. The company has to earn it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Authorize a reversible workflow trial first; change the org chart only when evidence, authority, recovery, and workforce commitments make the second decision defensible.</p></div>
