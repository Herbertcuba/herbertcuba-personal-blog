Most discussions about AI and jobs begin with a task: can software write the memo, analyze the file, or answer the question? Organization design begins one step later. Who moves the result, decides whether it is safe to use, and takes responsibility when it is wrong?

An [AI agent](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/) is a program in which an AI model can follow instructions, use approved tools, and pursue a task through several steps. That lets it do more than return an answer. It can collect the inputs for the next stage, apply a rule, prepare an action, and send an unusual case to a person.

That movement is where the structural argument starts. In May 2026, engineer turned business coach Benjamin Simkin published *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, an independently published guide to rebuilding a company around AI. The book uses “AI-first” for a business that starts its process design with demonstrated AI capabilities. Simkin calls the familiar alternative the “Retrofit Trap”: placing AI inside work built for manual human operation while leaving the surrounding structure intact.

In February 2026, [OpenAI appointed Arvind KC as Chief People Officer](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/). OpenAI develops ChatGPT, the generative AI assistant, and KC later shared public reflections about how the company works. Two of his claims matter here: “every role is a technical role,” and bottom-up innovation, where decisions come from people near the work, can make the company look chaotic.

I think Simkin and KC are describing the same pressure from different sides. When software carries more of the work between tasks, a company may need fewer people whose main function is to keep those tasks connected. A flatter structure is therefore a possible consequence of a changed workflow, never proof that the workflow has changed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI agents affect the shape of a company when they carry work across stages. Fewer layers may follow, but only after leaders can show where coordination moved and where human judgment still belongs.</p></div>

## The chart follows the handoff

Coordination is the work that preserves continuity between tasks. Someone gathers status, finds the right record, translates a request, chases an approval, and tells the next person to begin. A management pyramid often carries this load because information moves slowly and each handoff needs a human relay.

Consider supplier onboarding. An agent could organize submitted documents, compare them with internal policy, draft questions about missing information, and route an unusual term to the procurement owner. If people no longer spend their time chasing forms or writing status updates, some coordination has genuinely moved into software.

The same workflow can also expose the Retrofit Trap. If every recommendation still waits for the old meetings and signatures because nobody trusts the new path, the agent has created faster preparation without changing the organization around it. The visible task improved; the relay survived.

This is why the workflow has to run before anyone redraws reporting lines. Leaders need to see where the agent hesitates, which cases escape its rules, who repairs its mistakes, and whether the human relay quietly reappears in email or chat. That evidence says more about the need for a layer than a workshop about becoming “flat.”

Managers also coach people, settle conflict, assign scarce resources, set priorities, and carry accountability. Those responsibilities do not leave with the status update. I think a layer becomes removable only when its routine relay work has shrunk and its remaining human duties have been placed somewhere visible.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Follow the handoffs before changing the hierarchy. An agent may remove routine chasing and routing, but a layer can disappear safely only when its remaining human responsibilities have clear homes.</p></div>

## Authority has to travel too

KC’s statement that every role is technical does not mean every employee must program. It means that anyone making a decision with an AI system needs to understand what the system saw, what rules constrained it, where it tends to fail, and when its output requires human review. That is technical judgment expressed at the level of the role.

In the supplier example, the procurement owner does not need to build the agent. They do need to know whether it used the current policy, whether a source document was missing, and why a contract term reached them as an exception. Without that knowledge, authority drifts back to a technical team that can explain the system but cannot own the commercial consequence.

Bottom-up innovation works only when authority and understanding meet near the work. Every consequential agent-run process therefore needs a named human who sets its limits, defines acceptable output, receives serious exceptions, and can stop it. This is structure, even if the chart has fewer lines.

I think this is the harder test for a flat company. Relay work can vanish, while judgment cannot. If decision rights remain vague, the organization develops a hidden hierarchy in which the small group controlling the agents also controls decisions that appear to be distributed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical literacy lets authority move closer to the work, while named human ownership keeps that authority accountable. A flatter chart without both conditions merely hides where decisions are being made.</p></div>

## The missing rung becomes a debt

A flatter company changes more than decision speed. The traditional career ladder often gives people greater pay and influence by putting them in charge of more staff and more information flow. When the relay roles shrink, that route to seniority narrows with them.

The replacement has to be explicit. People need a path to gain scope through deeper expertise, responsibility for more consequential decisions, better system design, or the development of other people’s judgment. Otherwise the company removes a layer from the chart while leaving ambition attached to a rung that no longer exists.

I don’t know whether people whose roles depend on coordination will see responsibility for a defined business area as a real next step, or how many employers can support that move. A new tool and a thinner chart still do not amount to a career plan. Leaders need to explain which activities are ending, which judgments remain human, how progression will work, and what support is available for the transition.

My take is that flattening without career design is cost reduction presented as organizational architecture. The human bargain has to change with the operating model. I think the AI-first company will earn its new shape when it removes needless movement while making responsibility, expertise, and a credible future easier to see.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>When coordination roles shrink, career growth needs a new basis in expertise, decision scope, system design, and the development of others. A credible AI-first structure removes needless relay work without removing the future people were working toward.</p></div>
