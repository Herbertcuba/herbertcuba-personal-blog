Do not remove a management layer because an AI pilot looks busy. Remove it only when you can prove that the coordination work carried by that layer has disappeared without hiding new cost, risk, or human review somewhere else. I call this the *relay-layer test*.

A relay layer collects updates, translates between teams, routes routine approvals, and makes work visible to people above it. The term describes a function, not a job title. A manager who coaches people, allocates resources, resolves conflict, sets policy, or carries accountability is doing work that an automated handoff does not replace.

This distinction matters because workflow automation and organizational redesign are different claims. A company can automate a large share of a queue and still need human authority for the remaining cases, especially when those cases carry most of the risk. The evidence in this article shows that AI software can change a routine path. It does not prove that any particular company has safely removed a management layer, and I think that boundary should be part of the thesis rather than hidden in a footnote.

Two sources published in 2026 helped sharpen this question. On 24 February, [Arvind KC became Chief People Officer at OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), the AI company behind the ChatGPT generative AI assistant. On 10 May, Benjamin Simkin published *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, an independently published field manual. Simkin calls the old approach the “Retrofit Trap”: adding AI to work designed for manual human execution while keeping the handoffs, approvals, and reporting structure intact. These are useful signals, but the proof still has to come from the work.

The question I keep getting from founders and executives is what an AI-first organization looks like in practice. My answer begins with *agentic production*. An [AI agent](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/) is software that uses an AI model, instructions, data, and tools to carry out a multi-step task. Agentic production is an operating model in which agents execute routine work and coordinate handoffs while people set goals, own policy, and decide consequential exceptions. The chart should change only after that operating model carries the work within declared limits and can return it safely to people when it fails.

The deeper redesign covers human authority and careers as well as reporting lines.</p></div>

## The relay-layer test

Two claims shape the AI-first company. First, people throughout the business need enough technical understanding to judge how AI changes decisions in their domain. Second, some hierarchies can become flatter where agents reliably take over execution and routine coordination.

The second claim is narrower than “management disappears.” A supervisor who mainly assigns cases, requests status, gathers context, and checks standard answers may see much of that relay work shrink. The same person may still be needed to coach the team, handle sensitive cases, settle cross-functional conflict, or remain accountable for the service. The test asks which work disappeared, not which title looks expensive.

That gives the argument a clear failure condition. If removing a layer increases unresolved exceptions, weakens decisions, raises the full cost of a completed task, or pushes hidden review onto domain experts, the agent has not replaced coordination. The company has made coordination harder to see. I think flattening belongs after demonstrated reliability, never inside the business case used to justify the pilot.

Simkin's Retrofit Trap helps locate the test. If the process still depends on the same human routing and approvals after the AI tool arrives, the company has improved a step without changing its architecture. If the routine relay work genuinely disappears, the chart may be able to follow, provided the old layer's authority and career functions are replaced deliberately.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A relay layer is removable only when its coordination function has been replaced and measured. Coaching, resource choices, conflict resolution, policy, and accountability remain human responsibilities even when routine routing becomes automatic.</p></div>

## Every role becomes technical

A technical role does not require everyone to code or put engineers in every seat. It means decisions in sales, operations, finance, marketing, and support now depend on what an AI system can do, where it fails, and how its output should be checked. The person responsible for the decision needs to understand those limits even when someone else builds the system.

I've watched this play out in organizations I advise on agentic production. A marketing team stalls when it cannot judge whether an AI-generated campaign brief reflects the strategy. An operations team cannot safely skip human review if it has no way to measure the accuracy of an agent's ticket classification. A finance team cannot choose well between a system that reduces repeated reconciliation and a tool that has not changed cycle time, error rates, or cost.

This is a literacy rather than a coding skill. I think AI systems act as amplifiers because clear instructions, sound source material, and a useful quality standard can spread through a workflow. Confused ownership and weak judgment spread just as easily, often behind a dashboard that looks like progress.

There is established engineering underneath this idea. Workflow engines and *state machines*, software that moves work through predefined stages according to rules, already route cases whose paths can be specified in advance. An agent changes a narrower part of the system: it can interpret material such as a customer email and propose one of the permitted next steps when every wording variation cannot be written as a rule. Permissions, records, and escalation paths remain ordinary workflow engineering, and they matter more when an AI model helps choose the path.

The old organization sends technical questions to a separate function. An AI-first organization still needs specialists, but technical reasoning becomes a shared language for decisions. That shared language allows authority to move closer to the work without turning local freedom into guesswork.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A role becomes technical when its owner can judge how AI affects the decision in front of them. Agents add flexible interpretation to established workflow engineering; they do not remove the need for rules, permissions, or specialist knowledge.</p></div>

## When a relay layer can disappear

Consider a customer-support workflow. An agent can receive a ticket, classify the issue, retrieve the customer's account history and contract terms, draft a response, and check it against policy. A routine case moves forward only when the source systems agree, the proposed action stays within an approved limit, and the answer passes the company's quality checks. An exception pauses the workflow and goes to a named support owner, who sees the evidence, makes the decision, and remains accountable.

The operating contract should be explicit. The normal path is received, evidence gathered, proposal prepared, authorized, executed, and closed. An exception moves from proposal prepared to escalated, then waits for a human decision. An audit trail, meaning the record of what the system used and did, stores the inputs, sources, tools, policy version, proposed action, authorization result, final action, and any human decision.

The model proposes a path; a deterministic authorization service, a rule-based component that does not rely on the model's judgment, enforces permissions. A named person approves exceptions. The forbidden transition is direct movement from escalated to automated execution. Each approved action also receives a unique transaction key, so a retry cannot issue the same refund or account change twice.

“Reliable enough” needs an acceptance gate, which is a set of conditions declared before the trial starts. Here is an illustrative ten-week charter for one support queue. The exact numbers should change with case volume, risk, and the customer promise, but the ownership should not remain vague.

| Trial decision | Illustrative commitment |
| --- | --- |
| Comparator | Support operations records a four-week human baseline, then runs a six-week supervised trial on a comparable case mix. |
| Quality | The support quality lead uses a blind review, where reviewers do not know whether a person or agent prepared the answer. A 200-case weekly sample must match or beat the human baseline, with no critical policy failure. |
| Economics and review | The finance partner owns full cost per resolved case, including model use, integration, supervision, and rework. The operations owner requires cost at or below baseline and sets the maximum review share before launch. |
| Authorization | The security and policy owners approve data access and tool permissions. The model may propose; the rule-based authorization service may permit; a named human decides exceptions. |
| Stop and rollback | Any privacy or security breach stops the trial. Two consecutive weekly quality misses trigger rollback, meaning the support operations lead returns the queue to the human process. |
| Decision forum and funding | The executive sponsor funds integration and scheduled reviewer time. Support, finance, security, privacy, and the relevant employee representative review the evidence before any reporting line changes. |

A representative failure shows why the parts of this gate cannot be merged into one success rate. Imagine that a weekly blind review scores 91 percent against a 94 percent human baseline. The audit trail shows that the model proposed a refund under an expired policy, while the authorization service rejected the action and sent the case to a person. The guardrail prevented customer harm, but the quality gate still failed. If the next weekly sample also misses, the rollback rule fires. Safe containment is evidence that control works; it is not evidence that the workflow is ready to replace a layer.

A public Swedish case illustrates both the opportunity and the limit. In February 2024, Klarna, the Swedish payments company, [reported that its AI assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month](https://www.klarna.com/international/press/klarna-ai-assistant-handles-two-thirds-of-customer-service-chats-in-its-first-month/), equal to two-thirds of its support chats. Klarna said customer satisfaction matched its human agents, repeat inquiries fell by 25 percent, and resolution time dropped from 11 minutes to under two. These are company-reported figures, so they describe Klarna's claim rather than an independent assessment. They still show the kind of baseline comparison an operating decision needs.

The case did not become a simple argument for removing the human path. In June 2025, [Klarna chief executive Sebastian Siemiatkowski said the company would keep using AI for manual support work while promising customers a human connection](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/04/klarna-ceo-says-company-will-use-humans-to-offer-vip-customer-service/). He described human service as a premium experience. Klarna's figures support redesigning the routine path; they do not show that a management layer was removed safely, that the full economics improved, or that a replacement career system worked.

That evidence boundary is useful. Automation volume can earn a closer look at the chart, but it cannot settle the decision. The company still has to show that quality, cost, review burden, permissions, accountability, and reversibility hold together. I think this is the difference between redesign and headcount theatre.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A relay layer can change only after a bounded trial beats a declared baseline and proves authorization, auditability, and rollback. Klarna shows the scale possible in a routine path, while its retained human option and the limits of its public evidence show why automation volume alone cannot justify flattening.</p></div>

## The compounding advantage

Once a domain expert can supervise an agent workflow, the role gains a second capability. An *agent manager* is a person who can specify work for agents, check their results, and improve the workflow over time. The title matters less than the combination of technical literacy and real domain expertise.

An editor can use agents to prepare drafts, collect sources for claims, and flag repeated language before the final edit. A finance analyst can have agents pull and reconcile records, then surface anomalies for investigation. A customer-success lead can use them to triage cases and prepare account summaries, preserving attention for relationship risk and difficult conversations. In each example, the agent handles repeatable preparation while the specialist remains responsible for judgment.

I think the advantage compounds only when saved effort improves the system that produced it. Human corrections need to change the instructions, source material, policies, or tests used by the workflow. If learning stays in one person's head, the same errors recur at higher speed and the apparent gain remains fragile.

I don't know a defensible productivity multiplier that applies across companies. The result depends on task volume, baseline quality, the cost of mistakes, and how much human review remains. The strategic advantage comes from the direction of learning: a team that turns corrections into a better shared system can improve faster than one that keeps adding isolated tools to the old process.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agent management can multiply a domain expert's capacity, but the gain compounds only when corrections improve the shared workflow. The size of the gain belongs in trial evidence, not in a universal productivity claim.</p></div>

## Distributed ownership needs governance

A flatter company will often look less tidy because more decisions happen close to the work. Leaders see more local movement and fewer requests for permission. That can be productive, but motion itself is not evidence that the design works.

The safeguard is explicit *decision rights*, meaning a written answer to who may decide what and when an issue must be escalated. Reversible choices that stay within one domain, budget, quality standard, and policy boundary can usually sit close to the work. Company strategy, major capital allocation, security and privacy rules, legal obligations, cross-domain conflicts, and high-impact people or customer decisions still need central authority.

The most useful frame from my own work is *domain-mapped ownership*. Each domain has one person who defines good output, sets operating limits, reviews exceptions, and can change the workflow. Its purpose is to connect an agent's permission boundary and model-proposed choices to a human owner who can make the final call. It also avoids the Everybody Reviews Everything Trap, where feedback conflicts and nobody owns the result.

For a European support workflow, the boundary must reflect the [EU General Data Protection Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng), the European Union's 2016 regulation governing personal-data processing. It must also account for any applicable duties under the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng), the European Union's 2024 risk-based regulation of AI systems. Legal scope depends on the use case, so privacy, security, legal, and supplier-risk owners need to shape permissions before they are delegated. Data access, retention, human oversight, and escalation then become enforceable workflow terms rather than a policy promise.

The same principle applies to worker governance. Where law, collective agreements, or company commitments require employee consultation, that work belongs before the reporting line moves. I think leaders can tolerate distributed experimentation only when customers, employees, and accountable executives can still see who made a decision and how to challenge it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Distributed ownership works when local authority has a named owner, measurable limits, a decision record, and an escalation path. Privacy, security, legal, supplier, and worker-governance boundaries must be built into the workflow before hierarchy is removed.</p></div>

## The career ladder is part of the architecture

The harder question is what happens to the people whose coordination tasks shrink. People are not absorbed by agents; parts of their work are. Some managers can become domain owners or agent managers. Others may retrain, move elsewhere in the company, or leave. Organizations I have advised during AI-first reorganizations have handled this differently, but the human transition has been harder than the technical one.

A flatter organization also breaks a familiar promise about ambition. If there are fewer management layers, progression can no longer depend mainly on acquiring more direct reports. Companies need credible paths through deeper craft, larger domain ownership, technical leadership, or responsibility that crosses domains. Otherwise they remove a layer of coordination and accidentally remove the main language of status and reward.

Consider a support supervisor whose relay work has largely disappeared. A credible next role could be service-domain owner, responsible for quality thresholds, policy changes, exception patterns, and improvements to the agent workflow. The company would fund training and scheduled learning time before the role changes, place the new position in a pay band with authority comparable to the former management role, and judge progression by the performance and safety of the domain rather than the number of direct reports. This is a structural example, not a promise that every supervisor will make the transition, but it shows what a real alternative career path has to contain.

That makes the employee compact, the set of promises about transition and progression, part of the operating model. Before a workflow changes the chart, leadership should name who owns consultation, who funds and schedules reskilling, how redeployment decisions will be made, and which non-managerial paths carry greater pay and authority. Leaving these choices implicit turns efficiency into a promise employees have no reason to trust.

My take is to begin with one workflow and one bounded decision. Draw the target process, record the baseline, name the human owner, limit the agent's tools, write the authorization, escalation, stop, and rollback rules, fund the people transition, and measure the complete result before changing a reporting line. Then use what the team learns to redesign the next part of the company.

The architecture of an AI-first company is more demanding than a flat chart. It asks leaders to prove which coordination work has become unnecessary, preserve authority where judgment still matters, and build a credible future for the people who carried the middle. A company has not redesigned itself when the diagram gets smaller; it has redesigned itself when the work, authority, and path forward still hold after the layer is gone.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An AI-first company earns a flatter structure by proving that relay work has disappeared, preserving human authority, and giving people a credible route into work with real pay and decision rights. Without those conditions, a smaller chart is only a smaller chart.</p></div>
