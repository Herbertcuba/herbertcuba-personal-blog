An AI-first company is designed around AI-enabled workflows rather than adding AI tools to jobs that otherwise remain unchanged. It begins with two claims. Every role acquires technical judgment, the ability to assess what AI can do reliably and where a person must intervene. Ownership can also move closer to the work as AI agents, software systems that interpret goals and take approved actions in business tools, absorb execution and coordination.

Technical judgment is already a leadership responsibility. A flatter structure is a possibility, not a forecast. I think it can be the result, but changing the hierarchy before the work has changed merely moves uncertainty from the workflow into the org chart.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Technical judgment should spread now; organizational flattening should wait until workflow evidence, authority, controls, and workforce commitments make it defensible.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment belongs in every function

[Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), appointed in 2026 as chief people officer of OpenAI, an AI research and deployment company, described the capability shift [in a recorded interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s). He said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role,” then explained that the technical role is “applied to a particular domain.” In the same conversation, he describes innovation at OpenAI as bottom-up, meaning initiative is not reserved for senior leadership.

That observation from one company does not prove that every enterprise can remove management layers. It does point to a capability leaders can build. Technical judgment lets a domain expert specify what evidence an AI-generated result needs, recognize likely failure, and know when to escalate. Coding may help some roles, but it is not the requirement.

A salesperson needs this judgment before relying on an AI-generated account recommendation. Finance needs it when an anomaly may be an error, fraud, or an unusual but valid transaction. Operations, marketing, and support need the same ability in their own domains. When that judgment sits at the edge, more decisions can be made there, but only within clear boundaries.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to test AI, recognize its limits, and preserve human authority; that is different from asking everyone to become a software engineer.</p>
</div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the hierarchy

Benjamin Simkin’s 2026 business book [*The AI First Company*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5) gives a useful name to AI added inside an unchanged organization: the Retrofit Trap. A local task may speed up while the surrounding approvals, handoffs, and repair work remain in place. The operating model then has no reason to change.

Consider a hypothetical enterprise-customer activation. An agent may produce a handover summary in seconds. If people still chase tax details, resolve duplicate records, request access, confirm billing, and ask managers to route every exception, the agent has accelerated a document rather than the workflow.

This matters because hierarchy is partly a coordination system. It gathers information, translates priorities, assigns work, routes approvals, and catches exceptions. An agent-supported workflow can absorb some of that movement by carrying the case, collecting evidence, and sending only defined exceptions to the right owner. Human leaders should still set policy, accept material risk, resolve genuinely novel cases, and coach people. The architectural opportunity appears only when routine coordination has actually left the layer.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>An isolated AI shortcut leaves the surrounding coordination system intact. A flatter structure becomes plausible only when the complete workflow needs fewer handoffs, approvals, and repairs.</p>
</div>

## Prove the workflow before flattening the company

The useful unit of evidence is a correctly closed case. I use that term for a case that reaches the right business outcome, follows policy, leaves every relevant record consistent, and does not depend on hidden human repair. For customer activation, the endpoint is not a polished handover. It is an active, billable account with required checks complete, exceptions recorded, and a named owner for anything unresolved.

Leaders should compare that outcome with the previous process across a representative mix of ordinary, difficult, and exception cases. The record should include human routing touches, repair effort, elapsed time, outcome quality, control failures, total cost, and the judgment still supplied by people. A smooth demonstration or a high task-completion rate cannot answer whether the organization is ready to remove a layer.

No universal threshold follows from this test. The evidence assembled for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production benchmark that establishes a safe point for management-layer removal. A leadership team must therefore state its own acceptance conditions before the trial begins, tie them to the workflow’s risk, and treat the scorecard as a decision instrument rather than an industry benchmark.

The practical test is simple: if the coordination layer disappeared tomorrow, which work would vanish, which work would move to a domain team, and which work would return as informal repair? If the last category is vague, the case for flattening is incomplete.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Judge complete, correctly closed cases and count hidden human work. No transferable Nordic threshold can make the decision to remove a layer on management’s behalf.</p>
</div>

## Keep authority in the governed workflow

A flatter organization needs more explicit authority, not less. Start by mapping each business domain to a human owner who is accountable for outcomes and policy. Then state which decisions an agent may execute, which it may only propose, which require approval, and who can grant an exception. Bottom-up ownership means local action inside published limits, not unrestricted autonomy.

The technical boundary can remain understandable to an executive. Ordinary rules-based software should enforce identity, permissions, limits, and authoritative state, meaning the official record of where a case currently stands. An activation might move through **proposed → approved → executed → reconciled**. Reconciliation is the check that the intended action, the external result, and the company’s records agree before the case closes. A request based on an outdated case state, or an action over an approved limit, should be rejected or held for review without changing the official record.

A retry, the same request sent again after a timeout or failure, needs similar discipline. The Internet Engineering Task Force, the standards body that develops internet protocols, defines an [idempotent method in its 2022 HTTP standard](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods) as one for which identical repeated requests have the same intended effect as a single request. HTTP is the request-and-response protocol used by web systems. Applied as a workflow design principle, idempotency means a retry should not create a second customer account. When an external system leaves the outcome uncertain, the workflow reconciles the records instead of asking the AI model to guess what happened.

Every override should record who acted, why, and what changed. Stop authority is the named power to pause the automated path; rollback is the tested way to restore the previous workflow or system version. The business process owner should own continuity, while the technology owner owns technical rollback. The agent can interpret messy input and propose a next action, but it should not become the hidden source of permission, policy, or final state.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Put domain ownership and decision rights in the open. Let agents interpret and propose while ordinary software controls permission, state, retries, audit, stop authority, and rollback.</p>
</div>

## Redesign the career system with the operating model

Coordination-heavy roles will change if agents take over routing, follow-up, and routine exception handling. Some people can move toward domain ownership, coaching, complex judgment, control design, or workflow improvement. Other roles may shrink. A serious operating model names both outcomes before presenting flattening as empowerment.

Before changing a layer, leaders also need to map the career system attached to it: pay, status, progression, and access to leadership. If a junior colleague currently gains context by taking part in approvals and cross-functional routing, removing that work may also remove a local learning channel. Leaders should test whether that is true in their organization, then replace any lost learning deliberately through supervised case ownership, coaching time, and clear progression criteria.

The transition record should cover changed role descriptions, pay implications, funded learning time, redeployment criteria, and support where no credible destination exists. The exact participation duty varies by jurisdiction. In Sweden, the [Co-Determination in the Workplace Act (1976:580)](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-1976580-om-medbestammande-i-arbetslivet_sfs-1976-580/) requires employers covered by a collective agreement to negotiate with the relevant employee organization before deciding on major changes to the business or to working and employment conditions. Participation belongs in the design process, not after the new chart is announced.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Flattening changes careers as well as coordination. Role paths, pay, learning, redeployment, employee participation, and support for those without a destination must be designed before a layer is removed.</p>
</div>

## Move the boxes only after the signatures

A CIO can turn this argument into an operating sequence. First, the executive sponsor appoints a process owner and convenes operations, technology, risk, finance, HR, and employee representatives. The process owner defines the workflow endpoint, chooses a representative case set, and establishes the old process baseline. An evidence owner who is independent of daily delivery then verifies outcomes and hidden human effort.

The review calendar and decision options should be agreed before the trial starts. It should include baseline sign-off, interim reviews timed to case volume and risk, and a final review before any structural decision. Finance validates end-to-end cost, while the risk owner signs the limits and exception path. The technology owner signs the state and audit controls and remains accountable for rollback. HR and employee representatives record the workforce commitments.

The accountable business executive owns the service outcome and chairs the choice between continuing, narrowing the scope, stopping and reverting, or opening a formal organization-design process. A breached stop condition triggers the agreed pause and reversal path. A missing sign-off blocks the structural option.

Before that last option, the executive forum should require one signed record containing:

- the workflow boundary, baseline, and case mix;
- correctly closed outcomes, failures, escalations, human touches, repair effort, and cost;
- domain owners, decision rights, exception routes, stop authority, and audit evidence;
- technical dependencies, reconciliation results, and the tested rollback path;
- role changes, learning and pay commitments, participation status, and the owner of the workforce transition.

This record does not prove that every AI-first company should become flat. It proves something more useful: whether a particular coordination layer still performs necessary work in this company, under these controls, with these consequences acknowledged. If that evidence is incomplete, the layer stays while the workflow improves. If the record is signed and the work has genuinely moved, changing the organization becomes a defensible leadership decision rather than a wager on AI.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The CIO should change the org chart only after independent workflow evidence, named authority, tested reversal, financial validation, and workforce commitments are signed by accountable owners.</p>
</div>
