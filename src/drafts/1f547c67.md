An AI-first company begins with two linked propositions. The first is that every business role acquires a technical dimension. The second is that innovation can become more bottom-up, with ownership closer to the people who understand each domain and a flatter organization above them.

The first proposition has a clear source. Arvind KC, identified in [this recorded conversation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ) as chief people officer at [OpenAI, an AI research and deployment company](https://openai.com/about/), says that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately connects that capability to a particular domain, meaning an area of the business with its own expertise and accountability.

The second proposition is an organizational hypothesis. If AI agents absorb enough routine execution and coordination, more decisions can sit with domain owners instead of travelling up and down a hierarchy. An AI agent here means software that uses a generative AI model to interpret context, choose steps, and act toward a goal. KC’s recorded observation supports the claim about technical roles; it does not establish that management layers can safely disappear.

That distinction sets the leadership test. A company can become materially flatter, but the org chart should change only after complete-workflow evidence shows that coordination has been removed rather than displaced into hidden repair work. The replacement also needs explicit decision rights, controlled technical complexity, and a credible transition for the people whose work and prospects will change.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Distributed technical judgment makes a flatter company possible. Delayering still depends on proof that agents have removed coordination safely and sustainably.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment moves to the edge

Technical judgment is the ability to decide what an AI system can do reliably, what evidence it needs, how failure will appear, and when a person must take over. This capability is broader than coding. People need to understand the operational consequences of using a probabilistic system, which produces likely answers rather than guaranteed ones, inside their own work.

A salesperson needs to know when an account summary is sufficiently grounded to shape a proposal. Finance needs to distinguish a useful anomaly from an unsupported accusation. Operations must understand which exceptions can follow policy and which require judgment. Marketing and support need to know when generated material may be sent, when it needs review, and who carries the consequence if it is wrong.

This changes where useful decisions can happen. Domain owners can shape an agent-supported workflow because they understand the evidence, trade-offs, and failure costs. Central technology teams still provide shared controls and platforms, while fewer routine questions need to climb the hierarchy merely to find someone allowed to route them. From the centre, this distribution may look untidy. A clear domain map and common controls distinguish an empowered edge from unmanaged improvisation.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Every role becomes more technical when people must judge AI reliability and consequences in their own domain. The required capability is informed ownership, whether or not the role includes coding.</p>
</div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the hierarchy

Benjamin Simkin, an entrepreneur and author, calls this failure mode the “Retrofit Trap” in his 2026 book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5). His term describes bolting AI onto an old structure instead of redesigning the work around what the technology makes possible. It is a useful diagnosis; evidence for a particular redesign must still come from the operation itself.

Consider supplier onboarding. An agent may summarize a questionnaire in seconds, yet the workflow remains largely unchanged if employees still chase missing documents, copy facts between systems, decide who must approve, repair bad records, and notify the requester. One task became faster while the surrounding coordination survived.

A traditional hierarchy performs several jobs at once. It moves information, grants approval, routes exceptions, resolves trade-offs, coaches people, and owns consequences. Agent-supported workflows may absorb the first three when policy and evidence are clear. Human leaders remain necessary where goals conflict, rules do not settle the case, people need development, or someone must accept accountability. A flatter structure is justified only by the coordination that genuinely disappears.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Speeding up one activity leaves the operating model intact when people still route, approve, and repair the rest. The relevant unit of change is the complete workflow, not the impressive AI task.</p>
</div>

## Prove the whole workflow before removing a layer

The useful measure is a correctly closed case: an end-to-end piece of work that reaches a valid outcome with the required records, permissions, approvals, and communication intact. For supplier onboarding, that means more than a good summary. The supplier record must be accurate, missing evidence resolved, the right decision made, and every required action traceable.

Compare the same case mix before and during a bounded trial. Record elapsed time, human routing touches, repair effort, quality failures, control failures, total cost, and the decisions that still required human judgment. Preserve failed and stopped cases instead of averaging them away. Otherwise, visible automation can hide a new queue of people checking, reconciling, and apologizing after the agent moves on.

The evidence boundary matters. The source set used here consists of the linked recording and public book description, the NIST and IETF documents cited below, the Swedish statute cited later, and task-local searches of public Nordic and European Economic Area material. Within that set, there is no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a transferable numerical threshold for removing a management layer because of agents. Treat the following charter as a decision instrument rather than an industry benchmark.

### A one-page trial charter

Fill these fields before the trial begins:

- **Workflow and owner:** [complete workflow] / [accountable business owner]
- **Baseline and provenance:** [case mix, period, measures] / [source system and evidence owner]
- **Agent boundary:** [allowed steps] / [forbidden actions] / [required human decisions]
- **Trial limits:** [volume] / [duration] / [spend] / [staff capacity]
- **Acceptance conditions:** [quality, control, cost, routing, repair, and residual-judgment conditions]
- **Authority:** [approver] / [exception owner] / [person with stop and restart authority]
- **Review cadence:** [review dates] / [decision date] / [signatories]
- **Decision and reversal:** [stop, extend, redesign, or change structure] / [restoration path]
- **People commitment:** [participation, paid learning, redeployment, role and pay treatment, and exit support]

The executive or portfolio owner accountable for the trial should bring this record to each review with raw exceptions and evidence sources attached. Predeclared acceptance conditions prevent enthusiasm, sunk cost, or a strong demo from becoming the reason to redraw reporting lines.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Delayering needs evidence from correctly closed cases across a representative case mix. A signed trial charter makes the measures, limits, owners, review date, people commitments, and reversal path visible before results are known.</p>
</div>

## Keep authority outside the model

A flatter company still needs an architecture of authority. Map each important workflow to a domain owner who defines the outcome, a policy owner who sets the rules, a system owner who protects the official record, and a named leader who accepts the remaining risk. Then state who may propose, approve, execute, override, stop, and restart each consequential action.

The agent can interpret a supplier document and propose the next step. Ordinary control software should decide whether that step is allowed by checking the acting identity and the person or function on whose behalf it acts. It should separately check the permitted resource and action, the current case version, and any approval limit. Expiry or revocation of authority must block new actions, while the organization needs an explicit rule for work already in progress.

The company’s official record of the case is its authoritative state. Model output cannot silently change that record or grant itself permission. This matters because the US National Institute of Standards and Technology’s [July 2024 generative-AI risk profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf) describes “indirect prompt injection,” where hostile instructions are placed in material an AI application later retrieves. Text inside a supplier attachment may inform a proposal, but it must never expand the agent’s authority.

Repeated actions need similar care. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods), a June 2022 Internet Engineering Task Force standard for the web, defines an idempotent request as one where repeated identical requests are intended to have the same effect as one request. A unique action key can help prevent a simple duplicate, but it cannot erase uncertainty if an external action succeeds and the internal record fails to update. The workflow must reconcile with the external system before retrying and route an unresolved case to a named human.

Stop authority means the named right to pause new agent-triggered actions without waiting for the model or its supplier. Overrides, stops, restarts, and corrective actions should all enter an audit record with the evidence used, the policy applied, the actor, and the outcome. Supplier contracts should support that operating model through log export, evidence about data location, notice of material service changes, continuity provisions, and termination assistance.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Agents may interpret and propose, while ordinary software enforces identity, scope, state, limits, and revocation. Named humans retain exception, stop, restart, and corrective authority, with an audit path that survives supplier change.</p>
</div>

## Change the career system with the operating model

When routine routing and follow-up shrink, coordination-heavy roles will change. Some people can move into domain ownership, exception judgment, control design, coaching, or improvement work. Other roles may narrow or disappear. A responsible plan must address both outcomes rather than treating disruption as a communication problem.

The old pyramid also carried a career system. It offered titles, pay progression, management experience, access to senior decisions, and a visible path for ambition. If the company removes layers without replacing those functions, it may gain a faster workflow while weakening learning and leadership succession. Expert paths need comparable status and pay, managers need time to coach, and employees need paid learning tied to real future roles rather than a generic course catalogue.

In Sweden, this work also has a formal participation boundary. [Section 11 of the 1976 Co-Determination in the Workplace Act](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-1976580-om-medbestammande-i-arbetslivet_sfs-1976-580/) requires an employer bound by a collective agreement to initiate negotiation with the relevant employee organization before deciding on a more important change to operations or employment conditions. This rule is specific to Sweden; organizations elsewhere need to establish the applicable local process.

For a Swedish trial, the executive sponsor and human resources team should convene the relevant employee representatives while the workflow scope, role design, acceptance conditions, learning provision, and redeployment options remain open to influence. The transition budget belongs in the business case from the beginning, including paid learning, redeployment support, transparent treatment of progression and pay, and fair support where no continuing role exists.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A flatter structure changes careers as well as workflows. Participation must begin before the decision, and the business case must fund new paths, learning, redeployment, transparent pay treatment, and support for people who cannot move into a continuing role.</p>
</div>

## Make the org chart the last decision

For the chief information officer, the executive question is whether operating evidence justifies structural change. Before approving a new reporting model, the CIO should be able to show that complete cases close correctly, coordination and repair have fallen across the declared case mix, residual human judgment has a clear home, and total cost remains acceptable.

The same decision record should identify every consequential right and its owner, explain how permissions and authoritative state are enforced, show how the workflow stops and recovers, and demonstrate that the company can inspect its evidence and leave its supplier. It should also contain the negotiated workforce commitments, named funding, review dates, and the path back if the assumptions fail.

If those conditions hold, a flatter structure can be a responsible consequence of a better operating model. If they fail, keeping the existing layer demonstrates discipline. The company should repair the workflow, narrow the agent’s authority, or stop the trial before asking people to live inside an unproven architecture.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Change reporting lines only after the workflow, authority model, recovery path, supplier controls, and workforce transition are defensible together. The org chart should record a proven operating model instead of forcing one into existence.</p>
</div>
