An AI-first company begins with two linked claims. Every role develops a technical dimension, and ownership can move closer to the people doing the work as AI agents absorb more execution and coordination. Together, those shifts could support a materially flatter organization with more innovation from the edges.

Here, an AI agent means software that can interpret a goal, choose steps, and act through connected tools. An AI-first operating model redesigns complete workflows and decision rights around that capability. Adding a model to one task while preserving the surrounding queues, approvals, and repair work leaves the operating model largely untouched.

The flatter company is therefore a design hypothesis, not an automatic consequence of buying agents. A CIO should change the hierarchy only after local evidence shows which coordination work has disappeared, which human judgment remains, where authority now sits, and how affected people will move into the new structure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An AI-first company can distribute capability and ownership, but a flatter structure becomes defensible only when agents remove complete-workflow coordination and the remaining authority and people consequences are explicit.</p></div>

## What “technical” means outside technology

[Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), chief people officer at OpenAI, an AI research and deployment company, put the first claim plainly in [a 2026 recorded interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ): “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately qualified it by saying that the technical role is applied to a particular domain, meaning the area of work whose rules, customers, and outcomes a person understands. In the same interview, he argues that innovation should emerge from the bottom up, through people applying AI within their domains, rather than depend only on a central program.

That does not mean everyone must code. Technical judgment is the business ability to decide what AI can handle reliably, what evidence it needs, how failure will appear, and when a human must take over. Sales needs that judgment when generated terms affect a commercial promise, finance when documents support a payment, and marketing when generated claims carry legal or reputational consequences. Operations and support need it wherever an unusual case escapes the standard path.

A central AI team can provide shared tools, security controls, and evaluation methods, but it cannot own the meaning of every business outcome. That belongs with named domain owners who understand the work and can be held accountable for it. Bottom-up innovation becomes useful when local freedom is paired with a visible boundary and evidence of results.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical judgment is the ability to apply AI responsibly inside a business domain, not a requirement that every employee become a programmer; domain expertise and accountable ownership must move together.</p></div>

## Prove the workflow, then discuss the layers

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, calls the act of bolting AI onto an old structure the “Retrofit Trap.” It is a useful name for a common design error, although it is a business framing rather than proof of a universal outcome.

Consider a request to buy a non-standard software service. An agent might summarize supplier documents and draft a risk note in seconds. Yet people may still collect missing information, send the request between procurement and security, chase budget approval, enter the same facts into another system, and repair a purchase order created from stale data. In that design, faster analysis does not establish that the end-to-end workflow improved.

The unit of proof should be the complete case, from the employee’s initial request to an approved, rejected, or withdrawn purchase recorded in the right systems. A **correctly closed case** reaches that agreed outcome, satisfies the required controls, and leaves no unresolved side effect. The trial should compare a representative case sample with a frozen local baseline, then record elapsed time, human routing touches, repair effort, quality, control failures, residual judgment, and total cost for accepted and rejected attempts.

There is no universal number at which a management layer becomes safe to remove. The evidence assembled for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes such a threshold. Each company must predeclare its own acceptance and reversal conditions, then expose them to executive review. I would put the contract on one page before the trial starts:

| Decision field | What must be fixed before launch |
| --- | --- |
| Workflow boundary | One repeated workflow, its start and end, included case types, and explicit exclusions |
| Baseline and sample | A frozen local comparison, a case-selection rule, a named evaluator responsible for judging the outcome, and the evidence used to judge correct closure |
| Trial envelope | An end date, case and capacity limits, a spend cap, approved suppliers and model versions, and a fixed review forum |
| Ownership | Executive sponsor, domain owner, control owners, people-participation lead, evaluator, and the person with stop authority |
| Evidence | Correct closure, time, human touches, repairs, failed traces, control incidents, residual judgment, and total cost |
| Decision conditions | Predeclared reasons to expand, redesign, pause, or reverse the trial, plus the accountable decision owner |

These fields are a proposed decision record, not an established benchmark. Their purpose is to prevent a successful demonstration from quietly becoming an organizational conclusion.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Judge agents by correctly closed cases across the complete workflow, using a bounded local trial with a frozen baseline, named evaluators, cost and control evidence, and acceptance and reversal conditions set in advance.</p></div>

## Where authority goes when coordination shrinks

A hierarchy is partly a system for moving information, approvals, and exceptions. Managers gather status, compare requests with policy, resolve conflicts, and decide where unusual cases go next. They may also coach people, interpret uncertainty, and accept consequences on behalf of the organization. Agents can absorb parts of the first category without making the second disappear.

This is the mechanism behind a potentially flatter company. An agent can collect evidence, apply a routine rule, propose a next step, update a record, and route an exception without a person acting as the relay. A layer becomes structurally questionable only when that recurring relay work has genuinely gone and its remaining judgment has moved to an owner with enough time, skill, and authority to exercise it.

The replacement architecture needs a domain map and an authority map. The domain map names who owns the outcome and policy. The authority map states who may view, propose, approve, execute, override, and stop each consequential action. An AI model may interpret a purchase request and propose a security review, while ordinary software checks identity, permissions, the current case **state**, meaning its recorded position in the workflow, and the evidence required for the transition. If the request is based on an old case version or lacks a mandatory assessment, the software rejects the proposal, leaves the state unchanged, records the reason, and routes the case to a named human.

For consequential actions, the record should show the case and version, actor, required preconditions, permitted effect, execution result, next state, and accountable owner. It also needs a unique repetition key. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-9.2.2), the 2022 internet standard for web request semantics, calls an operation **idempotent** when repeating it has the same intended effect as performing it once. A repetition key applies that idea by helping software recognize a repeated command. However, it does not prove that an external payment, message, or supplier action happened exactly once, so the organization still needs reconciliation and, where an effect cannot be rolled back, a corrective step such as an adjustment or follow-up transaction.

Input trust belongs in the same design. **Prompt injection** is an instruction hidden in an email, document, or webpage that tries to redirect an AI model. Content supplied by a vendor or customer must never be able to raise an approval limit, grant access, or change a stop rule. Supplier contracts should make control testable through log export, evidence of data location, notice of model or service changes, failure notification, continuity arrangements, and help moving data and operations away at termination.

Finally, name the human who can stop the workflow and the conditions that trigger that decision. Overrides should require a reason and leave an audit trail, meaning a durable record of who did what and when. A rollback should restore a reversible software change to a known state; irreversible external effects need reconciliation and an owned recovery action. These visible limits are what allow domain teams to act without concealing responsibility.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents may interpret and propose, while ordinary software enforces permissions, state, and required evidence; named humans must own exceptions, overrides, stop authority, supplier controls, audit, and recovery.</p></div>

## The pyramid carried careers too

The old pyramid does more than route work. It provides coaching, status, pay progression, and a visible story about ambition. Removing a coordination layer can therefore erase a promotion step even when the new workflow performs well. Any serious redesign has to account for that loss before presenting flattening as empowerment.

Start by separating routine routing and repair from domain judgment, relationship work, coaching, and control ownership. Some tasks may pass to agents, some may move to specialists closer to the decision, and some will still need dedicated people leaders. The answer should come from the work map rather than an assumption that every coordination-heavy role is either obsolete or protected.

Employee participation should begin while the trial boundary is being drawn. A named people owner and participation lead should involve affected employees and their representatives in mapping hidden exceptions, testing new responsibilities, and reviewing role impacts under the applicable local labor rules. Those employees can identify unofficial repair work, so excluding them also weakens the operating evidence.

The transition needs money and decisions attached to it: paid learning time, credible redeployment routes, new role definitions, revised progression and pay criteria, and fair selection and exit support if roles disappear. A flatter company can still support ambition through broader domain responsibility, better decision quality, coaching, and control stewardship. Without a new career architecture, bottom-up ownership simply gives people more responsibility under a lower ceiling.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Flattening changes careers as well as workflow, so employee participation, learning, redeployment, progression, pay, coaching, and possible exits must be designed and funded as part of the operating trial.</p></div>

## What the board should authorize

The board’s question is whether a bounded operating trial justifies moving work, authority, and careers, regardless of how impressive the AI appears. The initial authorization should cover one workflow, the one-page evidence contract, the time and spend envelope, the control design, supplier obligations, employee participation, and the resources for transition. It should not approve a flatter org chart in advance.

At the review, the CIO should be able to answer five questions:

1. Did a representative case sample improve against the local baseline without hiding repair effort or rejected-attempt cost?
2. Which recurring coordination work disappeared, and which human judgment remains?
3. Did permissions, stale or repeated actions, hostile inputs, overrides, recovery, and supplier controls behave as designed?
4. Does each residual decision and exception have a named owner with the capacity to carry it?
5. Is the workforce plan funded, consulted, and credible for the people whose roles change?

A signed decision record should connect those answers to one of four choices: expand within the same boundary, redesign and test again, stop and reverse the trial, or change the operating structure. If the evidence supports the last choice, the company can move ownership outward and reshape the layer whose work has gone. If agents have only made isolated tasks faster, changing the hierarchy would hide the remaining coordination rather than remove it.

My recommendation is to approve the experiment against a written evidence-and-transition contract, then let the resulting work map determine the structure. This preserves the strategic possibility of a flatter, more bottom-up company while requiring the proof, visible authority, and human commitments needed to make the change defensible.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Authorize a bounded workflow trial, not a predetermined reorganization; change the structure only when complete-case evidence, visible residual ownership, working controls, supplier obligations, and a funded workforce transition support the decision.</p></div>
