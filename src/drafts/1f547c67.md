On 24 February 2026, [Arvind KC became Chief People Officer at OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), the AI company behind ChatGPT. The appointment is worth noticing because the most important AI-first decisions are no longer confined to model development. They now reach roles, authority, careers, and the shape of the company itself.

On 10 May, Benjamin Simkin published *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, an independently published field manual. Simkin gives the design problem a useful name: the “Retrofit Trap,” which means adding AI to processes built for manual human execution while leaving the underlying structure intact. The result may look modern without changing how work or decisions move.

The question I keep getting from founders and executives is what an AI-first organization looks like in practice. Who does what, how are decisions made, and why should the chart change? I think the answer rests on two claims: every role becomes technical, and some hierarchies become flatter because software can perform coordination work that once required management layers.

The causal link is an operating model I will call *agentic production*. An [AI agent](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/) is software that uses an AI model, instructions, data, and tools to carry out a multi-step task. Agentic production is a workflow in which agents execute routine work and coordinate handoffs while people set goals, own policy, and handle consequential exceptions. This can remove layers built mainly to relay information, but it does not make management disappear. It works only where the workflow is reliable enough to deserve that trust.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first design changes the org chart when agents can reliably perform routine work and route its exceptions. The resulting company needs broad technical literacy and fewer relay layers, while human authority remains essential.</p></div>

## The two claims

Simkin's Retrofit Trap explains why buying more tools is not the same as redesigning a company. If the old process still depends on the same handoffs, approvals, and information bottlenecks, a faster step inside that process leaves the architecture largely untouched. An AI-first design begins with the work that is now possible and builds the workflow around it.

That leads to two distinct changes. First, people throughout the company need enough technical understanding to judge how AI affects decisions in their domain. Second, fewer managers may be needed to collect updates and route routine approvals once agents can do that work reliably. I think companies get into trouble when they treat either claim as a slogan. A company with unstable workflows, poor data, or unclear authority will lose coordination if it removes a management layer before replacing the work that layer performs.

The claims are therefore conditional. Shared technical reasoning makes local judgment possible, while tested automation reduces the need for human relay points. Together they describe a flatter structure that follows from a better operating system rather than from a policy announcement.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Shared technical reasoning and automated coordination can support a flatter organization, but only after the old layer's real responsibilities have been identified and reassigned.</p></div>

## Every role becomes technical

A technical role does not require everyone to code or put engineers in every seat. It means decisions in sales, operations, finance, marketing, and support now depend on what an AI system can do, where it fails, and how its output should be checked. The person responsible for the decision needs to understand those limits, even when someone else builds the system.

The practical test is whether a domain leader can evaluate the work. I've watched this play out in organizations I advise on agentic production. A marketing team stalls when it cannot judge whether an AI-generated campaign brief reflects the strategy. An operations team cannot safely skip human review if it has no way to measure the accuracy of an agent's ticket classification. A finance team cannot choose well between a system that measurably reduces repeated reconciliation and a tool that has not changed cycle time, error rates, or cost.

This is a literacy rather than a coding skill. I think AI systems act as amplifiers because clear instructions, sound source material, and a useful standard for quality can spread through a workflow. Confused ownership and weak judgment spread just as easily, often behind a dashboard that looks like progress. Technical literacy lets each domain owner tell the difference.

The old organization treats technical people as a separate function that other teams request work from. The AI-first organization still needs specialists, but technical reasoning becomes a shared language for decisions. That change is what allows more authority to move closer to the work without turning local freedom into guesswork.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A technical role is one whose owner can judge how AI affects the decision in front of them. Coding is optional; judging capability, failure modes, and evidence of quality is part of the job.</p></div>

## Why some hierarchies flatten

One important function of middle management is coordination, but it is not the only one. Managers also coach people, allocate resources, resolve conflict, make personnel decisions, and carry accountability. The flattening argument applies most directly to layers whose main work is collecting status, translating between teams, and routing routine approvals.

Consider a customer-support workflow. An agent can receive a ticket, classify the issue, retrieve the customer's account history and contract terms, draft a response, and check it against policy. When a request stays within an approved refund limit and passes the company's quality checks, the agent can move it through the routine path. If the customer requests an exception, the source systems disagree, or the case raises a legal risk, the agent pauses and presents the evidence to the named support owner. That person still decides and remains accountable for the policy.

In the old process, a supervisor might assign the ticket, ask for status, collect the relevant context, and review a standard answer. Once an agent can perform those steps reliably and leave a record of what it did, that part of the supervisor's role can shrink. The role still matters if the same person coaches the team, handles sensitive cases, sets service policy, or resolves a conflict between support and product.

This boundary is what the flattening claim often leaves implicit. Before removing a layer, a leader needs a repeatable workflow with clear inputs, tested agent performance, access to the right systems, an auditable record, and a person who owns the exceptions. I think flattening should follow demonstrated reliability rather than serve as a starting principle.

The most useful frame from my own work is *domain-mapped ownership*. Each domain has one person who defines good output, sets operating limits, reviews exceptions, and can change the workflow. This avoids the Everybody Reviews Everything Trap, where feedback conflicts and nobody can make the final call. Agents can absorb much of the routing, but they cannot absorb accountability.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents reduce hierarchy when they take over proven coordination work, such as gathering context and routing standard cases. Coaching, policy, conflict resolution, and accountability still need human owners.</p></div>

## The compounding advantage

Once a domain expert can supervise an agent workflow, the role acquires a second layer. An *agent manager* is a person who can specify work for agents, check their results, and improve the workflow over time. The title matters less than the capability, because the work still requires real expertise in the domain.

An editor can use agents to prepare drafts, collect sources for claims, and flag repeated language before the final edit. A finance analyst can have agents pull and reconcile records, then surface anomalies for investigation. A customer-success lead can use them to triage cases and prepare account summaries, preserving more attention for relationship risk and difficult conversations. In each example, the agent handles repeatable preparation while the specialist remains responsible for judgment.

I think the advantage compounds only when saved effort improves the system that produced it. Human corrections need to change the instructions, source material, policies, or tests used by the workflow. If that learning stays in one person's head, the same errors recur at higher speed and the apparent gain is fragile.

I don't know a defensible productivity multiplier that applies across companies. The gain depends on task volume, the cost of mistakes, baseline quality, and how much human review remains. The strategic point is the direction of learning: a team that reinvests saved effort into a better system can improve faster than one that keeps adding isolated tools to the old process.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agent management can multiply a domain expert's capacity, and the gain compounds when human corrections improve the underlying workflow. The size of that gain must be measured in the work itself.</p></div>

## Distributed ownership needs governance

A flatter company will often look less tidy because more decisions happen close to the work. Leaders see more local movement and fewer requests for permission. That can be productive, but motion itself is not evidence that the design works.

The safeguard is explicit *decision rights*, meaning a written answer to who may decide what and when an issue must be escalated. Reversible decisions that stay within one domain, budget, quality standard, and policy boundary can usually sit close to the work. Company strategy, major capital allocation, security and privacy rules, legal obligations, cross-domain conflicts, and high-impact people or customer decisions still need central authority.

A leader can distinguish distributed ownership from confusion by looking for a named owner, a shared measure of success, a record of what the agents and people did, and an escalation path. If two teams change the same workflow in incompatible ways, agents follow conflicting policies, or exceptions bounce between people, ownership is missing. The response is to clarify the boundary and the final decision owner instead of pulling every routine choice back to the top.

Simkin's design question remains useful: *“If I were building this business today, from scratch, knowing what AI can do, would it look like this?”* Applied honestly, it can remove handoffs and work that survive mainly because they are familiar. The same exercise should preserve controls that still protect customers, employees, and the company. I think this is the discipline distributed ownership needs: leaders can tolerate local experimentation only when its risks remain visible.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Distributed ownership works when local authority has a named owner, measurable limits, a decision record, and an escalation path. Leaders should centralize shared risks and cross-company choices, not every routine action.</p></div>

## The architecture is the strategy

KC's appointment and Simkin's book bring the same underlying issue into view: the structure of an organization shapes what it can produce. What has changed is that an AI agent can now participate in a workflow instead of merely making one task faster. For repeatable digital work, the cost of maintaining several relay layers may eventually exceed the cost of redesigning the process. Ambiguous, regulated, or deeply human work will often keep more human coordination.

The harder question is what happens to the people whose coordination tasks shrink. People are not absorbed by agents; parts of their work are. Some managers can become domain owners or agent managers. Others may retrain, move elsewhere in the company, or leave. Organizations I have advised during AI-first reorganizations have handled this differently, but the human transition has been harder than the technical one.

A flatter organization also needs a different account of ambition. If there are fewer management layers, progression can no longer depend mainly on acquiring more direct reports. Companies will need credible paths through deeper craft, larger domain ownership, technical leadership, or responsibility that crosses domains. I don't have a clean answer for how many people can make that transition, and pretending otherwise would turn an organizational argument into a headcount slogan.

My take is to begin with one workflow. Draw the target process, name the human owner, automate the routine path, write the escalation rules, and measure quality before changing the reporting line. Then use what the team learns to redesign the next part of the company. The real test of AI-first leadership is whether you can change the chart while remaining accountable for the people and decisions it used to hold.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An AI-first redesign replaces routine relay work with tested agent workflows and redraws careers around ownership rather than layers. The structure is credible only when its authority and escalation paths account for the human consequences as carefully as the automation.</p></div>
