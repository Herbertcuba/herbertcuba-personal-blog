The organizational question is no longer whether employees will use AI. It is whether AI agents can remove enough execution and coordination from complete workflows to justify moving decisions closer to the work.

Two linked propositions sit underneath that question. Every business role acquires a technical dimension. If agents absorb routine coordination as well as individual tasks, the company can become flatter and ownership can move downward and outward. An AI agent here means software that can interpret a case, propose steps, and use approved tools within boundaries set by the organization.

I think that direction is plausible, but the org chart cannot be the starting assumption. Hierarchies route information, grant approvals, handle exceptions, develop people, and carry accountability. Agents may take over parts of the first three jobs. Leaders still have to prove which coordination has disappeared, keep consequential authority with named owners, and build a credible future for the people whose roles change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An AI-first architecture may distribute ownership and reduce layers, but only after workflow evidence, visible authority, and a funded people transition make that change defensible.</p></div>

## Technical judgment becomes a business capability

In a May 2026 interview, [Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), chief people officer at the artificial-intelligence company OpenAI, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately [qualified the statement](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ): the technical role is applied to a particular domain.

That qualification matters. Technical judgment does not mean that every salesperson, finance specialist, operations lead, marketer, or support agent must become a programmer. It means they need enough understanding to judge what an AI system can do reliably, where its output needs checking, what data and tools it may use, and when a person must make the decision.

A finance specialist might define the evidence required before a disputed charge can be approved. A support lead might decide which complaints can follow policy and which involve reputational or customer-harm questions. The business expert supplies the domain judgment; the agent can extend that person’s capacity only inside a clear operating boundary.

KC’s observation supports the capability claim. It does not establish that management layers can safely be removed. The second proposition, that distributed technical judgment can support more bottom-up ownership, remains an operating-model hypothesis. It becomes credible only when coordination actually leaves the workflow rather than landing as extra work on business specialists.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every role needs practical judgment about AI in its own domain, not coding ability. That distributed capability creates the possibility of bottom-up ownership, but it does not prove that the hierarchy can shrink.</p></div>

## The unit of redesign is the complete case

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls the act of bolting AI onto an old structure the “Retrofit Trap.” The trap is easy to enter because a faster task is visible while the old coordination around it is harder to see.

Consider an illustrative customer-refund case. An AI tool drafts a response, but a support employee still asks operations for evidence, sends the proposed amount to finance, waits for a manager’s approval, corrects mismatched records, and checks whether the payment reached the customer. The drafting task improved. The company still pays for the same routing, waiting, approval, and repair system.

A complete workflow starts when the request arrives and ends when the customer has the correct resolution, the financial action is reconciled, the company record is current, and any exception has a named owner. Traditional hierarchy often carries this flow by collecting information, approving departures from policy, and routing unusual cases. An agent-supported workflow may gather evidence, compare it with policy, propose an outcome, seek the right approval, carry an approved action into a business system, and update the case record.

Human authority remains where the organization must set policy, weigh a novel or harmful exception, resolve a contested decision, or accept accountability. Managers also coach people and handle conflict, neither of which should be disguised as routing work simply because both happen in the same role.

The useful evidence unit is therefore a **correctly closed case**: a case that reached the intended business outcome, followed policy, left an auditable record, and required no hidden repair after closure. Evaluate those cases for outcome quality, human routing touches, correction effort, control failures, elapsed time, full cost, and the judgment that still required a person. Full cost includes the model, integration, oversight, exception handling, fallback capacity, and the work needed to reconcile external systems.

These measures are a proposed local evidence contract, not established benchmarks. The research available for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer. Each organization must set its own scope, review period, risk limits, and evidence standard, then expose the actual result rather than a forecast.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Task speed does not justify structural change. Measure correctly closed cases across the full workflow, including routing, repairs, control failures, total cost, and the human judgment that remains.</p></div>

## Bottom-up ownership needs enforceable boundaries

Moving decisions closer to the work requires a visible governance model. Start by mapping each workflow to a domain, meaning a bounded area of business responsibility such as refunds for one product and market. Name the accountable business owner, the policy owner, the people allowed to approve each class of action, the exception owner, and the person with authority to stop the workflow.

The agent should interpret evidence and propose or execute permitted steps. Ordinary workflow software should enforce permissions and the **authoritative state**, the agreed current status of the case. In the refund example, the allowed states might move from received to evidence checked, proposed, approved, paid, and closed. The software should reject a move from proposed directly to paid when approval is required, regardless of how persuasive the agent’s explanation sounds.

That control needs a reusable event record. For every consequential step, retain the case identifier, human or agent actor, permission used, policy version, model and tool version, previous and new state, external action, outcome, override, and recovery evidence. Auditability then shows who or what acted under which rule; it is more than storing a transcript.

Retries require similar discipline. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-9.2.2), a 2022 standard for the behavior of web requests, defines **idempotency** as repeated identical requests having the same intended effect as one request. A request key, a unique identifier for an attempted action, can help prevent a retry from issuing the same refund twice. But a key stored inside the company cannot guarantee what an external payment provider did. Use the provider’s duplicate-protection feature where it exists; otherwise, a transactional outbox, a durable record of intended external actions, and a reconciliation process must compare the internal case with the external result before closure.

Override, stop, and recovery paths also need owners and exercises. Rollback means restoring a prior safe state, but a sent message or completed payment may not be reversible. In those cases, recovery requires a compensating action, such as a correction or repayment, plus an accountable person who decides it. This is what separates empowered edges from unmanaged ambiguity: local action is broad enough to matter, while its limits and consequences remain visible.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Agents may interpret and act within a domain, but ordinary software must enforce permissions and case state. Named people retain policy, exception, stop, override, and recovery authority, with an audit record that makes every consequential action inspectable.</p></div>

## A flatter company needs a new career contract

Organizational layers contain more than routine coordination, so removing a layer redistributes all of its work. Before changing roles, separate status collection and case routing from coaching, conflict resolution, policy judgment, capability building, and accountability. Agents may reduce the first category. The remaining work still needs time, skill, authority, and a named home.

If that work is quietly spread across already busy specialists, the company has reduced titles without reducing coordination. The new structure must therefore define how people receive coaching, how performance is judged, and where complex cross-domain decisions go. Domain ownership also needs a career path; otherwise, the company asks people to accept wider responsibility while management remains the only route to status and pay.

A credible people system offers progression through deep expertise, domain leadership, coaching, and broader business accountability. It funds learning during working time, states which capabilities qualify people for new roles, and makes redeployment decisions early enough to be real. Some roles will still contract or disappear. Where transition is not possible, the process should be transparent about timing, selection, support, and exit terms rather than hiding the consequence inside an automation program.

For a Nordic organization, employee participation belongs in the design stage. Sweden’s Co-Determination in the Workplace Act (1976:580), a national labor law governing employer-union participation, gives Swedish leaders a concrete reason to involve labor relations, counsel, and employee representatives before decisions are fixed. The exact duties depend on the situation, but the operating principle travels well: the people affected should be able to examine the role assumptions, evidence, and transition plan while those choices can still change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Removing coordination layers also removes career rungs, coaching capacity, and role identities. A defensible redesign gives the remaining human work an owner and funds progression, learning, participation, redeployment, and transparent exits.</p></div>

## Put three decisions in one record

The executive team needs one inspectable decision record rather than separate technology, risk, finance, and workforce narratives. The fields below are a recommended local contract, not a universal scorecard.

| Record field | What it must make visible |
| --- | --- |
| Workflow boundary | Start and end of the complete workflow, included and excluded cases, affected teams, and residual manual work. |
| Authority | Domain and policy owners, local decision limits, permissions, exception routes, override and stop owners, and prohibited actions. |
| Operating result | Actual correctly closed cases, quality, routing touches, repair effort, control failures, elapsed time, and remaining human judgment. |
| Delivery envelope | Budget owner, locally chosen cost cap, delivery capacity, review date, and actual model, integration, oversight, fallback, and transition costs. |
| Recovery | Tested fallback, external-system reconciliation, irreversible actions, compensating actions, and the owner of restoration after a failure. |
| People consequence | Tasks removed, work reassigned, role and pay effects, coaching capacity, learning and redeployment plan, employee participation, and funded exits. |
| Decision log | Evidence dates, approvers, unresolved conditions, production decision, organization decision, and the trigger for restoring capacity after a structural change. |

That record should pass through three distinct forums. First, the business workflow owner requests a bounded trial. The CIO’s technology, data, security, and risk owners approve the technical limits, while HR, labor relations, and employee representatives review the role assumptions. The output is a fixed scope, exclusions, budget, delivery capacity, stop conditions, and review date. No organizational change is approved at this stage.

Second, a steering committee decides whether to allow limited production. It reviews completed-case results against the local limits, verifies that the fallback and stop paths were exercised, and confirms who will handle unresolved judgment and external-system reconciliation. A successful demonstration is not enough; the record needs actual workflow outcomes and actual cost.

Third, the executive team, and the board where its governance role requires it, considers the organizational redesign. The business owner signs for the outcome, the CIO signs for controls and recovery, and the people leader signs for career paths, coaching capacity, participation, transition funding, and the conditions that would restore coordination capacity if performance deteriorates. Approval to run the workflow and approval to remove a layer remain separate decisions.

If the workflow improves individual tasks but leaves routing and repair intact, keep working on the operating model. If sustained evidence shows that coordination has genuinely left the system, the controls hold under exceptions, and the people commitments are funded, a flatter architecture becomes a defensible option. The CIO’s responsibility is to prevent the organization from surrendering authority faster than it learns to govern it, or dismantling a career system before the replacement is ready.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Authorize the trial, production workflow, and organizational redesign separately. One shared record should carry the evidence, owners, costs, controls, recovery tests, workforce commitments, approval dates, and conditions for restoring capacity.</p></div>
