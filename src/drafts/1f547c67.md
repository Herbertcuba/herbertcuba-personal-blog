An AI-first company makes two linked bets about organization. Every business role gains a technical dimension: people need enough judgment about AI to decide where it is dependable, where it is brittle, and when a human should take over. At the same time, AI agents, meaning generative AI systems that can interpret a goal, choose steps, and use approved software tools, can absorb enough routine execution and coordination to support a flatter structure with more ownership close to the work.

Neither bet justifies an immediate reorganization. Distributed technical judgment can leave employees pushing faster outputs through the same approvals and handoffs. A flatter chart can remove managers while leaving their routing, exception handling, coaching, and accountability work untouched. I think the flatter-company thesis is credible, but the org chart should be the last artifact to change.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>An AI-first operating model combines technical judgment in every function with agents that remove real coordination work. A flatter organization is a result to earn through evidence, governance, and a workable people transition.</p>
</div>

## Every role gains a technical dimension

During a recorded DECODE interview about OpenAI, an AI research and product company, people leader Arvind KC put the first proposition sharply: “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He then explained that the technology is applied to a domain. The practical meaning is broader than coding.

KC also described innovation at OpenAI as bottom-up. That observation connects the two originating claims: when technical judgment sits inside a business domain, people close to the work can shape useful changes instead of waiting for a central technology group to prescribe each one.

Technical judgment is the ability to reason about what a system can do reliably, the evidence it needs, the damage an error could cause, and the point at which a person must decide. A salesperson may use AI to prepare an account brief but still own the commercial judgment behind a concession. A finance team may accept suggested classifications while requiring evidence and approval for a payment. A support specialist may let an agent retrieve policy and draft a response, then take over when the facts or customer impact are ambiguous.

The boundary matters because AI performance depends on the task. A Harvard Business School summary of an experiment with 758 consultants reported gains on work inside the model’s “capability frontier,” the set of tasks it could handle well, but lower correctness on a task outside that boundary. Technical judgment helps a function recognize that a confident answer is not proof that the work sits inside the frontier.

This distributes responsibility as well as capability. Technology teams still provide platforms and controls, but domain experts decide what good work looks like and where an exception carries business meaning.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Making every role more technical means spreading judgment about AI’s limits and business consequences, not turning every employee into a programmer. Domain expertise makes bottom-up innovation useful and governable.</p>
</div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the old company

In his business book *The AI First Company*, Benjamin Simkin calls the habit of adding AI to old processes the “Retrofit Trap.” The term captures a common design failure: one task becomes faster while the surrounding work remains organized around manual routing, approval, and repair.

Consider a customer refund. An agent may draft the reply in seconds, yet an employee still copies order data into another system, asks a manager to interpret policy, waits for finance approval, sends the message, and repairs the case record afterward. The visible task improved. The company still pays for the same coordination system, with an extra model and review step added to it.

An AI-first operating model therefore has to be evaluated across a complete workflow: the path from a valid trigger to a correctly closed case, including exceptions, approvals, records, and downstream effects. Faster text or analysis matters only if it helps that workflow close with fewer unnecessary human touches, less repair, and at least the required level of quality and control.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up an isolated task without changing the operating model around it. The useful unit of redesign is the complete, correctly closed workflow.</p>
</div>

## Hierarchy is also a coordination system

A hierarchy does more than distribute power. It carries information upward, turns policy into approvals, reconciles competing priorities, and routes unusual cases to someone with authority. Many management roles bundle that coordination with distinct human work such as coaching, resource choices, conflict resolution, and accountability.

An agent-supported workflow can absorb part of the coordination bundle. It can gather case context, compare facts with an approved policy, propose a next action, execute routine steps within a limit, update records, and route a genuine exception to its owner. When this works, people closest to the domain need fewer relays between insight and action.

The rest of the bundle does not disappear with the routing. A person still has to own policy changes, trade-offs between business goals, the allocation of scarce resources, and decisions whose consequences the organization must defend. Agents can prepare those decisions, but they should not become a convenient place to hide human authority.

That distinction makes flattening conditional. A layer can become unnecessary when its recurring coordination work has been removed or reassigned clearly. If the same work moves into informal chats, shadow review queues, or late-stage repair, the organization has only made its hierarchy harder to see.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Agents may remove information routing, routine approvals, and case administration, but management also contains judgment, coaching, and accountability. Separate those jobs before deciding that a layer is redundant.</p>
</div>

## Flattening has to be earned by the workflow

A pilot should be measured from trigger to stable closure, not at the moment an agent produces an answer. For each workflow, I would ask for evidence on six dimensions:

- **Correct closure:** the share of valid cases completed with the intended business result and record state.
- **Human coordination:** routing touches, approval touches, and waiting time created by handoffs.
- **Repair effort:** human time spent correcting outputs, recovering failed actions, and reconciling systems.
- **Quality and control:** customer harm, policy breaches, security failures, and other material defects.
- **Total cost:** models, software integration, review, support, incident handling, and the people still required.
- **Residual judgment:** the cases that continue to need interpretation, negotiation, or accountable choice.

This is a proposed evidence contract, not an established industry benchmark. The available evidence provides no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a numerical threshold for safely removing management layers. A board paper should not manufacture one.

The evidence may support a structural change in one domain and reject it in another. Routine, reversible work with clear policy can justify greater autonomy sooner than high-impact work with ambiguous facts. That uneven result is useful because it replaces a company-wide slogan with a domain-by-domain operating decision.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Judge agents by correctly closed workflows, including human routing, repair, quality failures, total cost, and remaining judgment. Use domain evidence rather than an invented universal threshold to decide whether fewer layers are justified.</p>
</div>

## The replacement architecture makes authority visible

Removing a coordination layer creates a gap unless ownership and decision rights become more explicit. Domain-mapped ownership assigns each bounded area of work, such as refunds or supplier onboarding, to a named person responsible for its outcome, policy, quality, and exceptions. Decision rights then specify what an agent may observe, recommend, or execute; which limits apply; and who decides when a case crosses them.

Ordinary software should enforce those boundaries. It should verify identity and permissions, maintain the authoritative case state, and provide idempotency, which means a retry cannot accidentally create a second payment, order, or ticket. The case state is the definitive record of where the work currently stands. Generative models choose responses from learned patterns, so their output can vary. That variability is why a prompt should not serve as the permission system or the case record.

The agent can interpret an email, retrieve approved context, prepare an action, and call a permitted tool. The surrounding software checks whether the action is allowed, records what changed, and blocks invalid transitions. Named owners need an auditable path to override a recommendation, stop the workflow, and roll back a reversible action. Stop authority is the right to pause further action; rollback is a tested way to reverse a committed change when that is safe. Auditability means the organization can reconstruct what happened, under whose authority, and with which result.

This architecture can look less orderly from the center because more decisions happen within domains. In practice, it is governed bottom-up ownership: local freedom inside visible boundaries, with central control over identity, policy, risk, and evidence.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A flatter model needs named domain owners, explicit decision rights, software-enforced permissions and case state, and auditable routes for override, stop, and rollback. Distributed execution must make authority clearer rather than conceal it.</p>
</div>

## A flatter company needs a new people contract

The old pyramid did more than route information. It also supplied status, pay progression, mentoring, learning opportunities, and a visible path toward greater responsibility. If layers shrink, those functions need a replacement rather than an optimistic assumption that employees will adapt around the gap. Employees still need somewhere for ambition to go.

Start by separating tasks from roles. Some people in coordination-heavy positions may move toward domain ownership, coaching, quality control, or designing how unusual cases are handled. Others may face a substantial loss of responsibility or of the role itself. A responsible transition has to acknowledge that some changes will hurt and that displacement is a real possibility.

The people plan needs to identify affected tasks and roles before the reorganization. It should establish paid learning with time to practise, transparent criteria for new roles, expert career paths that do not depend on team size, and pay that recognizes judgment and domain responsibility. It also needs named owners for coaching, routes for redeployment, early involvement of employee representatives, and fair terms where a move is not possible.

This work belongs in the operating-model decision, not in the communications plan that follows it. Otherwise the company removes a familiar career system before it has built a credible new one, which weakens both trust and the distributed ownership the redesign was meant to create.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Flatter structures remove parts of the career and learning system as well as coordination. Leaders need funded transitions, new expert paths, explicit coaching ownership, honest redeployment choices, and fair treatment where roles disappear.</p>
</div>

## Make the decision before changing the chart

Before changing reporting lines, a CIO should demand three commitments. The business and domain owner should sign off on complete-workflow evidence across routine cases and exceptions. Technology and risk owners should sign off on permissions, case state, decision rights, audit, override, stop, and rollback. Business and HR leaders should sign off on the role map, career architecture, learning investment, redeployment process, and treatment of people whose jobs materially change.

The executive discussion should distinguish what has been demonstrated from what remains a hypothesis. Task-level productivity evidence must be followed through to end-to-end cost. Routine performance also needs a named owner for rare, high-impact exceptions, while the people case must show how coaching and progression will survive a flatter structure.

When those questions have good answers, fewer coordination layers can follow logically from redesigned work. Where they do not, the honest decision is to keep the structure while improving the workflow and controls. An AI-first architecture becomes defensible when technical judgment reaches the business edges, authority stays visible, and the organization is prepared to carry the human consequences of its design.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Change the org chart only after workflow evidence, accountable control design, and a funded people transition have named owners and survived executive scrutiny. The goal is a defensible operating model, not flatness for its own sake.</p>
</div>
