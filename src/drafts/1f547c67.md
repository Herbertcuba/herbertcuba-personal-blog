Two claims belong at the center of an AI-first operating model. Every business role is acquiring a technical dimension, and companies may become flatter as more execution and coordination move into software. Together, those shifts allow more ownership at the edges of the organization.

An AI agent is software that uses a generative model to interpret context and carry out a sequence of actions toward a goal. Agents can gather information, apply policy, update systems, and route exceptions. Yet that possibility is not evidence that a management layer can safely be removed.

I think the direction is credible. The org chart should still change last, after the workflow evidence, authority model, and consequences for people are visible. Otherwise, a company can automate fragments while quietly transferring more repair, risk, and accountability to the humans who remain.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first design joins two shifts: technical judgment spreads across business roles, and reliable agent-supported workflows may move ownership closer to each domain. A flatter structure becomes defensible only after coordination work has disappeared under visible controls and with a credible people transition.</p></div>

## Technical judgment belongs in every function

Arvind KC, a people leader at OpenAI, the company that develops generative AI systems, stated the first claim in a recorded DECODE interview: “Every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He then explained that the technical role is applied to a domain, meaning a bounded area of business responsibility such as sales, finance, or support.

That does not turn everyone into a programmer. Technical judgment is the ability to reason about what an AI system can do reliably, what information and permissions it needs, how success will be checked, and when a person must decide. The domain expert remains responsible for the meaning of the work.

A salesperson may decide which product claims an agent can draft and which require approval. Finance must separate a routine reconciliation from an exception with policy or fraud implications. Marketing has to verify claims and content rights before release, while support defines when a case calls for a human. Operations owns how those decisions connect across the complete process.

This is how bottom-up ownership becomes practical. People close to a domain can shape its agent-supported workflows instead of sending every useful change through a central technical team. Central technology still provides common infrastructure and controls, but it no longer owns every idea.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical judgment means understanding AI capability, limits, evidence, and escalation inside a business domain. The necessary skill is accountable reasoning rather than universal coding. When that capability sits with domain experts, useful changes can originate closer to the work without abandoning shared enterprise controls.</p></div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the old company

Benjamin Simkin, author of the management book *The AI First Company*, calls a common failure the “Retrofit Trap”: adding AI to isolated tasks while leaving the surrounding operating model intact. One visible step becomes faster, but the same handoffs, approvals, queues, and repair work continue around it.

Consider a customer refund. An agent might read the complaint and draft a response in seconds. If a supervisor still classifies the case, another team checks eligibility, finance approves the amount, and an operator repairs the status after a failed update, the company has accelerated a task without redesigning the workflow.

A complete workflow is the entire path from a valid request to a correctly closed case, including exceptions and control checks. That unit matters because hierarchy has long served as an information and coordination system: managers collect context, allocate authority, settle ambiguity, approve actions, and route exceptions. Agents may absorb some of that work. Policy-setting, coaching, consequential trade-offs, and accountability still need clear human owners.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Retrofit Trap makes one AI-assisted task look like an operating-model change. A genuine AI-first workflow redesigns the surrounding routing, approval, state updates, and repair work while preserving human ownership of policy, consequential exceptions, and accountability.</p></div>

## A flatter company has to be earned

Task productivity is too narrow a basis for organization design. In the working paper *Generative AI at Work*, distributed by the National Bureau of Economic Research, a US organization that publishes economics research, researchers studied 5,179 customer-support agents and found a 14 percent average productivity improvement. The authors also warned that the study could not establish effects on aggregate employment or wages. Faster work and evidence for removing a management layer are different findings.

For an organizational decision, I would ask for a workflow evidence pack with four views:

- **Correctly closed cases:** cases that reach the intended outcome without an undiscovered error, measured with service quality and control failures.
- **Human work:** routing touches, review time, exception handling, and effort spent repairing AI or system errors.
- **Total cost:** model use, integration, human review, incident handling, and the conventional systems that keep the workflow safe.
- **Residual judgment:** decisions that remain after routine execution and coordination have been removed, along with the people accountable for them.

If managers still resolve novel exceptions, coach teams, negotiate priorities, or carry risk that the workflow cannot hold, deleting the role merely hides its work elsewhere. The evidence here also supplies no Swedish or Norwegian production threshold for safe flattening. This scorecard is a proposed decision method, not a benchmark; each company needs its own baseline and criteria agreed before a pilot begins.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Evidence for a flatter structure must cover complete, correctly closed workflows rather than faster tasks. Measure human work, quality, control failures, total cost, and residual judgment against the company’s own baseline; no universal or Nordic threshold in the available evidence makes the decision automatically safe.</p></div>

## Put authority outside the model

Once a workflow earns broader use, the replacement architecture has to make ownership more visible than the hierarchy it changes. Start with domain-mapped ownership: a written map of each business area, its accountable owner, its data, and its outcomes. Then define decision rights, which specify who may decide, approve, override, or stop each class of action.

Conventional software should enforce identity, permissions, financial limits, and workflow state, the authoritative record of where a case stands. It should also enforce idempotency, the rule that retrying the same action cannot create a second refund or duplicate order. A generative model’s flexibility helps with messy language and incomplete context, but it is a weak substitute for hard access and transaction controls.

Within those boundaries, an agent can interpret a request, retrieve policy, propose an action, and execute what its permission allows. A named human handles defined exceptions and retains override, stop, and rollback authority. Override changes an action through an authorized path; stop authority halts further processing; rollback restores a known safe state when that is technically and legally possible.

Auditability means keeping a record of the inputs, policy version, proposed action, approvals, system changes, and responsible person or service. In the refund example, the agent may interpret the complaint, conventional software verifies eligibility and prevents duplication, and a business owner controls exceptional amounts and can suspend the workflow. More decisions can then happen inside domains without making authority invisible.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A governed AI-first architecture gives domains clear owners and decision rights. Conventional software enforces permissions, current state, limits, and safe retries; agents interpret and act within those boundaries; named humans retain auditable authority to handle exceptions, override actions, stop processing, and roll back where possible.</p></div>

## The pyramid carries more than approvals

A management pyramid is also a career and learning system. Promotion into management often brings progression, pay, status, and a larger field for ambition. Managers may coach less experienced colleagues, resolve conflict, translate strategy, and carry knowledge about exceptions that was never written into a process.

When agent-supported workflows reduce coordination-heavy roles, those functions need a new home. A flatter company needs expert career tracks with credible progression and pay, named coaching responsibility, protected learning time, and routes into domain ownership, workflow design, quality evaluation, or relationship leadership. Renaming a coordinator as a strategist changes little unless the work and authority change too.

Some people can move into new roles; others may face role loss. Responsible leadership requires transparent criteria, applicable employee consultation, realistic training and redeployment where a credible path exists, and honest terms where it does not. A hidden destination gives people a reason to protect themselves rather than expose the workflow failures the company needs to see.

Before removing a layer, map the coaching, judgment, conflict resolution, and practical knowledge it carries. Decide which responsibilities move to domain owners, which become explicit roles, and which the organization is consciously choosing to lose. The human transition is part of the architecture.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Flatter structures remove more than approval steps: they disturb careers, pay, coaching, learning, and stores of practical knowledge. The replacement model needs credible expert progression, explicit homes for human leadership work, and a transparent transition for people whose coordination-heavy roles genuinely shrink.</p></div>

## Change the org chart last

Before taking a structural proposal to the board, a CIO should bring three connected commitments. Workflow evidence must show which complete blocks of coordination disappeared and at what quality, risk, and total cost. A control map must name owners for permissions, exceptions, overrides, stop decisions, audit records, and rollback. A workforce plan must state which roles change, how careers and pay will work, and what support or terms affected people will receive.

The proposal should answer three questions in plain language:

- Which complete workflow now closes correctly with fewer human routing and repair touches?
- Who can approve an exception, inspect the record, override an action, stop the system, and restore a safe state?
- What credible path exists for the people whose work, progression, or role is changed by the design?

The defining question is where judgment and authority sit, not how much AI the company has purchased. A materially flatter organization becomes possible when agents absorb real coordination and domain experts can own more at the edges. It is an outcome to earn through operating evidence, controlled authority, and explicit responsibility for the people inside the change. Until those conditions hold, the hierarchy may still be doing necessary work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Take an organizational redesign to the board only with complete-workflow evidence, a named control and decision-rights map, and a funded workforce transition. If the company cannot show what coordination disappeared, who retains stop authority, and what happens to affected people, it has not yet earned the flatter org chart.</p></div>
