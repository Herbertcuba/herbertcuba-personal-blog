Every business role is acquiring a technical dimension. At the same time, AI agents may allow more decisions to move toward the people closest to the work, making the organization flatter and more bottom-up.

[Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), chief people officer at OpenAI, the artificial-intelligence company that develops the ChatGPT conversational service, gave the first claim a sharp formulation. In a [June 2026 interview with the business-interview channel DECODE](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s), he said every role is becoming “fundamentally a technical role,” with the technology applied to a particular domain. KC has also [described OpenAI](https://www.linkedin.com/in/arvindkc/) as designed for “bottom-up innovation.” That describes one unusual AI company. It does not prove that the same structure will work in a bank, manufacturer, or public agency.

Still, the two ideas belong together. I use *AI agent* here to mean software built around a generative AI model, a system that can interpret and produce language, which can pursue a defined goal through approved business tools. If agents absorb enough routine execution and coordination, fewer decisions need to travel up and down a management chain. A responsible CIO should treat that as a possibility to be earned through operating evidence, explicit authority, controlled recovery, and a credible plan for the people affected.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Distributed technical judgment and flatter structures are linked possibilities. Neither justifies an organizational redesign until complete workflows, controls, and workforce consequences have been tested.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment belongs inside the role

Technical judgment does not mean that every salesperson, finance partner, marketer, support specialist, or operations lead must become a programmer. It means they can judge what an AI system can do reliably, what evidence it needs, where it tends to fail, and when a decision still belongs to a person. The technical dimension sits inside the business role rather than beside it in a specialist team.

Consider a customer-contract renewal. The account owner should know which terms an agent may extract, which standard options it may prepare, and which unusual concession requires legal, financial, or relationship judgment. A central AI team can provide safe components and shared controls, but it cannot own the commercial meaning of every exception.

This is why domain ownership matters. A domain is simply an area of business responsibility, such as renewals or service recovery. The people who understand that domain should help define acceptable outcomes, failure cases, and escalation rules. Bottom-up ownership then means bounded authority near the work, not a free-for-all in which everyone chooses their own tools and risk limits.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Every role needs enough technical judgment to set useful boundaries for AI inside its own domain. Coding is optional; understanding capability, failure, evidence, and escalation is not.</p>
</div>

## A faster task does not remove a layer

[Benjamin Simkin](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), author of the 2026 business book *The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*, calls a common failure the **Retrofit Trap**. The term describes adding AI to a business process while leaving the surrounding manual structure intact.

An agent might draft a renewal offer in seconds. Yet the account owner may still chase service data, ask a manager for a margin exception, wait for legal review, copy details into the customer system, and repair an incorrect clause. The generated document became faster while the workflow remained full of routing and correction. That is useful automation, but it is weak evidence for removing a coordination layer.

A hierarchy does more than define reporting lines. It moves information, approves commitments, resolves exceptions, reconciles competing goals, and notices when standard rules no longer fit. Agents can absorb parts of that load by assembling case history, applying routine policy, routing known exceptions, and keeping participants informed. Human authority remains necessary for novel trade-offs, changes to risk appetite, employment consequences, and accountability for commitments the company makes.

The evidence therefore has to follow the case from request to resolution. I call the unit a *correctly closed case*: an outcome completed to the required quality, within policy, with its records updated and no hidden cleanup left behind. A company-set evaluation should compare a representative mix of ordinary and difficult cases, then track human routing touches, repair effort, quality, control failures, full operating cost, and the judgment still required. This is a proposed management test, not an established benchmark.

The available task evidence contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a numerical threshold for safely removing a management layer. That gap matters. A CIO can define a local evidence window and acceptance conditions, but should not present them as a universal rule.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Flattening becomes defensible only when whole cases close correctly with less routing, repair, and exception handling over a declared period. Faster output from one task is not enough.</p>
</div>

## Authority must become more visible

When a coordination layer shrinks, its authority cannot disappear into an agent. The replacement architecture needs domain-mapped ownership and decision rights that say who may propose, approve, override, and stop each consequential action. A risk tier, meaning the company’s own classification of the harm a workflow could cause, should determine how much human approval and independent review it receives.

The agent can interpret an unstructured renewal request and propose a next step. Company-owned, rule-based software should remain the system of record, the official place that holds the case’s authoritative state, meaning what has actually been approved and committed. That software enforces identity, permissions, policy limits, and the current case version. The language model should never decide whether its own permission is valid.

Before a binding offer leaves the company, the release system should recheck the current version, the approver’s authority, and the applicable limit. It should also record a stable request identifier and reject the same request if it arrives again, a property engineers call *idempotency*. The duplicate check and official state change need to happen together at the point of release, or the action should fail. This prevents a stale or repeated proposal from creating a second commitment.

Every consequential action needs an audit trail, and a named human needs stop authority: both the right and the practical mechanism to halt new actions. The same design should name who can override a recommendation, who investigates failed controls, and who authorizes restart. Auditability is useful only when someone is accountable for reading the record and acting on it.

Recovery also needs precise language. *Rollback* can return workflow rules or software to an earlier safe version. If a customer has already accepted a binding renewal, the code cannot erase that commitment; the contract owner may need a *compensating action*, such as a documented amendment that corrects the external effect. Both the failure and the correction belong in the audit record.

This control core should survive a supplier change. Business policy, authoritative case state, permissions, and audit records remain under company control; model access is limited to the data required for the task; and the workflow has an exit path to another model or a manual mode. Apparent decentralization is fragile when a vendor owns the practical ability to understand, stop, or recover the work.

The mapping is precise: an agent-supported company can look decentralized while real authority remains buried in vendor settings, approval code, or inaccessible model instructions. A defensible design keeps those boundaries visible, auditable, company-owned, and changeable by named people.

## Redesign the career system before the chart

Management layers carry a career system as well as a coordination system. They provide status, pay progression, coaching, access to decisions, and a familiar route for ambition. If routine routing and follow-up decline, the company cannot simply remove boxes and assume that empowerment will fill the space.

Start by separating the work inside coordination-heavy roles. Routine forwarding, status collection, and predictable repair may shrink. Judgment about people, conflicting goals, unusual exceptions, and accountability may become more valuable. Some employees can move toward domain leadership, coaching, control ownership, or difficult-case work. Other roles may narrow or disappear, and a credible plan must say how redeployment, learning support, selection, and any remaining reductions will be handled.

The new model also needs progression without headcount ownership. Expert paths require defined levels, decision scope, pay ranges, learning time, and recognized coaching responsibility. Otherwise the company asks people to carry more distributed accountability while preserving promotion rules built for the old pyramid.

For a Nordic organization, employee participation should be a formal part of the operating rhythm. Employee representatives should be involved before a trial changes work design, and again before trial evidence is used to change roles, pay, or reporting lines. Applicable legal duties still require local review, but participation should not wait for the final restructuring announcement.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The hierarchy is also a system for careers, coaching, pay, and voice. Those structures must be redesigned with employees before coordination roles are removed, not repaired after the fact.</p>
</div>

## Put the proof on one page

The first executive decision should concern a bounded workflow trial, not a new org chart. A candidate workflow should have a named owner, a measurable closed outcome, enough past cases to test ordinary and difficult conditions, clear data boundaries, and a recovery path. Work with irreversible or poorly understood effects should not enter merely because it offers a dramatic demonstration.

Use a one-page decision record with company-set terms:

- **Scope and ownership:** the workflow, funder, domain owner, technology owner, control owner, evidence owner, and executive sponsor.
- **Evidence plan:** the baseline, representative case mix, review period and date, quality conditions, permitted human involvement, and the coordination or repair work expected to fall.
- **Economic boundary:** a cost ceiling and full-cost view covering models, integration, monitoring, human review, exception handling, and supplier charges.
- **Authority and participation:** the risk tier, permissions, exception owner, override and stop conditions, required signatories, and the two workforce-participation checkpoints.
- **Recovery and decision:** a tested rollback for workflow changes, a tested path for correcting completed external effects, and the choices to expand, narrow, move into normal operations, or stop.

At review, look for a sustained reduction in coordination and exception load without that work resurfacing as frontline cleanup, risk work, or informal supervision. Keep quality and control failures within the acceptance conditions set before the trial, and make the evidence owner independent enough to challenge the workflow owner. Structural change should be a separate decision after the operating result holds for the declared period.

Ask the board to authorize a reversible trial with a written evidence contract and workforce commitments. Return to the structure only when the work has genuinely changed and the authority around it remains enforceable. At that point, a flatter chart records an operating reality. Before that, it is an unsupported promise.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A CIO should demand a bounded, costed, time-boxed trial with agreed evidence, controls, recovery, signatories, and people commitments. The org chart changes only after those terms show that coordination has durably left the workflow.</p>
</div>
