Two claims define the architecture of an AI-first company, an operating model built around what artificial intelligence can reliably do across a complete workflow. Every role acquires a technical dimension. And when AI agents, systems that can use approved tools across several steps, absorb enough routine execution and coordination, ownership can move toward the people closest to the work and the organization can become materially flatter.

The claims belong together. Local ownership fails when people cannot judge the systems acting for them, while a flatter chart fails when the old approval and exception work merely moves into hidden queues. I think the direction is right, but the leadership standard has to be higher than a convincing demonstration.

A language model is software trained to interpret and produce language. One common form of agent combines such a model with instructions and approved tools, then works through several steps toward a goal. [OpenAI's Agents SDK](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/), a developer toolkit from the company that makes the ChatGPT AI assistant, documents one version of this pattern, including pauses for human approval. Organization design enters when the system can carry the complete flow of work while people remain accountable for the judgment it cannot make.

The cited sources do not show companies removing management layers safely at scale. That evidence boundary matters. The question for a CIO is therefore conditional: what must the work prove, where must authority sit, and what must happen for employees before a new organizational shape becomes defensible?

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>An AI-first company can distribute technical judgment and become flatter, but only when agents remove coordination across complete workflows and the replacement has visible authority, operating proof, and a credible human transition.</p></div>

## Technical judgment belongs close to the work

Arvind KC, OpenAI's chief people officer, told the founder community DECODE in a [May 2026 discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s) that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role,” with that technical role applied to a particular domain. Here, a domain is a bounded area of business work with its own outcome and expertise. Programming is only one form of technical work. Across commercial and support functions, as well as operations and finance, the practical demand is to judge what an AI system can do reliably, what evidence it uses, and how failures become visible.

A finance owner, for example, needs to know whether an agent is reading the authoritative figures, which approvals it may request, and when uncertainty must stop the workflow. A customer-operations owner needs to define which cases may proceed automatically and which require human discretion. Engineers still own dependable tools, access controls, and system behavior, but they cannot decide the business meaning of every exception.

This leads to domain-mapped ownership. Each area gets a named owner who defines what good looks like, including the relevant data and rules as well as its risks and exceptions. The central technology function supplies shared foundations and specialist support. If every unusual case still travels to that function for interpretation, the company has recreated a hierarchy around technical knowledge.

KC has also described OpenAI as designed for bottom-up innovation, even when the surface can look chaotic. I think the important part is the location of judgment, not the appearance of disorder. Decisions can move closer to context only when the people at that edge understand the system and hold explicit authority over the domain.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Technical judgment means understanding capability and evidence, including how failure surfaces and when people must exercise discretion. Domain owners need that judgment close to the work, while the technology function provides reliable foundations rather than becoming a new approval queue.</p></div>

## The Retrofit Trap preserves the pyramid

Benjamin Simkin's independently published May 2026 book, *[The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy](https://www.amazon.com/AI-First-Company-Engineering-Business/dp/B0H45WZRX5)*, calls a common failure the Retrofit Trap. A company makes one task faster with AI but leaves the surrounding manual process intact. The output arrives sooner, yet people still collect missing information, route it for approval, repair mistakes, and record the final decision elsewhere.

Consider supplier onboarding. An AI system might summarize a supplier questionnaire in seconds, while procurement still chases documents by email, information security reviews every case, finance re-enters approved details, and managers resolve ownership through meetings. The summary improved. The operating model did not.

A complete agent-supported workflow would carry the case from request to a correct close. It could request missing evidence, compare the submission with policy, recommend the standard route for its risk, assemble the record for a named exception owner, and update approved systems after the required decision. The agent handles interpretation and routine movement; ordinary software and accountable people enforce the boundaries.

This is where the hierarchy enters the mechanism. A traditional management structure does more than supervise. It aggregates information, directs approvals, routes exceptions, allocates scarce resources, coaches people, and carries accountability. Agents may absorb some information movement and routine routing. They do not automatically replace conflict resolution, people development, risk acceptance, or the final responsibility for a business outcome.

I think flattening becomes plausible only after those functions are separated. A coordination-heavy role may shrink when the workflow removes the routing burden that sustained it. A management responsibility that still matters needs a visible owner, even if that owner no longer sits in the same layer.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up an isolated task while preserving the queues around it. Organizational change begins only when an agent-supported workflow removes routine coordination across the full case and the remaining management responsibilities have named owners.</p></div>

## Prove that coordination has actually gone

The right unit of evidence is a correctly closed case: a complete unit of work that reached the right business outcome with required permissions and records, while every required control remained intact. In supplier onboarding, a polished risk summary does not count if a person still finds the documents, repairs the record, or creates the supplier manually after the system says the case is finished.

The trial needs three matched designs. Compare the current process, the strongest conventional workflow-automation alternative, and the agent-supported design using the same mix of ordinary and exceptional cases, outcome standard, time period, and definition of total cost. Conventional automation means defined rules and process software without free-form model interpretation. If it removes the same coordination with lower cost or risk, the agent has not earned the extra complexity.

Before launch, lock an evidence contract with six fields:

- **Work boundary:** the start, finish, permitted actions, exception routes, and named business owner.
- **Matched alternatives:** the current process, conventional automation, and the agent-supported workflow.
- **Outcome evidence:** correct closure by risk category, including quality failures and prohibited actions.
- **Human work:** routing touches and repair time, kept separate from judgment that was declared human in advance.
- **Economics and resilience:** total software and human cost, supplier dependence, and a tested route back to the previous process.
- **Decision rule:** the fixed case count or period, numerical thresholds derived from the baseline, immediate stop triggers, independent verifier, and decision date.

These fields form a proposed leadership test, not an established benchmark. This article cites no Swedish or Norwegian production case with before-and-after values for routing and repair, control failures, quality, or full cost. I think that absence should remain visible. A Nordic enterprise should derive its thresholds from its own operating baseline and the consequence of failure rather than borrow false precision from a different workflow or context.

One good trial cannot justify removing a layer across a large organization. Repeat the comparison across material workflows, including periods when volumes or exceptions rise, and name the exact responsibility expected to move or disappear. If the evidence shows faster work but the same human coordination, the claim that the structure should change has failed.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Compare the current process, the best conventional automation, and the agent-supported design on the same complete cases. Change the structure only after repeated evidence shows lower routing and repair, acceptable outcomes and controls, full-cost value, and the disappearance of a named responsibility.</p></div>

## Make authority visible in the replacement

A flatter organization needs an operating constitution. Decision rights state who may approve an action, who owns an exception, who can override or stop the workflow, and who answers when the outcome is wrong. Reporting lines once made some of this power visible. Agent-supported work can hide it inside prompts, access settings, thresholds or supplier accounts.

The replacement architecture should keep four responsibilities distinct. The domain owner defines the outcome and policy, while an agent interprets evidence and proposes permitted actions. Ordinary workflow software checks permissions and the current state of the case before anything changes, and a named person decides exceptions that require discretion and holds stop authority.

Current state simply means where the authoritative case record says the work stands now. Idempotency means that retrying the same request cannot create the same external effect twice. In the supplier example, an exception must resolve to an explicit human-approved or rejected state before activation. If an external account creation times out, the case should remain pending until the system reconciles what happened; a retry with the same request key must not create a duplicate supplier.

That boundary matters because established workflow controls already exist. [Camunda](https://docs.camunda.org/manual/latest/user-guide/process-engine/process-engine-concepts/), a business-process automation platform with controls that recorded process state before the current wave of generative AI agents, documents software that stores a process instance's state and follows a defined process. A language model can add flexible interpretation, but it should not become the authority for permissions, committed state, or whether an external action occurred.

Auditability follows the action rather than an invented account of a model's inner reasoning. The record should show the evidence supplied, model and system versions, actions sent to approved tools, permission checks, state changes, retries, human decisions, and final outcome. Rollback means there is a tested way to stop the workflow, restore access and records, and return to a safe previous process.

Legal and workforce authority belong in the same design. The European Union's 2016 [General Data Protection Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng), or GDPR, governs personal-data processing, while the 2024 [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng) applies obligations according to an AI system's use and risk. A legal or control owner must determine what applies to the actual workflow. In Sweden, the government's English translation of the [1976 Employment (Co-Determination in the Workplace) Act](https://www.government.se/government-policy/labour-law-and-work-environment/1976580-employment-co-determination-in-the-workplace-act-lag-om-medbestammande-i-arbetslivet/), commonly called MBL, says an employer bound by a collective agreement must initiate negotiations with the relevant employees' organization before deciding on significant changes to its activities or affected employees' working or employment conditions.

For a material trial, the decision forum should include the business owner; the control owner, who answers for required safeguards and their evidence; the engineering owner; HR; employee representation where applicable; and an independent verifier. It should pre-agree the stop trigger and expected role change, then take verified results to the relevant steering committee or board decision point. I think this is how bottom-up ownership becomes governable without pulling every decision back to the top.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The replacement needs domain ownership and explicit decision rights, with ordinary software enforcing permissions and case state and named human authority for exceptions and stops. Legal obligations, supplier controls and employee-participation duties belong in the operating design before the trial begins.</p></div>

## Build the next career system before removing the old one

The old pyramid is also a career system. Coordination roles have carried pay, status, coaching responsibility, exposure to difficult decisions, and a route toward broader leadership. Removing routine routing without replacing that infrastructure makes the chart flatter while making development less visible.

Leaders should map each affected role before declaring it redundant. Which work is routine routing? Which part is judgment built through experience? Who coaches, resolves conflict, allocates resources, or accepts risk? This decomposition shows whether a role has disappeared, narrowed, or become a different job.

The new paths can include deeper specialist authority, ownership of a larger business domain, responsibility for controls and exceptions, or a formal people-leadership role focused on building judgment. Those paths become credible only when titles and pay recognize them, selection criteria are clear, and learning is funded. Otherwise “move up a level” becomes a promise with no position attached.

I don't know what share of people in coordination-heavy roles will want or be able to move into the new architecture. Some will grow into broader ownership, while some roles will lose their central purpose and some people will leave. A responsible transition says that early and involves employee representatives where required. It gives people time and support to qualify for new roles, and it funds the change instead of treating displacement as a free efficiency gain.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A flatter company removes part of an old career system as well as coordination work. Leaders must separate routing from judgment, define recognized paths to wider responsibility and fund a transparent transition for people whose roles narrow or disappear.</p></div>

## Change the chart last

The CIO's recommendation to the executive team or board should contain five conditions:

- **Operating evidence:** repeated complete-workflow trials outperform both the current process and the best conventional automation on outcomes, control, human coordination and full cost.
- **Structural result:** the evidence identifies a specific responsibility that has moved or disappeared rather than a task that merely became faster.
- **Working replacement:** named domain and control owners, explicit permissions, exception routes, stop authority, audit records, supplier exit and rollback already operate in practice.
- **Workforce commitment:** the organization has agreed how coaching and resource allocation will work after the role change, along with progression, pay and funded learning.
- **Decision legitimacy:** an independent verifier and the proper employee forums, steering committee or board have reviewed the evidence before the reporting line moves.

My take is that flattening should be treated as an outcome of operating evidence, never as the transformation program itself. Agents can make a more distributed and materially flatter company possible, but leadership earns that architecture by proving the work, exposing the authority, and carrying the human cost.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Demand repeated workflow evidence, a matched conventional-automation comparator, visible ownership and stop authority, independent verification, and a funded workforce transition before changing reporting lines. The org chart should record a proven change in work, never serve as the experiment used to create one.</p></div>
