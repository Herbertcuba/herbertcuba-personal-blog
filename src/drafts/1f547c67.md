The consequential board question is not whether an AI system can produce a useful answer. It is whether the company still needs the same coordination layers when AI agents can carry work across several steps.

Two linked claims define that possibility. Every business role acquires a technical dimension, and an organization can become flatter when agents absorb enough routine execution and coordination for ownership to move closer to the business domains. An AI agent here means software that can pursue a defined goal through several steps, consult tools and data, and take bounded actions.

The second claim depends on the first, but neither makes delayering automatic. Hierarchy also carries authority, resolves conflict, coaches people, and absorbs exceptions. A CIO can defend a redesign only after operating evidence shows which coordination work has disappeared, where human judgment remains, and who can intervene when the new system fails.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>AI-first organization design joins two ideas: technical judgment spreads into every domain, and hierarchy may become flatter when agents remove real coordination work. Evidence and explicit human authority determine whether that possibility is safe.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment belongs in the business

In a [video interview published by DECODE on 31 May 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ), [Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), chief people officer at OpenAI, the company behind the ChatGPT AI chatbot, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately qualified the statement: the technical role is applied to a particular business domain.

That qualification matters. Technical judgment is the ability to decide what an AI system may do reliably, what information it may use, how its output will be checked, and which conditions require a person to take over. Most employees will not write code, yet they will need enough technical understanding to make those decisions within their own work.

In sales, that means separating approved product facts from language an agent may draft. Finance must define which variances can be explained automatically and which require formal approval. Marketing has to protect consent, brand rules, and intellectual property, while operations and support need to recognize the exceptions where a plausible answer could still create the wrong outcome.

A central technology team can provide platforms, security controls, and shared standards. It cannot define good work for every domain from a distance. The people closest to the work therefore need bounded room to discover useful applications and own their results. From the center, that bottom-up activity may look less orderly than a single transformation program, but clear boundaries make empowered edges governable.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Technical judgment does not require universal coding. It requires each domain to understand AI reliability, verification, data boundaries, and escalation well enough to own the quality of AI-supported work.</p>
</div>

## A faster task can preserve the old company

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls the mistake of adding AI to an unchanged structure the “Retrofit Trap.” The label is useful because task speed can hide an operating model that still depends on the same routing, approvals, and repair work.

Suppose a marketing agent prepares a campaign proposal in minutes. The proposal still travels by email to brand, privacy, and finance reviewers; a manager chases missing responses; an analyst copies the approved budget into another system; and someone reconciles conflicting versions before launch. The generated document is faster, while the company continues to coordinate the work as before.

One way to understand hierarchy is as an information, approval, and exception-routing system. Managers gather context, decide where a case belongs, secure decisions from other functions, and repair work that crosses organizational boundaries. Those duties are not the whole of management, but they explain why automating a visible task may leave the layer around it untouched.

An agent-supported workflow can reduce that load when it gathers the relevant case state, requests missing information, applies routing rules, proposes the next action, updates the record after approval, and reports the outcome. Human authority still sets the objective, decides consequential exceptions, resolves competing priorities, and remains accountable. The organization becomes AI-first when the complete path of work changes, not merely the speed of one output.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>The Retrofit Trap appears when AI accelerates an activity but leaves manual routing, approval chasing, data transfer, and repair intact. A new operating model must change the complete path from request to verified outcome.</p>
</div>

## Let the workflow earn the flatter structure

The right unit of evidence is a correctly closed case: a request that reaches the agreed outcome, meets its quality and control requirements, updates the proper records, and resolves any exception. The complete workflow begins when demand enters the organization and ends when that closure is verified. It includes model activity, software actions, and every human coordination touch, meaning a manual hand-off, approval, or reassignment needed to move the case forward.

Averages are not enough. Leadership needs results across routine, ambiguous, rare, and high-consequence cases, with failures shown by type rather than hidden in one overall rate. The review should track correct closure, manual coordination touches, repair effort, control failures, elapsed time, full operating cost, and the amount of judgment that still belongs to people. Full cost includes integration, model use, monitoring, human review, incident response, and ongoing service ownership.

The evidence assembled for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer. That gap should remain visible. Each company has to set acceptance rules against its own baseline, obligations, risk, and case mix before a test begins.

**Illustrative local decision record. These are fields to complete, not external benchmarks.**

| Decision field | What must be agreed before the test |
| --- | --- |
| Workflow scope | Start and end points, excluded work, and the business outcome being tested |
| Case sample | Expected volume and a representative mix of routine, ambiguous, exceptional, and high-impact cases |
| Comparator | Baseline period, current process, and the same measures for human-led and agent-supported cases |
| Test envelope | Review window, budget ceiling, production owner, and fixed decision date |
| Acceptance rule | Locally chosen requirements for correct closure, quality by case type, coordination reduction, repair effort, and total cost |
| Control limits | Events that stop the workflow automatically, plus the person authorized to suspend it manually |
| Residual judgment | Decisions that remain human, the role that owns them, and the capacity required |
| Scale-or-stop choice | Conditions for production handover, another bounded test, redesign, or abandonment |

The abandonment condition deserves the same precision as the success condition. If coordination merely moves into hidden review queues, if severe failures escape the agreed controls, or if full cost exceeds the value of the removed work, the evidence does not support delayering. A demo can justify further learning; only sustained workflow performance can justify changing roles.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Measure correctly closed cases across a representative case mix, including every hand-off, repair, failure, and cost. Predeclare local acceptance and abandonment rules because no Nordic production case in the available evidence supplies a safe delayering threshold.</p>
</div>

## Make authority more visible as layers shrink

When coordination moves into software, authority must become easier to inspect. Domain-mapped ownership starts by naming the business area accountable for the complete outcome, rather than distributing responsibility across whichever teams happen to touch the case. That owner defines acceptable closure, normal exceptions, and the point at which another authority must decide.

Rules-based workflow software should hold the authoritative state, which is the official record of where a case stands and which actions are currently allowed. It should enforce permissions, preserve an audit trail, and make retries safe. The Internet Engineering Task Force’s 2022 [HTTP Semantics standard, RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html), uses *idempotency* for an operation whose intended effect is the same after one or several identical requests. In business terms, a retry must not create a second payment, approval, or account change.

The agent can interpret an email, assemble context from approved sources, detect a likely exception, and propose the next action. It should not grant itself access, alter the policy that constrains it, or decide whether its own control breach is acceptable. Those boundaries belong in rules-based software and in decisions made by named people.

Overrides, stopping, and recovery need equally clear paths. An override is a recorded human decision to depart from the normal rule for a stated reason. Stop authority is the right of a named role to suspend the workflow without waiting for the agent or vendor. Rollback means returning reversible system changes to a known safe state; where an external effect cannot be reversed, the service owner needs a recovery and reconciliation plan.

| Owner | Decision and obligation |
| --- | --- |
| Executive sponsor and funding owner | Authorize scope, budget, workforce provision, and the final scale-or-stop decision |
| Domain owner | Own the end-to-end outcome, case definitions, quality, and business exceptions |
| Privacy, security, legal, and procurement owners | Set the controls relevant to the workflow and decide whether unresolved risks are acceptable |
| Production service owner | Own reliability, monitoring, incident response, audit evidence, handover into ongoing operations, and recovery |
| People owner and employee representatives | Address consultation, role impact, learning, redeployment, progression, and pay under applicable rules and agreements |
| Stop authority and review forum | Suspend operation, approve restart conditions, and review evidence on predeclared dates |

The governance sequence follows from those duties. The sponsor sets the test envelope; domain and control owners agree the baseline, boundaries, and failure rules; the people process begins before role decisions are fixed; and the service owner proves that production support and recovery are ready. Only then can the review forum authorize ongoing use or an organizational change.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Agents may interpret and propose, while rules-based workflow software protects permissions, case state, safe retries, and audit evidence. Named people must own outcomes, controls, exceptions, stopping, restart, recovery, funding, and production service.</p>
</div>

## Redesign careers before removing coordination roles

A coordination-heavy role rarely consists only of forwarding information. The same person may coach a new colleague, settle a conflict, interpret weak signals, allocate scarce capacity, and carry accountability when the standard process fails. A responsible redesign separates that human judgment from routine routing before concluding that the role itself is redundant.

This changes the career system as much as the org chart. Fewer management layers mean fewer traditional promotion steps, so expert paths need comparable status, pay progression, and influence. Coaching must have an explicit owner and enough capacity. Entry-level roles need access to real decisions and feedback, or the company will automate the work through which future experts once learned.

The transition also needs money and time. People whose work changes need paid learning tied to roles that will actually exist, transparent selection criteria, and early consultation with employee representatives under the laws and agreements that apply. Some employees can move into domain ownership, quality, exception handling, service management, or specialist work. Where redeployment is not credible, leaders still owe people a fair and clear exit rather than a promise that every role will somehow survive.

These commitments are part of the operating case. If the proposed structure saves coordination cost but leaves coaching, progression, or transition unfunded, the design has transferred a liability rather than removed one.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Separate routine coordination from coaching, conflict resolution, scarce-resource decisions, and accountability. Any flatter design must fund expert careers, learning, consultation, redeployment, and fair exits before role changes are authorized.</p>
</div>

## Make the organizational decision conditional

The executive decision should be narrower than “become AI-first.” Choose one domain, identify a complete workflow, sign the local evidence record, and name every owner before production testing begins. The fixed review date should permit several outcomes: retain the current layer, redesign the workflow, change role boundaries without delayering, run another bounded test, or remove a layer within the proven scope.

For a board-defensible decision, the evidence and the commitments must arrive together. The workflow results should show where coordination and repair fell, where quality or control failed, what the service costs in full, and which judgment remains human. The authorization should also show who can stop and restart the service, how irreversible effects will be handled, who funds ongoing operation, and what happens to the people whose roles change.

A flatter company is defensible when work, authority, and human development still have clear homes after a layer is removed. Until that is visible in operating evidence and funded commitments, the hierarchy remains part of the control system, whether or not an agent can produce an impressive result.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
  <span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
  <p>Authorize organizational change only for a bounded workflow that has met predeclared evidence, governance, service, and workforce conditions. The org chart should follow proven changes in how work and authority operate.</p>
</div>
