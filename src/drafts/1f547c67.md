Two linked claims define the architecture of an AI-first company. Every business role acquires a technical dimension, and organizations can become flatter as more execution and coordination move to the people and systems closest to the work.

The first claim has a useful source. [Arvind KC is chief people officer at OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), the company that develops ChatGPT, an AI chatbot. In [a video interview published by the DECODE channel on 31 May 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ), KC said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately clarified that the technical role is applied to a particular business domain. The second claim, that ownership can move downward and reduce the need for some coordination layers, is the design proposition this article examines. It is not an established outcome at scale.

Here, an AI agent means a software system that interprets context and uses approved tools to carry work through several steps, consistent with the working definition in [OpenAI’s practical guide to building agents](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf). Agents make a flatter company possible only when they remove meaningful work across a complete workflow. A responsible leadership team still needs proof, explicit decision rights, controlled system complexity, and a credible plan for the people whose roles will change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Distributed technical judgment and agent-supported coordination can support a flatter company, but that organizational shape remains a hypothesis until complete workflows, controls, and workforce commitments make it defensible.</p></div>

## Technical judgment belongs inside every domain

Technical judgment is the ability to decide where AI is reliable enough to help, what evidence is needed, when an exception matters, and which outcome still requires a person to take responsibility. Coding may be useful for some roles, but it is not the defining skill. The defining skill is understanding both the business domain and the limits of the system acting inside it.

That changes ordinary functional work. A sales leader needs to know when an AI-generated discount recommendation lacks account context. Finance must decide which policy checks can run automatically and which exceptions expose the company to material risk. Support teams need to recognize when a plausible answer is still wrong for a vulnerable customer, while operations must understand the downstream effect of changing an order or service state.

This is why bottom-up ownership matters. A central AI team can provide shared platforms, security controls, evaluation methods, and technical support, but it cannot hold every domain’s policy, history, and edge cases. People closer to the work should be able to shape agent-supported workflows within clear boundaries, with named owners deciding what the agent may propose, what software may execute, and what a human must approve.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Every role becomes more technical when domain expertise includes judging AI reliability, evidence, and exceptions. Bottom-up ownership works only when local expertise operates inside shared controls and explicit decision rights.</p></div>

## Follow the work that hierarchy carries

A hierarchy does more than arrange titles. Managers collect information, sequence work, reconcile competing priorities, check permissions, approve exceptions, and repair cases that fall between systems. If an AI tool speeds up one activity while those coordination duties remain, the organization has gained a faster task rather than a new operating model.

Business author Benjamin Simkin calls this the “Retrofit Trap” in his 2026 book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5). The term describes adding AI to an old structure instead of redesigning the flow of work around what the technology can now do. The label matters because local productivity can hide unchanged routing and repair costs.

Consider a hypothetical warranty claim at an industrial manufacturer. An agent might extract the product details, compare the claim with policy, and draft a response in seconds. Yet a manager may still need to discover missing service records, ask finance to validate a credit, route a safety concern to an engineer, update two systems, and confirm that the customer received the right remedy. The generated response is faster, but the coordination system remains intact.

The useful unit of evidence is therefore a correctly closed case: a complete instance of work that reaches the right business outcome, leaves every required system in a consistent state, and satisfies the relevant controls. For the warranty claim, closure includes the customer decision, any payment or replacement, the accounting entry, the product record, and the audit evidence. Evaluation should compare representative case types and measure quality, elapsed time, human routing touches, repair effort, control failures, full cost, and the judgment that still remains with people.

The evidence available for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer. That absence does not disprove the architecture, but it rules out borrowing a universal benchmark. Each enterprise has to declare its own acceptance rules before the test begins and preserve the hierarchy until the workflow earns a different design.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The hierarchy can shrink only when agent-supported workflows remove the information gathering, routing, approval, and repair work that managers currently perform. Task speed is weak evidence; correctly closed cases across the whole workflow are the relevant test.</p></div>

## A flatter structure needs firmer boundaries

Removing coordination layers does not remove the need for coordination. It shifts that work into an operating architecture, so the boundaries of authority must become easier to inspect. The cleanest division is domain-mapped: business owners define outcomes and policy, agents interpret information and propose actions, ordinary rules-based software enforces permissions and valid state changes, and named people own consequential exceptions.

The system also needs an authoritative state, meaning the trusted record of where a case stands now. That record belongs in a case-management system or database rather than in an agent’s conversation history. A retry should have the same intended effect as one request instead of issuing a second credit or replacement. This property is called idempotency, a term defined in [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html), the 2022 Internet standard governing the meaning and behavior of web requests.

A compact rule makes the boundary concrete. A warranty claim may move from “proposed” to “approved” only after identity, entitlement, amount, and safety checks have passed. An agent’s confidence score cannot move the claim directly from “received” to “paid.” The software rejects that invalid transition, records the attempt, and routes the case to the named exception owner.

[NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework 1.0](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf), a voluntary 2023 framework from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, treats governance as a continuing part of AI risk management and calls for clear roles and responsibilities. For an enterprise workflow, those responsibilities should be visible before live use:

| Responsibility | Named ownership |
| --- | --- |
| Business outcome, policy, and ordinary exceptions | Domain and workflow owner |
| Permissions, privacy, security, and procurement conditions | Relevant control owners |
| Agent behavior, approved tools, and evaluation | Technical owner |
| Live service, logs, incidents, and recovery | Production service owner |
| Stop and restart decisions | One designated operational authority |
| Role changes, learning, and employee participation | Executive sponsor and people leadership, with employee representatives involved |

The sequence matters. The executive sponsor funds the test, and domain and control owners set its boundaries. Privacy, security, and procurement reviews happen before production access, while the service owner accepts ongoing operation only after the evidence review. Every override needs an audit trail. Rollback means restoring a previous safe system state where that is possible; irreversible actions such as a completed payment require a defined correction and reconciliation process. After a stop, restart should require a documented cause, a verified fix, and approval from the designated authority.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A flatter company needs visible authority in place of managerial routing. Agents may interpret and propose, while rules-based software protects permissions and case state, and named people own exceptions, stopping, restart, recovery, and audit.</p></div>

## Redesign the career system with the hierarchy

When routine routing and repair leave a role, the role’s old shape may no longer be justified. That does not make all management work disposable. Coaching, conflict resolution, resource choices under uncertainty, performance accountability, and the development of judgment remain human responsibilities even when agents handle much of the administrative flow.

The difficult part is that many companies have tied status, pay, and progression to managing larger teams. A flatter structure therefore needs credible expert paths, wider opportunities to own business outcomes, and compensation that does not punish people for choosing mastery over headcount. Otherwise the company asks for bottom-up ownership while preserving a career system that rewards the old pyramid.

The transition belongs inside the operating decision. Leadership should map affected tasks and roles, involve employee representatives while options are still open, fund paid learning, redesign jobs, and define routes into new work. Some changes may still lead to fair exits, and pretending otherwise damages trust. The full business case should include consultation, training, redeployment, and transition costs rather than treating them as somebody else’s budget after approval.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Delayering changes how people learn, advance, earn, and exercise ambition. The design is incomplete until leadership separates enduring human judgment from routine coordination and funds a fair transition for the roles that change.</p></div>

## Ask the board to authorize evidence

The useful board decision is narrower than a promise to become flatter. It is permission to test whether one bounded workflow can remove enough execution and coordination to justify a later structural decision. The test charter can fit on one page, with local values agreed in the blanks:

- **Scope and comparator:** the workflow from **[start event]** to **[verified business outcome]**, compared with **[current baseline process and period]**.
- **Case population:** **[number]** cases over **[review window]**, covering **[routine, ambiguous, high-consequence, and failure-prone case groups]** in declared proportions.
- **Correct closure:** at least **[local threshold]** of cases reach the right outcome with consistent records and complete control evidence.
- **Coordination removed:** human routing touches fall from **[baseline]** to **[required level]**, and repair effort falls from **[baseline]** to **[required level]** without moving hidden work to another team.
- **Control limits:** **[maximum error and policy-breach levels]**, with **[named critical events]** triggering an automatic stop and review.
- **Economics:** a budget ceiling of **[amount]** and a full cost per correctly closed case of **[amount]**, including models, integration, review, service ownership, controls, and workforce transition.
- **Ownership and handover:** **[executive sponsor]**, **[workflow owner]**, **[control owners]**, **[production service owner]**, **[people and employee-representative forum]**, and **[stop authority]** accept their responsibilities in writing. Production funding and the ongoing review cadence are agreed before scale.
- **Decision date:** on **[date]**, the review forum chooses **scale, redesign, hold, or stop**. Missing a predeclared condition blocks delayering rather than inviting a new story about why the result was close enough.

The blanks are deliberate because no responsible threshold can be copied from a demonstration or an unrelated company. Reporting lines should remain stable during the test. If the workflow meets its declared conditions and the remaining human judgment has a clear home, leadership can defend a new structure with evidence. If it does not, the company may still keep useful task improvements, but it has not justified removing the coordination layer.

An AI-first company can be materially flatter. The CIO’s responsibility is to make sure the workflow, the control model, and the workforce commitment earn that shape before the org chart records it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Authorize a bounded workflow test with predeclared evidence, cost, control, ownership, transition, and stop conditions. Change the hierarchy only after that record shows that coordination has truly disappeared and every remaining responsibility has a named home.</p></div>
