An AI-first company changes where judgment sits before it changes who reports to whom. People in sales, finance, operations, marketing, and support gain a technical dimension: they need to judge what AI can handle, where it can fail, and when a person must decide. If AI agents, software systems that interpret a goal and act through approved tools, also carry routine execution and routing, ownership can move closer to the work and the organization may support fewer coordination layers.

In February 2026, [OpenAI, an AI research and deployment company, appointed Arvind KC as chief people officer](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/). In a [recorded DECODE interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s), KC argued that “every role … is fundamentally a technical role,” with technical capability “applied to a particular domain.” He also described innovation as bottom-up: people apply the capability inside work they understand, instead of waiting for a central AI function to invent every use.

The capability is judgment rather than programming. A commercial leader should know when an agent lacks current contract terms, when a proposed discount crosses policy, and what evidence a human approver needs. Central teams still provide shared platforms and controls, but domain specialists decide how the capability should serve their work.

That creates a real possibility of a flatter company, though the arrival of agents proves very little by itself. A new structure becomes defensible only when whole workflows need less human relay work, decision rights remain explicit, operational complexity is controlled, and the people affected have a credible path through the change.

## Prove that the relay work has disappeared

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls AI added inside an unchanged structure the “Retrofit Trap.” The term names a common design error; it does not prove that any particular hierarchy should be removed.

Consider a contract-renewal workflow. An agent may produce a polished proposal in seconds, yet pricing still checks the margin by hand, legal receives unusual terms through email, a manager routes approval, sales operations updates the customer record, and someone repairs mismatches later. One task became faster. The operating model did not change because the surrounding coordination survived.

That relay work is one reason hierarchies exist. Organization-design scholar Jay R. Galbraith described organizations in a [1974 paper on information processing](https://doi.org/10.1287/inte.4.3.28): standing rules handle predictable work, while hierarchy helps carry information and resolve exceptions when those rules are insufficient. An agent-supported workflow can gather facts, apply routine policy, direct a genuine exception to the right owner, and update the case record. It cannot decide the company’s risk appetite or quietly rewrite policy.

Evaluate the complete workflow, from the renewal trigger to accepted terms and consistent records. A useful internal unit is the **correctly closed case**: the business outcome is right, policy was followed, the relevant systems agree on the final status, and no concealed human repair was needed. This is a proposed decision test, not an established industry benchmark.

The evidence should cover representative normal cases, edge cases, failures, and escalations. Track human routing touches, repair effort, outcome quality, control failures, elapsed time, total cost, dependencies, and the judgment still left with people. There is no Swedish or Norwegian production case in the available evidence that supplies a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer, so each leadership team must set and defend its own criteria before a trial begins.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A faster task does not justify a flatter company. Leaders need evidence that complete workflows close correctly with less routing and repair, at an acceptable cost and without transferring unresolved risk elsewhere.</p>
</div>

## Replace hidden coordination with visible authority

A flatter operating model needs an explicit operating constitution: a compact record of who owns each business outcome, which decisions may be automated, and where human authority begins. Start with a domain map. A domain is a defined area of business responsibility, such as commercial renewals, with a named owner for its policy, outcomes, and exceptions.

Inside that boundary, ordinary rule-based software should enforce identity, permissions, limits, and the **authoritative state**, meaning the official current status of the case. The agent can interpret documents, prepare a recommendation, and call approved tools within those rules. This keeps flexible model reasoning useful without asking it to remember who is allowed to do what.

For a renewal, the visible state path might be **proposed → policy-checked → approved → executed → reconciled**. Reconciled means that the intended action has been confirmed against the contract and customer systems. A stale proposal or an over-limit discount must be rejected without changing the official state. Retries also need an [idempotency control](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#name-idempotent-methods), an established software principle under which repeating the same approved request has the same intended effect as making it once. If execution is uncertain, the case should pause for reconciliation rather than let the model guess.

Decision rights then become inspectable. The business owner sets policy; the domain owner carries routine outcomes; named control owners decide defined exceptions. An override is a deliberate authorized departure from the normal path, and the audit record should show who made it and why. Stop authority names the person who can suspend the workflow immediately, while a rollback owner is responsible for restoring the last stable process and records. These controls are established workflow practices applied to agents, not new powers delegated to model reasoning.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Agent reasoning belongs inside boundaries enforced by ordinary software. Named people must still own policy, exceptions, overrides, stopping the workflow, restoring a stable state, and the record that makes every decision auditable.</p>
</div>

## Make the people commitment part of the approval

Coordination-heavy roles are rarely made of coordination alone. A manager may route approvals and translate priorities, but also coach people, settle conflict, allocate scarce resources, and carry accountability. Workflow evidence should separate the routine relay work an agent can absorb from the human judgment and care the organization still needs. Removing the role before reallocating those obligations merely hides them.

Fewer layers also remove rungs from the career ladder. Progression can no longer depend mainly on acquiring a larger team, so the replacement architecture needs senior expert and domain-owner paths with credible pay, influence, and learning. It must say who provides coaching, how people qualify for the new roles, which learning time is funded, and what redeployment or fair exit looks like when work genuinely disappears. In a Nordic organization, the people executive and employee representatives should enter while the design can still change, with the exact participation process set by local obligations.

Treat the structural decision as an operating process with named owners:

1. **Establish the baseline.** The business domain executive convenes the work. A process owner documents the complete workflow and selects normal, difficult, failed, and escalated cases; finance records total cost, and the people function maps the roles and time involved.
2. **Set the boundary.** The CIO and domain owner agree which actions the agent may take. Risk, security, legal, and workforce representatives join where their responsibilities apply. Stop authority and the rollback owner are named before live operation.
3. **Verify the trial.** An evidence owner separate from the build team checks outcomes, logs, human touches, repair effort, residual judgment, and control failures. The executive operating forum reviews the same record at a cadence chosen in advance, while the control owner can stop the workflow between meetings.
4. **Record the decision.** A structural proposal carries the signatures of the business executive, CIO, control or risk owner, and people executive, with finance attesting the cost evidence and employee participation recorded. The forum chooses to continue, narrow, stop, reverse, or change the structure, and records the reason.

Only then should leadership ask whether a coordination layer still carries necessary work. Coaching, policy ownership, exception judgment, and accountability need a visible destination before a role changes. The org chart should record an operating model that has already earned confidence through evidence and commitments; it should not serve as the experiment.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Changing the hierarchy requires both a workforce bargain and a signed operating case. Baseline the work, set the controls, verify the evidence independently, name stop and reversal owners, and reassign every human obligation before approving a new structure.</p>
</div>
