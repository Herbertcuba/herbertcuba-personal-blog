An AI-first company rests on two linked claims. Every business role acquires a technical dimension. And when AI agents absorb meaningful execution and coordination, more ownership can sit with domain teams close to the work, allowing the company to become flatter. An AI agent is software that uses a generative model to interpret a goal and take steps through connected tools within defined limits.

Arvind KC, chief people officer at OpenAI, the AI company that develops ChatGPT, [said in a video interview published on 31 May 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ) that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately qualified the point: the technical role is applied to a business domain. That supports the first claim. The move toward bottom-up ownership is my architecture argument: if domain teams can resolve more work where it originates, less of it needs to travel upward for translation, status reporting, and routine approval.

Here, AI-first means designing work around agents across complete workflows, rather than attaching assistants to isolated tasks. A responsible CIO changes roles and layers only when operating evidence, explicit decision rights, controlled systems, and a credible transition for affected people make the redesign defensible.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Distributed technical judgment can support more bottom-up ownership and a flatter company, but organizational change must follow proof across the full workflow, visible authority, reliable controls, and a funded people transition.</p>

## Technical judgment changes where work begins

Technical judgment is the ability to decide what an AI system can do reliably, which data and permissions it may use, how its work will be checked, and when a person must decide. It does not require everyone to write code. Sales and support teams need to know when an agent has enough customer context to act; finance and operations must define which exceptions require authorized review; marketing must test whether AI-produced claims are supported before they reach the market.

This capability changes where improvement can start. A domain team, meaning the people accountable for a business process or area, can identify a problem, shape an agent-supported workflow, and improve it within company-wide security rules, investment choices, and risk limits. Bottom-up ownership therefore means bounded initiative, with clear escalation and accountability.

A hierarchy still has important roles in strategy, coaching, conflict resolution, and risk judgment. It also carries information, approvals, status updates, and exceptions between teams. Agents may absorb standard routing, record updates, consistency checks, and some routine approvals within policy. Humans must still own policy changes, material risk, contested exceptions, and decisions about people. A flatter structure becomes plausible only when enough of the routing work really disappears.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Technical judgment puts AI capability, limits, permissions, and review inside each business function. It can move initiative toward domain teams, while enterprise controls and consequential human decisions remain explicit.</p>
</div>

## Test the workflow, not the task

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls it the “Retrofit Trap” when a company adds AI to an operating structure that remains essentially unchanged. Task speed can hide an intact coordination burden.

Consider supplier invoices as a structural example. An agent may read an invoice and extract its fields in seconds. People may still chase a missing purchase order, compare records, route an exception to the budget owner, confirm approval, update the ledger, answer the supplier, and repair a duplicate or incorrect payment. One step became faster, but the complete case still depends on the same handoffs.

The better unit is a correctly closed case: the workflow reaches a valid business outcome, updates the required records, captures approvals, handles exceptions, and leaves no hidden repair queue. The evidence available for this article includes no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer. A Nordic leadership team therefore needs a local evidence contract, written before the trial, that covers:

- **Scope and comparison:** the end-to-end case, the current baseline, a relevant mix of routine, ambiguous, rare, and high-consequence cases, and a fixed review point.
- **Outcome and coordination:** closure quality, reopened cases, control failures, human routing touches, repair effort, elapsed time, and the judgment still left to people.
- **Full value bridge:** coordination and repair costs removed, set against new model and service costs, control work, transition spending, and residual human capacity.
- **Production and reversibility:** a funded service owner and handover date, data-storage and processing boundaries, applicable risk review, and the ability to replace a supplier or model without losing case history or control.
- **People and decision rules:** affected roles, consultation ownership, learning or redeployment funding, plus acceptance, stop, and abandonment conditions set before results are known.

These are decision categories, not universal benchmarks. They prevent a promising pilot from advancing while its failures, full cost, service obligations, and workforce consequences stay outside the record.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Retrofit Trap accelerates one step while preserving the surrounding handoffs. Test correctly closed cases through a predeclared local contract that exposes coordination, repair, quality, control, full cost, reversibility, production ownership, and people commitments.</p>
</div>

## Make authority visible in the system

A model is useful for interpreting messy language and proposing a next action. It should not be the final source of permission or the official record of where a case stands. Map each workflow to a domain owner for the business outcome, a control owner for policy and risk, a service owner for reliability and change, and a people owner for role consequences.

Then separate the system responsibilities. A case-management record should hold the authoritative state, meaning the controlled record of the case’s current position. A durable workflow service should remember progress and resume work after interruption. A policy engine, software that applies prewritten permission rules, should enforce which actor may take which action. The agent interprets and proposes within those boundaries.

For the invoice example, the allowed states might be received, matched, approved, and paid. The agent may extract data and propose that an invoice is matched; the authorized budget owner approves it; the payment service records payment. The system rejects a direct move from received to paid. To enforce idempotency, meaning that a repeated command must not create another business effect, the payment service records a unique command key and rejects a retry with the same key.

An audit trail records who or what acted, on which information, and with what result. An override lets an authorized person replace a system decision and records the reason. Stop authority names the person who can halt the workflow. Rollback returns reversible digital state to a last valid point; an external action that cannot be undone requires a compensating action and reconciliation. After an incident or a material model or policy change, teams should rerun known past cases, add tests based on the failure, review changed behavior, and reauthorize the workflow before expanding it.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Agents may interpret and propose, while controlled software holds state, enforces permissions, prevents duplicate effects, and preserves evidence. Named humans own policy, exceptions, overrides, stopping, recovery, and reauthorization.</p>
</div>

## Change careers before changing layers

Management roles often combine reporting and routing with coaching, conflict resolution, capability building, risk judgment, and care for team performance. If agents reduce the first set of tasks, leaders still have to decide where the second set will live and how it will be valued. A flatter company also needs expert and workflow-owner career paths, clear pay progression, accountable time for coaching, and paid learning. Ambition needs somewhere to go when progression no longer depends mainly on acquiring more direct reports.

The transition will not be frictionless. Some coordination-heavy roles will change substantially, and some may disappear. HR, accountable business leaders, and employee representatives where relevant should enter the process before decisions are fixed. Redeployment is credible only when there is a real role and funded learning; when there is not, the organization still owes people a fair process. A business case that excludes these costs is incomplete.

At an executive or board discussion, I would separate two decisions. First, is the agent-supported workflow ready for production across the relevant case mix, controls, costs, recovery paths, and service commitments? Second, has it removed enough coordination to justify changing roles or a layer, with residual judgment assigned and the workforce transition agreed?

A positive first decision does not force a positive second one. The workflow can run under the current accountability model while the organization gathers stronger evidence. If both decisions pass, leaders can redesign that part of the company with a review date, explicit stop conditions, and a path back if outcomes or controls worsen. The CIO is then approving a specific, governed organizational change, rather than a flatter future in the abstract.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Rebuild coaching, careers, pay progression, learning, and the transition process before removing a layer. Authorize production first, then decide separately whether proven coordination changes and explicit people commitments justify redesigning the organization.</p>
</div>
