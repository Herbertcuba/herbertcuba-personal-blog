Two linked claims sit at the center of an AI-first company. Every role acquires a technical dimension, and ownership can move closer to the work as the organization becomes flatter.

[Arvind KC](https://openai.com/index/arvind-kc-chief-people-officer/), chief people officer at OpenAI, an AI research and deployment company, gave the first claim useful precision in a [2026 interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ): “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately qualified the statement by explaining that the technical role is applied to a particular domain. A finance professional still works in finance; the difference is that judging what the technology can and cannot do becomes part of the job.

The second claim is an organizational hypothesis. AI agents, software that can interpret a goal, choose steps, and act through connected tools, may absorb enough routine execution and coordination to reduce the need for some management layers. That does not make flattening automatic. A responsible leader changes the organization only after the workflow evidence, authority model, technical controls, and workforce transition make the decision defensible.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>AI-first design links distributed technical judgment with more ownership near the work, but a flatter structure is a possibility to prove locally, not a guaranteed result of deploying agents.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment belongs in every domain

Technical judgment is the ability to decide where AI is useful, specify the result it should produce, test that result against domain reality, and recognize when a person must take over. It requires enough understanding of the technology to challenge its output, but it does not require everyone to write code.

That capability looks different across the business. Sales must know which account signals are reliable enough to shape an offer. Operations must distinguish a normal case from an exception that could interrupt service. Finance must decide which classifications can be automated and which commitments need formal approval. Marketing must protect evidence, rights, and brand judgment when content is AI-assisted, while support must separate help with interpreting a request from authority to grant a remedy.

This is why a central AI team cannot own the whole transformation. It can provide shared technology, security controls, and support, but domain teams must own the meaning of correct work. When they also have room to improve their workflows inside visible boundaries, innovation can move from a central program toward the people closest to customers, suppliers, and operations. What looks less orderly from the center can be more accountable at the edge, provided ownership is explicit.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Every function needs enough technical judgment to specify and evaluate AI-supported work. Central teams provide common controls; domain teams remain responsible for what a correct business outcome means.</p>
</div>

## The unit of change is the complete case

Benjamin Simkin, author of the 2026 business book [*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls the act of bolting AI onto an old structure the “Retrofit Trap.” The term matters because task acceleration can leave the operating model untouched.

Consider a customer asking for a refund after a failed delivery. A language model might summarize the complaint and draft a response. Yet a person may still identify the policy, send the case to finance, copy information into a payment system, chase approval, notice a duplicate, repair the record, and tell the customer what happened. The visible task improved, while the same queues and coordination work survived around it.

An AI-first design treats the complete workflow as the unit of change. The refund case begins when the request enters and ends when the correct decision has been executed, recorded, and communicated. A **correctly closed case** is one that reaches that defined outcome without an unresolved error, missing control, or hidden repair step. Measuring the draft alone says little about whether the organization can change.

Hierarchy has always carried more than formal authority. Managers collect and compress information, secure approvals, and route exceptions when the standard process breaks. An agent-supported workflow may gather missing evidence, maintain context, propose a policy-consistent next step, update systems, and route a genuine exception directly to its owner. People still set policy, resolve ambiguous or high-impact cases, make trade-offs, coach others, and remain accountable for the outcome.

The distinction is decisive. If employees must check every action, repair routine failures, and manually carry cases between systems, the coordination layer has not disappeared. It has merely become less visible.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>The Retrofit Trap speeds up an isolated task while preserving routing, approvals, and repair work. Flattening becomes plausible only when agents reduce that coordination across correctly closed business cases.</p>
</div>

## Evidence comes before flattening

The evidence assembled for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that establishes a universal numerical threshold for safely removing a management layer. That boundary should shape the decision. Any acceptance level in a trial is a local commitment based on the workflow, risk, and baseline, not an industry benchmark.

Start with a bounded, repeated workflow whose outcome and exceptions can be observed. Before adding an agent, compare the proposal with a simpler **deterministic workflow**, where predefined rules produce a specified result. If better forms, clearer policy, or conventional automation can remove the coordination at lower risk and cost, that may be the right design.

A credible trial also needs an operating charter. Name the executive sponsor and the business owner accountable for the outcome. Fund the technology and the required time from domain experts. Assign risk and security ownership, a people owner for role consequences, and a procurement owner for supplier terms. Fix the review date before the results arrive.

The evidence should be grouped so the decision remains readable:

- **Outcome:** correctly closed cases, business quality, and the distribution of normal and exceptional cases.
- **Coordination and control:** human routing touches, repair effort, exception load, failed controls, and the logged sequence of proposed, approved, rejected, and executed actions.
- **Economics and use:** elapsed time, adoption, and total cost per correctly closed case, including supplier, integration, review, repair, and control costs.
- **Residual human work:** the judgment, coaching, relationship work, and new oversight load that remain with people.

The team should test stopping and recovery, not only successful execution. It should also declare acceptance and reversal conditions in advance, then use the same case definition and cost basis at every review. Only a sustained reduction in coordination, without weaker outcomes or work pushed into hidden queues, supports an organization change.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Use a funded local trial to compare an agent-supported workflow with a simpler rules-based redesign. Judge both through correctly closed cases, visible coordination and control failures, total cost, and the human work that remains.</p>
</div>

## Authority must become more visible

Removing a coordinator without replacing the coordination mechanism creates an unmanaged system. The replacement architecture therefore begins with a domain map: every important workflow has a named business owner, a defined outcome, and a known set of exceptions.

Next come **decision rights**, the explicit rules for who may propose, approve, execute, override, or stop an action. Ordinary software should enforce permissions and the **case state**, meaning the authoritative record of where a case is now and which move is valid next. The agent can interpret documents and propose a transition from one state to another, but free-form model reasoning should not decide whether its own action is permitted.

A refund workflow might express that boundary like this:

| Actor | Current case state | Proposed transition | Policy result | Accountable owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Agent | Evidence complete | Approve a refund inside delegated policy | Allowed and logged | Support operations owner |
| Agent | Evidence missing | Release a refund | Rejected; state unchanged and case routed | Support operations owner |
| Finance controller | Refund approved | Release payment | Allowed through the payment system | Finance process owner |
| Incident owner | Any active state | Stop new actions | Workflow paused and pending effects reviewed | Named duty owner |

This small contract makes a rejected action as important as an approved one. It also creates **auditability**, a record showing what was proposed, which rule was applied, who approved an exception, and what actually happened. Override and stop authority must point to named people, while recovery procedures must say how the organization pauses, reconciles, and resumes the workflow.

Duplicate actions deserve special care. [RFC 9110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-9.2.2), the 2022 internet standard that defines HTTP request semantics, uses **idempotent** for a request method whose repeated execution has the same intended effect as one execution. A stable case identifier and duplicate checks apply the same design idea to business work, but they do not prove that every connected system acted exactly once. The recovery plan must therefore distinguish changes software can reverse from external effects that require reconciliation or a compensating action. That is the practical meaning of **rollback** here: a controlled route back to a known state where reversal is possible, plus an explicit remedy where it is not.

The same ownership discipline applies outside the workflow engine. A named risk owner records the privacy, AI, data-location, sector, and labor obligations that apply to this particular use case. A procurement owner secures access to logs and data, defines supplier failure and exit paths, and makes sure the organization can operate or retreat if a service changes. One universal checklist will not fit every domain, but invisible responsibility fits none of them.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>A flatter organization needs stronger visible control: named domain owners, explicit decision rights, software-enforced permissions and case state, logged rejections and overrides, human stop authority, and tested recovery and supplier-exit paths.</p>
</div>

## The pyramid also carried careers

The old hierarchy did more than move information. It distributed status, pay, coaching, learning opportunities, and a visible route for ambition. If coordination-heavy roles shrink, those functions do not disappear with them.

Some managerial work remains distinctly human: setting direction under uncertainty, resolving conflict, coaching, judging unusual trade-offs, and accepting accountability. Routine status collection, handoffs, reminders, and standard approvals are more exposed to redesign. In some areas a role may become broader and more valuable; in others fewer roles may be needed. Pretending otherwise turns an operating-model proposal into a trust problem.

A flatter company therefore needs a career architecture that does not make headcount the only route to progression. Deep domain mastery, workflow ownership, control responsibility, cross-domain design, and coaching can carry greater scope, pay, and status without requiring a larger reporting line. Those paths need real criteria and compensation, not new labels attached to old jobs.

Before changing the organization, leaders should publish a role-impact map and fund the transition it implies. That includes protected learning time, fair skill assessment, redeployment routes, employee participation appropriate to the local context, updated pay and progression, and honest support where an exit cannot be avoided. The people owner belongs in the workflow trial from the beginning because the exception load, oversight burden, and learning demand are operating evidence, not an announcement to prepare after the technology decision.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Flattening removes parts of a career and learning system as well as coordination work. Leaders must rebuild progression, pay, coaching, participation, and transition support before they remove the roles that carried them.</p>
</div>

## What belongs in the board record

The final decision should live in one record that is updated from trial through any later organization change. It is a local contract, not a universal scorecard.

| Decision field | Required entry |
| --- | --- |
| Workflow and outcome owner | Named workflow, accountable executive, and accountable domain owner |
| Baseline and case mix | Current outcome quality, routing and repair load, cost basis, and the mix of normal and exceptional cases |
| Alternative design | Comparison with policy simplification, process redesign, or deterministic automation |
| Agent scope and authority | Actions the agent may propose or execute, prohibited transitions, exception owner, override owner, and stop owner |
| Applicable controls | Named owners for risk, security, privacy, AI, data location, sector rules, labor obligations, and supplier controls |
| Evidence basis | Correctly closed cases, control failures, adoption, residual judgment, and total cost per correctly closed case |
| Funded capacity | Sponsor, budget, domain time, technical capacity, people participation, risk support, and procurement support |
| Workforce commitment | Affected roles, learning time, progression and pay changes, redeployment options, participation, and exit support |
| Local acceptance conditions | Predeclared conditions for continuing, changing scope, or considering organization redesign |
| Reversal conditions | Events that trigger a stop, responsible owner, recovery route, supplier exit, and treatment of people already affected |
| Review and sign-off | Fixed decision date, evidence period, open uncertainties, and named signatories |

The board is not deciding whether an agent can complete an impressive task. It is deciding whether a complete operating system has earned a different distribution of work and authority. If the record is incomplete, the organization should keep learning without changing the hierarchy. If the evidence holds, leaders can remove proven coordination work, move ownership toward the domain, and change only the part of the structure that the evidence supports.

That is the architecture of an AI-first company: technical judgment distributed across the business, agents working inside explicit boundaries, and organizational change tied to evidence and responsibility. The chart comes after those commitments because it is their consequence.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Before changing the org chart, require one signed record covering the baseline, alternatives, agent authority, complete-workflow evidence, funded capacity, workforce commitments, local acceptance conditions, and reversal plan. The hierarchy changes only where that record supports it.</p>
</div>
