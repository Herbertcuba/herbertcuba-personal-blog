An AI-first company begins with two linked propositions. Every role acquires a technical dimension, and organizations can become flatter when AI agents absorb enough execution and coordination for ownership to move closer to the work. The first proposition deserves action now. The second remains a hypothesis that each workflow must prove.

[In a DECODE video interview published on 1 June 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ&t=343s), Arvind KC, chief people officer at OpenAI, the company behind the generative AI assistant ChatGPT, said that “every role as we go into the future is fundamentally a technical role.” He immediately grounded the claim in domain expertise and [later described innovation inside OpenAI as bottom-up](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8atlQX8HTwQ). This is an insider’s account of one AI company, so it gives us a direction to examine rather than proof that other companies can safely remove management layers.

My position is deliberately uneven: spread technical judgment across the business, then make any structural change wait for operating evidence. AI-first should describe how the company runs complete work. Counting isolated tasks that now include a model tells us very little about the operating model.

<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Distributed technical judgment is an immediate capability decision. A flatter organization is a separate structural decision that must be earned workflow by workflow.</p>
</div>

## Technical judgment moves to the edge

Technical judgment is the ability to understand what an AI system can do reliably, where its output needs checking, and when a human must take over. For most people, this is a judgment skill rather than a programming job. They need to shape work, evidence, and escalation around the real limits of the technology.

That changes expertise inside each function. A salesperson must recognize when an AI-generated proposal contains an unsupported claim. Finance must distinguish a routine policy match from an exception with material consequences. Operations, marketing, and support teams need to know when fluent output hides weak evidence or a customer situation falls outside the approved path. The domain expert remains responsible for meaning; the technical dimension is knowing how to direct and verify the system doing part of the work.

This is where bottom-up ownership becomes practical. People closest to a customer, policy, or operational constraint can improve the workflow because they understand both the domain and the agent’s boundaries. Seen from the corporate center, many local changes may look like disorder. That apparent chaos becomes defensible when leadership sets shared controls and each domain has visible authority for its outcomes.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Technical judgment combines domain expertise with a working knowledge of AI reliability, verification, and escalation. It lets improvement begin near the work without turning every employee into a developer.</p>
</div>

## The Retrofit Trap lives between tasks

*The AI First Company: Engineering Your Business to Thrive in the AI Economy*, [a 2026 business book by Benjamin Simkin](https://www.amazon.co.uk/dp/B0H45WZRX5), calls it the “Retrofit Trap”: placing AI inside an existing structure instead of rebuilding work around what the technology makes possible. The trap becomes visible in the handoffs surrounding an impressive demo.

Consider supplier onboarding. An [AI agent](https://openai.com/index/new-tools-for-building-agents/) is a software system that can use context and approved tools to carry a task across several steps. It might read company documents, compare them with policy, request missing evidence, and prepare a recommendation. Yet the operating model has barely changed if people still copy the result into another system, chase approvals by email, discover missing checks late, and repair cases that the agent routed incorrectly.

Traditional hierarchy carries much of this coordination. Managers collect information, interpret policy, allocate attention, approve exceptions, and settle conflicts between functions. An agent-supported workflow can absorb status gathering, routine follow-up, evidence assembly, and predictable routing. Named humans must still own policy choices, consequential exceptions, accountability, coaching, and conflicts that cannot be resolved by applying a rule.

A layer becomes removable only when its work has actually gone away. Moving that work into hidden review queues or after-the-fact repair creates a thinner chart and a more fragile company.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Faster task output is not an AI-first operating model. Structural change becomes plausible when the surrounding information, routing, and repair work also declines while human authority remains visible.</p>
</div>

## Evidence decides whether the company can flatten

The unit of proof should be a correctly closed case: a complete supplier-onboarding case that ends in an authorized activation, a justified rejection, or a documented human exception, with the required controls intact. Measuring only document-review speed says little about the workflow and even less about the hierarchy supporting it.

Before a trial begins, the executive sponsor should agree an authorization record with five lines:

- **Outcome:** correctly closed cases across the declared mix of routine and difficult work, with independent review of quality.
- **Remaining human work:** routing touches, waiting time, repair effort, and judgment that still cannot be delegated.
- **Control:** unauthorized actions, missed checks, complete audit records, and successful stop and recovery tests.
- **Economics:** model, integration, supervision, failure handling, and change costs across the whole case.
- **Dependency and reversibility:** where data is stored and processed, supplier licensing, substitution or exit rights, and the ability to move the workflow without losing its records.

The trial design also needs to expose retries, repeated runs, unusual cases, and the observed spread of failures rather than hiding them inside an average. Acceptance levels should be set in advance for that workflow’s risk. They are local management choices, not established industry benchmarks. The evidence available for this article contains no Swedish or Norwegian production case that provides a safe numerical threshold for removing a management layer, so one local result cannot be inflated into a general rule.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Judge complete cases across outcome, hidden human work, control, full cost, and reversibility. Predeclare the local acceptance test, because no verified Nordic benchmark makes delayering automatically safe.</p>
</div>

## The replacement is governed domain ownership

Start with a domain map that assigns every workflow outcome to a business owner. A control owner defines policy and decides who may approve exceptions and overrides, while technical and data owners remain responsible for the systems and records. Stop authority belongs to a named person, along with clear conditions for restart. Their force comes from allocated authority rather than committee labels.

The boundary between agent and ordinary software matters. Ordinary software should hold the authoritative state, meaning the official record of where a case stands, and enforce permissions and allowed transitions with fixed rules. The agent can interpret supplier documents and propose the next action, but the system must reject a jump from “sanctions review pending” to “active supplier,” regardless of how persuasive the model’s explanation sounds.

Consequential actions also need idempotency. [RFC 9110, the 2022 standard that defines core behavior for the web’s HTTP protocol](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-9.2.2), uses the term for an operation whose intended effect stays the same when an identical request is repeated. In this workflow, a retry must not create a second supplier record or duplicate an approval. Audit records should show the evidence used, policy applied, proposed action, approver where required, and actual result; override, stop, and rollback paths should be tested rather than assumed.

These controls come from established software practice. Their new organizational role is to form the authorization boundary within which people at the edge can move faster and own more.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Flatter does not mean ownerless. Domain accountability, explicit decision rights, software-enforced state and permissions, and tested stop paths replace coordination by hierarchy with governed autonomy.</p>
</div>

## Meanwhile in sci-fi

<p class="scifi__film">Alien (1979)</p>
<p>The crew of the commercial spacecraft Nostromo believes it understands the mission. Its computer, Mother, is also enforcing a concealed corporate order that gives recovery of the alien organism priority over the crew.</p>

The mapping is concealed authority. A system can behave consistently and still be ungovernable to its operators when its true owner, priorities, and override path are hidden. An enterprise agent therefore needs a visible mandate, named human authority, and an auditable way to stop what the system is doing.

## The pyramid carries careers too

Coordination-heavy roles carry more than routing. They often combine approval work with coaching, conflict resolution, professional development, and the judgment that holds a team together. If routine routing disappears, those responsibilities must be separated and reassigned before a layer is removed.

The career system also changes. Many companies use team size and movement up the management pyramid as signals of progression, status, and pay. A flatter model needs credible expert and domain-owner paths with comparable recognition, along with new ways for junior employees to learn when fewer managers are available as daily translators of the organization. Otherwise, distributed ownership can expand while human ambition is squeezed into a career model built for the old pyramid.

The transition therefore belongs in the operating design. Leaders should publish which work is changing, fund learning during paid time, create real redeployment capacity, and explain the criteria affecting roles, pay, and progression. In a Nordic setting, early consultation with employee representatives under applicable national rules and collective agreements is part of responsible change. Where redeployment is unavailable, the process still needs clear criteria and fair support.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Removing coordination work also changes coaching, learning, status, and pay. A defensible AI-first design funds new career paths and handles consultation, redeployment, and separation as core architecture decisions.</p>
</div>

## The org chart follows the authorization decision

The practical next step is to choose one bounded, cross-functional workflow and name the executive investment-and-risk forum that will decide whether to scale, extend, or stop it. Before the trial, that forum should approve the case mix, evidence record, decision-right map, supplier exit path, and workforce commitments. After the trial, it should ask whether coordination demand truly fell without an unacceptable rise in hidden labor, control failure, cost, or unresolved judgment.

Only then does a change to the hierarchy become board-defensible. The board’s decision concerns a specific redistribution of work and authority whose effects can be inspected, stopped, and reversed. A claim about managers becoming obsolete cannot substitute for that evidence.

That is the architecture of an AI-first company: technical judgment distributed to the domains, coordination removed where evidence supports it, and human accountability made more explicit as the organization becomes flatter. Until a workflow meets that standard, redesign the work and leave the reporting lines alone.

<div class="chapter-tldr">
<span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span>
<p>Take a bounded workflow, named owners, a predeclared evidence test, supplier reversibility, and a funded people plan to a scale-or-stop forum before proposing delayering. The org chart should record proven operating change, not anticipate it.</p>
</div>
