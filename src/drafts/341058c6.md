I tried to map out what an AI-first company actually looks like after reading Benjamin Simkin's *The AI First Company* twice. The first read gave me a manual. The second gave me a warning.

Simkin's core observation cuts deep: the AI-first competitors in your market are building a structural advantage that compounds. They asked a different question from the start.

Most organizations ask "how can AI improve what we already do?" That question anchors you to the existing architecture. Every answer becomes a bolt-on. The AI-first question is: "If I were building this company today, from zero, knowing what AI can do, would it look like this?"

I think most organizations are asking the wrong question. The cost of that mistake is higher than most leaders realize.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>What if AI-first is an organizational architecture decision?</p></div>

## The retrofit trap

Simkin tells a story about two agency owners. Marcus runs a 15-person shop doing $3M a year. He bought eleven AI tools over four months and bolted them onto his existing workflow. Revenue stayed flat.

Sarah runs a 12-person agency doing $2.5M. She asked the clean-sheet question instead. She redesigned her offer, her delivery, her onboarding, her reporting. AI handled the first 80% of content production. Her team shifted from production to quality control and strategy. She cut headcount from 12 to 7, raised salaries for those who stayed, and watched margins climb from 22% to over 45%.

Same market. Same clients. Completely different company under the hood.

An OpenAI engineer, in an interview with *The Information* about the company's internal pace, put it this way: "The pace is intense. It is something that I have not seen before. What takes months or years somewhere else gets done in days." I think the org model creates the speed: bottom-up innovation and individual empowerment, with decisions pushed to the people closest to the work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Bolting AI onto an old structure makes the structure worse. Redesigning around AI makes the organization faster than you thought possible.</p></div>

## Three levels, one gate

I have written before about the Three Levels of AI Acceleration. Level 1 is individual productivity. Level 2 is team workflows. Level 3 is agentic production, where AI systems execute end-to-end processes with human oversight at decision points.

Simkin's AI-first company maps directly to Level 3. His Context Library and Skills-and-Agents stack are what make agentic workflows possible: context as the asset, agents as the executors. But I think Simkin gets one thing right that most frameworks miss. The gate to Level 3 is organizational.

Enterprise readiness is gated by decision rights. In my experience decision rights is the phrase that gets thrown around without ever being pinned down. So here is a working definition: a decision right is the named person who owns the outcome of a choice, paired with the authority to override the agent when the choice matters. Two clauses. Who owns it, and who can stop it. Without those two clauses answered for every workflow an AI touches, the organization cannot move past Level 2, because nobody knows who is on the hook when the agent acts.

That is an organizational design decision, and most companies have not made it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first maps to Level 3 agentic workflows. The barrier is decision rights: a named owner of the outcome and named authority to override the agent.</p></div>

## Months rather than years

Simkin's most provocative claim is about timing. Re-engineering takes months rather than years. In my experience Simkin is right about the direction and less certainly right about the exact timeline.

The months-rather-than-years timeline is about organizational redesign speed. The technology is ready. What takes time is the decision to redesign, the authority to cut, and the willingness to let go of structures that no longer serve the mission.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>I'm less sure than Simkin about the exact timeline, but the direction is right. The constraint is decision speed.</p></div>

## The architecture question

So what does an AI-first company actually look like in practice? Every role reasons about AI at the altitude where their decisions live. Workflows are designed around AI capabilities with human judgment at the points where it matters. And the owner has shifted from operator to architect.

I think the difference between designing the system and living inside someone else's design is the difference between a shipwright sketching a hull on a slipway and a stoker shoveling coal in someone else's engine room. The question is whether you will design it or retrofit it. One path takes months. The other takes years and delivers less.

My take: the window for clean-sheet design is shorter than most leaders think. The technology moves fast. The compounding advantage moves faster.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Design it or retrofit it. The window is shorter than you think, and hedging slows you down.</p></div>

## Where an existing company starts

Most leaders reading this are not starting from zero. They run an organization with legacy revenue, a full headcount, and a board that wants certainty. The clean-sheet question is the right one to ask, but it is the wrong one to start with.

The first move I would make is a small one. Pick one workflow where the AI already does the heavy lifting well today, and ask two questions about it: who owns the outcome when the agent is wrong, and who has the authority to stop it. Write the two names down. Make them accountable in a review. That is a single workflow's decision rights, made operational. It takes a meeting, not a redesign.

Then pick the second workflow. Then the third. The point is not to redesign the org chart. The point is to install the smallest possible version of the new operating model inside the old one, in places where the cost of being wrong is contained and the learning is observable. After three or four of these, the pattern is visible enough that leadership can see what an AI-first version of the whole organization would look like. Then you can ask the clean-sheet question with evidence behind it instead of a slide.

I think this is the migration path that actually works. Bottom-up, narrow, decision-rights-first, and visible. Not a moonshot reorg, but a series of contained experiments that earn the right to scale.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>You don't start with the clean-sheet redesign. You start with one workflow, name the owner and the override, and repeat until the new operating model becomes visible from the bottom up.</p></div>

## Closing

I don't know which companies will win. But I think the experience of working at a place designed for AI-first will prepare you for any role in the future. The intensity is high. The ones who thrive there tend to be the ones who would rather rebuild the machine than operate it.

If you cannot rebuild the machine from scratch, rebuild the smallest piece you can. The architecture question is the same either way.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Design it or retrofit it, one workflow at a time. The compounding advantage belongs to the companies that start now, not the ones that wait for the perfect reorg.</p></div>
