I tried to map out what an AI-first company actually looks like after reading Benjamin Simkin's *The AI First Company* twice. The first read gave me a manual. The second gave me a warning.

Simkin's core observation cuts deep: the AI-first competitors in your market are building a structural advantage that compounds. They asked a different question from the start.

Most organizations ask "how can AI improve what we already do?" That question anchors you to the existing architecture. Every answer becomes a bolt-on. The AI-first question is: "If I were building this company today, from zero, knowing what AI can do, would it look like this?"

I think most organizations are asking the wrong question. The cost of that mistake is higher than most leaders realize.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>What if AI-first is an organizational architecture decision?</p></div>

## The retrofit trap

Simkin tells a story about two agency owners. Marcus runs a 15-person shop doing $3M a year. He bought eleven AI tools over four months and bolted them onto his existing workflow. Revenue stayed flat, and two of his senior people quit because the tools kept breaking the briefs they were responsible for.

Sarah runs a 12-person agency doing $2.5M. She asked the clean-sheet question instead. She redesigned her offer, her delivery, her onboarding, her reporting. AI handled the first 80% of content production. Her team shifted from production to quality control and strategy. She cut headcount from 12 to 7, raised salaries for those who stayed, and watched margins climb from 22% to over 45%.

Same market. Same clients. Completely different company under the hood.

I saw this at OpenAI. The pace is something I have not seen before. What takes months or years somewhere else gets done in days. I think the org model creates the speed: bottom-up innovation and individual empowerment, with no committees gating every decision.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Bolting AI onto an old structure makes the structure worse. Redesigning around AI makes the organization faster than you thought possible.</p></div>

## Three levels, one gate

I have written before about the Three Levels of AI Acceleration. Level 1 is individual productivity. Level 2 is team workflows. Level 3 is agentic production, where AI systems execute end-to-end processes with human oversight at decision points.

Simkin's AI-first company maps directly to Level 3. His Context Library and Skills-and-Agents stack are what make agentic workflows possible: context as the asset, agents as the executors. But I think Simkin gets one thing right that most frameworks miss. The gate to Level 3 is organizational.

Enterprise readiness is gated by decision rights. Who decides what the AI system should optimize for? Who owns the outcome when the system fails? Who has authority to override the agent? Most organizations cannot answer these questions because their decision rights were designed for a pre-agentic world.

At OpenAI, this plays out in a specific way. Every role is a technical role. And every role has a suffix: "agent manager." You need to wield agents and use them in a way that significantly amplifies you or your team. That is an organizational design decision.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first maps to Level 3 agentic workflows. The barrier is whether your organization has redesigned decision rights for a world where agents execute.</p></div>

## Months rather than years

Simkin's most provocative claim is about timing. Re-engineering takes months rather than years. In my experience Simkin is right about the direction and less certainly right about the exact timeline.

The months-rather-than-years timeline is about organizational redesign speed. The technology is ready. What takes time is the decision to redesign, the authority to cut, and the willingness to let go of structures that no longer serve the mission.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>I'm less sure than Simkin about the exact timeline, but the direction is right. The constraint is decision speed.</p></div>

## The architecture question

So what does an AI-first company actually look like in practice? Every role reasons about AI at the altitude where their decisions live. Workflows are designed around AI capabilities with human judgment at the points where it matters. And the owner has shifted from operator to architect.

I think the difference between designing the system and living inside someone else's design is the difference between Neo seeing the Matrix and everyone still plugged in. The question is whether you will design it or retrofit it. One path takes months. The other takes years and delivers less.

My take: the window for clean-sheet design is shorter than most leaders think. The technology moves fast. The compounding advantage moves faster.

OpenAI is all in on AGI for the benefit of humanity. Going all in on something creates incredible focus. Most companies cannot do this. They have legacy revenue, legacy structures, legacy people. But the principle holds: the more you hedge, the slower you move.

I don't know which companies will win. But I think the experience of working at a place designed for AI-first will prepare you for any role in the future. The intensity is high. The ones who thrive there tend to be the ones who would rather rebuild the machine than operate it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Design it or retrofit it. The window is shorter than you think, and hedging slows you down.</p></div>
