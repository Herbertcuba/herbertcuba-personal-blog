I tried to map out what an AI-first company actually looks like after reading Benjamin Simkin's *The AI First Company* twice. The first read gave me a manual. The second gave me a warning.

The AI-first competitors in a market are building a structural advantage that compounds. Simkin's book was the first place I saw the argument framed that way.

Most organizations ask "how can AI improve what we already do?" That question anchors you to the existing architecture. Every answer becomes a bolt-on. The more useful question is the clean-sheet version: "If I were building this company today, from zero, knowing what AI can do, would it look like this?"

I think most organizations are asking the wrong question. The mistake locks each AI investment into structures built for a different era, raising the cost of every later redesign.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>What if AI-first is an organizational architecture decision?</p></div>

## The retrofit trap

The retrofit trap shows up whenever a company buys AI tools to speed up a workflow it has no intention of redesigning. The tools sit on top of the old process. Output per person may rise for a quarter, but the structure stays the same, so the gains flatten.

The clean-sheet approach asks what the company would look like if it were designed around AI from the start. The same market and the same clients can support a very different organization under the hood. Roles shift from production to judgment. Margins expand because the cost structure is no longer the same.

Arvind KC, OpenAI's Chief Product Officer, reflecting on his first months there, put it this way: "The pace at OpenAI... is intense. It is something that I have not seen before. What takes months or years somewhere else gets done in days." I think the org model creates the speed: bottom-up innovation and individual empowerment, with decisions pushed to the people closest to the work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Bolting AI onto an old structure makes the structure worse. Redesigning around AI unlocks a velocity the old structure cannot reach.</p></div>

## Three levels, one gate

I have written before about the Three Levels of AI Acceleration. Level 1 is Chat: individual productivity in a synchronous prompt-and-response loop. Level 2 is Agentic: team workflows where you dispatch multi-step tasks to an agent and it runs async. Level 3 is Autonomous, where the system carries persistent memory across sessions, behaves proactively, and acts on its own initiative without waiting to be prompted.

In Simkin's model, a Context Library, skills, and agents can provide the foundation for Level 3. Context gives the system something to remember, while skills and agents give it ways to act. They do not, by themselves, create persistence or unprompted initiative. I think Simkin gets one thing right that most frameworks miss. The gate to Level 3 is organizational.

Enterprise readiness is gated by decision rights. In my experience decision rights is the phrase that gets thrown around without ever being pinned down. So here is a working definition: a decision right is the named person who owns the outcome of a choice, paired with the authority to override the agent when the choice matters. Two clauses. Who owns it, and who can stop it. Without those two clauses answered for every workflow an AI touches, the organization cannot move past Level 2, because nobody knows who is on the hook when the agent acts.

That is an organizational design decision, and most companies have not made it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Simkin's model can provide the foundation for Level 3 Autonomous systems, but the components alone do not guarantee persistence or unprompted initiative. The barrier is decision rights: a named owner of the outcome and named authority to override the agent.</p></div>

## Visible gains before separation

Simkin maps his formal roadmap across three horizons: the first 90 days, the first year, and the first three years. Visible gains can arrive within months, while full structural separation is a multi-year project. In my experience he is right about the direction, even if each company will move at a different pace.

Months may be enough for early organizational redesign. The harder work takes longer. Decision authority has to spread. Obsolete structures have to be cut. The new system has to stop feeling new.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Early wins land inside a quarter or two. The structural rebuild runs on a multi-year clock, and the constraint is decision speed.</p></div>

## The architecture question

So what does an AI-first company actually look like in practice? The owner has shifted from operator to architect. Workflows are designed around AI capabilities, with human judgment reserved for the points where it matters. People spend less time executing and more time designing the system that executes.

I think the difference between designing the system and living inside someone else's design is the difference between a shipwright sketching a hull on a slipway and a stoker shoveling coal in someone else's engine room. The question is how you will design it. Early changes pay off fast. The deep rebuild takes years, which is exactly why the design question cannot wait for the rebuild to finish.

My take: the clean-sheet window is shorter than it feels. The technology moves fast. The compounding advantage moves faster.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Design it or retrofit it. The window is short, and hedging erodes it.</p></div>

## Where an existing company starts

Most leaders reading this are not starting from zero. They run an organization with legacy revenue, a full headcount, and a board that wants certainty. The clean-sheet question is the right one to ask, but it is the wrong one to start with.

The first move I would make is a small one. Pick one workflow where the AI already does the heavy lifting well today, and ask two questions about it: who owns the outcome when the agent is wrong, and who has the authority to stop it. Write the two names down. Make them accountable in a review. That is a single workflow's decision rights, made operational. One focused meeting is enough to put it in place.

Then pick the second workflow. Then the third. Install the smallest possible version of the new operating model inside the old one, in places where the cost of being wrong is contained and the learning is observable. After three or four of these, the pattern is visible enough that leadership can see what an AI-first version of the whole organization would look like. Then you can ask the clean-sheet question with evidence behind it instead of a slide.

I think this is the migration path that actually works: contained experiments that begin from the bottom, define decision rights, make progress visible, and earn the right to scale.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Start with one workflow, name the owner and the override, and repeat until the new operating model becomes visible from the bottom up.</p></div>

## Closing

I don't know which companies will win. But I think the experience of working at a place designed for AI-first will prepare you for any role in the future. The intensity is high. The ones who thrive there tend to be the ones who would rather rebuild the machine than operate it.

If you cannot rebuild the machine from scratch, rebuild the smallest piece you can. The architecture question is the same either way.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Design it or retrofit it, one workflow at a time. Starting now creates a compounding advantage with every workflow redesigned.</p></div>
