I tried to map out what an AI-first company actually looks like after reading Benjamin Simkin's *The AI First Company* twice. The first read gave me a manual. The second gave me a warning.

Simkin's core observation cuts deep: the AI-first competitors in your market are building a structural advantage that compounds over time. They asked a different question from the start.

Most organizations ask "how can AI improve what we already do?" That question anchors you to the existing architecture. Every answer becomes a bolt-on. The AI-first question is: "If I were building this company today, from zero, knowing what AI can do, would it look like this?"

I think most organizations are asking the wrong question. The cost of that mistake is higher than most leaders realize.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first is an organizational architecture decision. The companies that understand this are redesigning in months rather than years.</p></div>

## The retrofit trap

Simkin tells a story about two agency owners. Marcus runs a 15-person shop doing $3M a year. He bought eleven AI tools over four months and bolted them onto his existing workflow. Revenue stayed flat.

Sarah runs a 12-person agency doing $2.5M. She asked the clean-sheet question instead. She redesigned her offer, her delivery, her onboarding, her reporting. AI handled the first 80% of content production. Her team shifted from production to quality control and strategy. She cut headcount from 12 to 7, raised salaries for those who stayed, and watched margins climb from 22% to over 45%.

Same market. Same clients. Completely different company under the hood.

My take: Marcus optimized a structure that should have been replaced. Sarah replaced the structure. That distinction sounds obvious when you read it. It is remarkably hard to see when you are inside it. Like Neo in The Matrix, you can feel something is wrong but you can't explain it. You keep optimizing within the structure because the alternative is admitting the architecture itself needs to change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Bolting AI onto an old structure makes things worse. The retrofitted company optimizes within constraints it should have removed entirely.</p></div>

## Three levels, one gate

I have written before about the Three Levels of AI Acceleration. Level 1 is individual productivity. Level 2 is team workflows. Level 3 is agentic production, where AI systems execute end-to-end processes with human oversight at decision points.

Simkin's AI-first company maps directly to Level 3. His Three-File Architecture (Context, Skills, Routines) is the operational stack that makes agentic workflows possible. But I think Simkin gets one thing right that most frameworks miss: the gate to Level 3 is organizational. The technology is already there.

Enterprise readiness is gated by decision rights. Who decides what the AI system should optimize for? Who owns the outcome when the system fails? Who has authority to override the agent? Most organizations cannot answer these questions because their decision rights were designed for a pre-agentic world.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first maps to Level 3 agentic workflows. The barrier is whether your organization has redesigned decision rights for a world where agents execute. The technology is ready.</p></div>

## Months rather than years

Simkin's most provocative claim is about timing. Re-engineering takes months rather than years. I think this is true, but I'm not sure he's right about the specific timelines. What I think he's right about is the direction.

The months-rather-than-years timeline is about organizational redesign speed. The technology is ready. What takes time is the decision to redesign, the authority to cut, and the willingness to let go of structures that no longer serve the mission.

Simkin puts it sharply: being late means waiting for clarity. And clarity is what lets you re-engineer correctly instead of re-engineering twice. The companies that build well are usually the ones that built second, after watching others build wrong.

I saw this pattern at a company I advised last year. They spent eight months evaluating AI tools, running pilots, building committees. A competitor redesigned their entire delivery model in twelve weeks. The competitor now operates with 40% fewer people and better margins. I could be wrong about the causality, but the correlation is hard to ignore.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Re-engineering takes months rather than years because the constraint is decision speed. Clarity about the target architecture is what separates fast movers from fast re-builders. I'm less certain about the exact timelines than Simkin is, but the direction feels right.</p></div>

## The architecture question

I tried to map out what an AI-first company actually looks like in practice. It is a company where every role reasons about AI at the altitude where their decisions live. Where workflows are designed around AI capabilities with human judgment at the points where it matters. Where the owner has shifted from operator to architect.

The question is whether you will design it or retrofit it. One path takes months. The other takes years and delivers less.

My take: the window for clean-sheet design is shorter than most leaders think. The technology moves fast, but the compounding advantage moves faster.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Design it in months or retrofit it for years. The compounding advantage belongs to the designers.</p></div>
