I tried to map out what an AI-first company actually looks like after reading Benjamin Simkin's *The AI First Company* twice. The first read gave me a manual. The second gave me a warning.

The AI-first competitors in a market are building a structural advantage that compounds. Simkin's book was the first place I saw the argument framed that way.

Most organizations ask "how can AI improve what we already do?" That question anchors you to the company as it already exists. Every answer becomes a bolt-on. The more useful question is the clean-sheet version: "If I were building this company today, from zero, knowing what AI can do, would it look like this?"

I think most organizations are asking the wrong question. The mistake locks each AI investment into an organization built for a different era, and it makes every later change more expensive.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>What if AI-first is a decision about how the company is built, not which tools it buys?</p></div>

## The retrofit trap

A company falls into the retrofit trap when it buys AI tools to speed up a process it has no intention of redesigning. The tools get layered over the old way of working. You might see output per person climb for a quarter or two. Then it levels off, because nothing underneath changed.

The clean-sheet version asks what the company would look like if you built it around AI from day one. Your market and your clients stay put, but the organization serving them can look nothing like the one you have now. Instead of a team that produces the work and a manager who checks it, you get people who review what the system produced and decide what happens next. Margins widen because the cost base underneath them changed.

Arvind KC, OpenAI's Chief People Officer, reportedly said in a recent interview, reflecting on his first months there: "The pace at OpenAI... is intense. It is something that I have not seen before. What takes months or years somewhere else gets done in days." I think the org model creates the speed: bottom-up innovation and individual empowerment, with decisions pushed to the people closest to the work.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Bolting AI onto the old org makes its weaknesses more expensive. Starting clean unlocks a speed the retrofit never reaches.</p></div>

## Three levels, one gate

I have written before about the Three Levels of AI Acceleration. Level 1 is Chat: individual productivity in a synchronous prompt-and-response loop. Level 2 is Agentic: team processes where you hand off multi-step tasks to an agent and it runs async. Level 3 is Autonomous, where the system carries persistent memory across sessions, behaves proactively, and acts on its own initiative without waiting to be prompted.

In Simkin's model, a Context Library, skills, and agents can provide the foundation for Level 3. Context gives the system something to remember, while skills and agents give it ways to act. They do not, by themselves, create persistence or unprompted initiative. I think Simkin gets one thing right that most frameworks miss. The gate to Level 3 is organizational.

Enterprise readiness is gated by decision rights. In my experience decision rights is the phrase that gets thrown around without ever being pinned down. So here is a working definition: a decision right is the named person who owns the outcome of a choice, paired with the authority to override the agent when the choice matters. Two clauses. Who owns it, and who can stop it. Without those two clauses answered for every workflow an AI touches, the organization cannot move past Level 2, because nobody knows who is on the hook when the agent acts.

That is a decision about how the company is organized, and most companies have not made it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Simkin's model can provide the foundation for Level 3 Autonomous systems, but the components alone do not guarantee persistence or unprompted initiative. The barrier is decision rights: a named owner of the outcome and named authority to override the agent.</p></div>

## Visible gains before separation

Simkin maps his formal roadmap across three horizons: the first 90 days, the first year, and the first three years. Visible gains can arrive within months, while the full separation of new from old is a multi-year project. In my experience he is right about the direction, even if each company will move at a different pace.

Months may be enough for the first rounds of reorganizing. The harder work takes longer. Decision authority has to spread. Obsolete structures have to be cut. And the new system needs time to stop feeling new.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Early wins land inside a quarter or two. The deep changes run on a multi-year clock, and the constraint is decision speed.</p></div>

## The operator becomes the architect

So what does an AI-first company actually look like in practice? The owner has shifted from operator to architect. Work gets organized around what AI can do, and human judgment is reserved for the points where it matters. People spend less time executing and more time deciding how the execution should happen.

I think working in an AI-first company feels more like being the shipwright than the stoker. You spend your days deciding how the thing should be built, not shoveling coal in someone else's engine room. The early changes pay for themselves quickly. The deep rebuild takes years, and that is exactly why waiting feels so expensive.

My take: the clean-sheet window is shorter than it feels. The technology moves fast, and the edge for whoever moves first keeps growing.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>You either build around AI or bolt it onto what exists. The window for the first option is shorter than it looks.</p></div>

## Where an existing company starts

Most leaders reading this are not starting from zero. They run an organization with legacy revenue, a full headcount, and a board that wants certainty. The clean-sheet question is the right one to ask, but it is the wrong one to start with.

The first move I would make is a small one. Pick one process where the AI already does the heavy lifting well today, and ask two questions about it: who owns the outcome when the agent is wrong, and who has the authority to stop it. Write the two names down. Make them accountable in a review. That is decision rights for one process, made operational. One focused meeting is enough to put it in place.

Then pick a second one. Then a third. Install the smallest possible version of the new operating model inside the old one, in places where the cost of being wrong is contained and the learning is observable. After three or four of these, the pattern is visible enough that leadership can see what an AI-first version of the whole organization would look like. Then you can ask the clean-sheet question with evidence behind it instead of a slide.

I think this is the migration path I would bet on. Small contained experiments, starting at the bottom. Each one has decision rights attached to it, and each one is visible enough that the next one gets easier to approve.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Start with one workflow, name the owner and the override, and repeat until the new operating model becomes visible from the bottom up.</p></div>

## Closing

I don't know which companies will win. But I think the experience of working at a place built for AI-first will prepare you for almost any role after it. The pace there is not for everyone. The people who do well tend to be the ones who like building things more than running them.

If you cannot rebuild the machine from scratch, start with the smallest piece you can. The question underneath is the same either way.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Start small, one piece at a time. Every process you redo adds to the lead.</p></div>
