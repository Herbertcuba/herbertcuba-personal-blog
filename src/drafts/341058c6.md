Benjamin Simkin wrote a book called *The AI First Company*. I read it twice. The first time as a business manual. The second time as a warning.

Simkin's core observation cuts deep: the AI-first competitors in your market are building a structural advantage that compounds over time. They asked a different question.

Most organizations ask "how can AI improve what we already do?" That question anchors you to the existing architecture. Every answer becomes a bolt-on. The AI-first question is: "If I were building this company today, from zero, knowing what AI can do, would it look like this?"

I think most organizations are asking the wrong question. The cost of that mistake is higher than most leaders realize.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first is not a technology decision. It is an organizational architecture decision. The companies that understand this are redesigning in months, not years.</p></div>

## The retrofit trap

Simkin tells a story about two agency owners. Marcus bought eleven AI tools and spent four months bolting them onto his existing workflow. Revenue stayed flat. Sarah asked the clean-sheet question instead. She redesigned her offer, her delivery, her onboarding, her reporting. AI handled the first 80% of content production. Her team shifted from production to quality control and strategy. She cut headcount from 12 to 7, raised salaries, and watched margins climb from 22% to over 45%.

Same market. Same clients. Completely different company under the hood.

My take: Marcus optimized a structure that should have been replaced. Sarah replaced the structure. That distinction sounds obvious when you read it. It is remarkably hard to see when you are inside it. Like Neo in The Matrix, you can feel something is wrong but you can't explain it. You keep optimizing within the structure because the alternative is admitting the architecture itself needs to change.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Bolting AI onto an old structure makes things worse, not better. The retrofitted company optimizes within constraints it should have removed.</p></div>

## Three levels, one gate

I have written before about the Three Levels of AI Acceleration. Level 1 is individual productivity. Level 2 is team workflows. Level 3 is agentic production, where AI systems execute end-to-end processes with human oversight at decision points.

Simkin's AI-first company maps directly to Level 3. His Three-File Architecture (Context, Skills, Routines) is the operational stack that makes agentic workflows possible. But I think Simkin gets one thing right that most frameworks miss: the gate to Level 3 is organizational, not technical.

Enterprise readiness is gated by decision rights, not by technology. Who decides what the AI system should optimize for? Who owns the outcome when the system fails? Who has authority to override the agent? These are architecture questions, not engineering questions. Most organizations cannot answer them because their decision rights were designed for a pre-agentic world.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first maps to Level 3 agentic workflows. The barrier is not technical capability. It is whether your organization has redesigned decision rights for a world where agents execute.</p></div>

## Months, not years

Simkin's most provocative claim is about timing. Re-engineering takes months, not years. I think this is true, but the reason matters.

The months-not-years timeline is not about AI adoption speed. It is about organizational redesign speed. The technology is ready. What takes time is the decision to redesign, the authority to cut, and the willingness to let go of structures that no longer serve the mission.

Simkin puts it sharply: being late does not mean being passive. It means waiting for clarity. And clarity is what lets you re-engineer correctly instead of re-engineering twice. The companies that build well are usually the ones that built second, after watching others build wrong.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Re-engineering takes months, not years, because the constraint is decision speed, not technology readiness. Clarity about the target architecture is what separates fast movers from fast re-builders.</p></div>

## The architecture question

I tried to map out what an AI-first company actually looks like in practice. It is a company where every role reasons about AI at the altitude where their decisions live. Where workflows are designed around AI capabilities with human judgment at the points where it matters. Where the owner has shifted from operator to architect.

The question is not whether your company will become AI-first. The question is whether you will design it or retrofit it. One path takes months. The other takes years and delivers less.

My take: the window for clean-sheet design is shorter than most leaders think. The technology moves fast, but the compounding advantage moves faster.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI-first is an architecture choice, not an adoption choice. Design it in months or retrofit it for years. The compounding advantage belongs to the designers.</p></div>
