Damir Trokić asked on LinkedIn: if a consulting assignment that costs 100 hours at SEK 2,000, or SEK 200,000, could hypothetically be delivered in 20 hours, what happens to the invoice? It was an illustration, not a measurement.

I see two answers. The simple one is that we will still sell time, since in my view that is what consulting is, but we will spend it on significantly harder problems, things few can do today. The difficult one is that pricing will probably become more complex, because model choice, compute and calendar time start to count alongside human hours.

## The frontier moves before the hours shrink

Consider a randomized experiment with 758 BCG consultants, published in Organization Science in March 2026. On tasks within GPT-4's capabilities, consultants with AI completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with better quality. On a task outside them, they were less likely to answer correctly.

So AI does not turn every 100-hour engagement into 20. It changes where expert attention goes. Framing the problem, validating the result and owning the recommendation still take people. There is counterevidence too: in a small METR study, experienced open-source developers using early-2025 AI tools took 19% longer while believing they were faster. METR later said newer speedups were likely but could not measure them reliably.

My forecast is that time freed from routine analysis goes into investigations once impractical within a budget or deadline. The evidence makes that plausible, not certain.

## Three variables instead of one

Human hours, machine spend and calendar time are separate variables, and model pricing already reflects that. OpenAI charges different rates by model and token type, and its Flex processing is cheaper in exchange for slower responses and occasional unavailability.

Picture a hypothetical case. A manufacturer needs a recommendation before a supplier contract renews. AI cuts the manual document comparison, so the team can test twenty scenarios instead of three. Specialists still define constraints, chase contradictory records and own the recommendation. Faster processing costs more than the delay-tolerant option, and the client's approvals may remain the bottleneck.

Human hours fall, while total delivery cost could rise or fall. Client value rises only if the extra scenarios, or the earlier answer, change the decision.

## A price with more than one clock

None of this means hourly billing is dead, and fixed or outcome-linked pricing is not new. US federal procurement rules describe firm-fixed-price contracts with performance or delivery incentives. What changes is how many variables a fair price must reflect.

Clients will ask whether they are paying for expert judgment, model capacity or speed. I think the firms that do well will explain each plainly and price the combination that helps the client decide.

The bigger story, as I see it, is consulting taking on harder problems at a price that has to read more than one clock.
