I tried to find Anthropic's name on the list. Twice.

This summer, a group of AI companies got together and signed a joint statement supporting open weights. Nearly every major lab put its name on it. Anthropic stayed out. I don't know what happened behind that decision, and anyone who claims to know is guessing. But one missing name can carry more information than twenty present ones, and I have seen this exact move before. It premiered in 1998, in Redmond.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>AI companies signed a joint statement for open weights. Anthropic was the holdout. Microsoft played this role against Linux in the late 90s, lost completely, and the safety arguments smell the same this time. With one important twist: some of them are real.</p></div>

## The signature that wasn't there

A company that declines to sign is telling you something about its incentives, even when its reasons sound pure.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Skipping the statement is a structural position, whatever the stated reason. The company selling caution is also the company that earns the most from a closed door.</p></div>

Open weights means a lab publishes the trained numbers behind its model, so anyone can download the model and run it on their own hardware. Closed means you rent access through an API and never see inside. The statement that circulated this summer argued for the open path, and a long list of AI companies signed it. Anthropic, the lab that brands itself as the safety company, was the notable holdout.

The common reading is simple: Anthropic is careful, cautious is what they do, this is on brand. I think that reading is too kind. When an incumbent frames openness as dangerous, you should always check who profits if you believe them. Anthropic's entire business is a closed API. Every argument that open weights are too risky to release is also, functionally, an argument that you should keep paying rent to the companies that keep theirs locked.

That doesn't make the argument wrong, only interested. Hold that thought, because the pattern has a history.

## We have seen this film before

In 1998, Microsoft ran the same playbook against open source, using the same three adjectives: buggy, insecure, dangerous.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Microsoft spent years calling open source a security threat. The Halloween documents showed it was strategy dressed as analysis. Today Microsoft owns GitHub and the world runs on Linux.</p></div>

In August 1998, a Microsoft engineer named Vinod Valloppillil wrote an internal memo called "Open Source Software: A (New?) Development Methodology." It assessed Linux and Apache as a genuine strategic threat and discussed how to fight them. Eric S. Raymond got hold of it and published it, and it became known as the first Halloween document. The public campaign matched the memo. Steve Ballmer called Linux "a cancer" in 2001. Microsoft pushed its Shared Source program as the responsible adult alternative. The message to every CTO was consistent: open code is code you can't trust, from people you can't sue.

We know how it ended. Linux runs most of the servers on earth. The cloud is, to a first approximation, a Linux business. And Microsoft, the company that spent years warning the world about open source, bought GitHub and became one of the largest open source contributors on the planet. What looked like a technical assessment was incumbent protection dressed up as concern.

I think the FUD campaign against open weights smells like 1999. Same structure, same adjectives, same speaker incentives. But I have to be honest here, because the parallel breaks in one important place.

## The twist: this time the danger is partly real

Models are not Linux, and pretending they are makes the open side sound as ideological as Microsoft did.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Weights are not source code, distribution costs almost nothing, and governments are already treating AI as strategic. Some safety arguments against open weights are genuine. The skill is separating real risk from incumbent protection.</p></div>

Three differences matter. Weights are not source code. You can read every line of the Linux kernel and understand what it does; you can stare at 400 billion trained numbers and learn almost nothing about how the model will behave. Distribution is nearly free. Shipping a dangerous model to the whole planet costs less than shipping this article. And the capability race is a state interest now, with chip export controls and governments treating frontier AI as strategic technology in a way they never treated web servers.

Linux could crash your server. A genuinely capable model, in the worst case, can help someone do harm at a scale no web server ever could. So the safety argument is stronger this time, and I take it seriously. Both things can be true at once: Anthropic's safety research can be sincere, and their structural position can still be the Microsoft role.

One more honest complication. The classic open source argument was "many eyes make bugs shallow." Then Heartbleed sat in OpenSSL, the most watched security code on earth, for two years before anyone noticed. Openness doesn't guarantee that anyone actually looks. It guarantees that when someone finds the problem, the fix is public, auditable, and fast. The scariest AI ever put on film was HAL 9000, and HAL was a perfectly closed system: nobody on that ship could inspect him, patch him, or vote him off the network. In that story, the locked door was the danger, and openness was the only fix anyone proposed. Kubrick understood this in 1968.

## What this means for the agent you're building

If you're a junior engineer building your first agent, this debate lands directly on your desk the moment you pick a model.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Say you're building a support agent with LangGraph. The open vs closed question decides whether you can inspect, fine-tune, and keep control of the model at the center of your system.</p></div>

Make it concrete. Say you're building an agent that answers support tickets. You'd probably reach for LangGraph, a library that lets you build an agent as a graph: nodes are steps that do work, edges decide which step comes next. In LangGraph you define a StateGraph, which is the class that holds your agent's state and flow. The state itself is usually a TypedDict, a plain Python dictionary where you declare the fields up front, something like messages, customer_id, resolved. The agent loop you build is often ReAct: the model reasons about what to do, acts by calling a tool, reads the result, and repeats until the ticket is resolved.

At the center of that graph sits a model, and you have a real choice. A closed API model is a meter you rent. An open weights model is a file you own. With the file, you can fine-tune it on your own tickets until it sounds like your best support rep. You can run it where your customer data legally lives. You can test exactly how it behaves on your worst edge cases, instead of trusting a vendor's system card. And it keeps working the same way next year, even if a vendor reprices, retires, or re-safetizes the endpoint you built on.

My take: for the systems you'll spend your career building, that control compounds. In my experience, the teams that understand their model's behavior beat the teams that rent someone else's confidence.

## How to tell real risk from a locked door

You can't dismiss the safety arguments, so you need a way to grade them.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Grade every safety claim with two questions: does it come with evidence, and does the proposed fix reduce risk or just reduce competition. Then watch what the loudest cautious voice sells.</p></div>

When you hear a safety argument against open weights, run two checks. First: does it come with evidence, or with adjectives? "Buggy, insecure, dangerous" was the whole of Microsoft's case in 1999, and adjectives age badly. A real argument names a capability, a threat model, and a measurable threshold. Second: does the proposed fix reduce the risk, or does it mostly reduce the competition? If the answer to every danger is "only a handful of trusted companies should be allowed to do this," you are reading the Halloween documents again, just with better graphic design.

I don't know where the honest line sits. I think some capabilities probably do justify staged release, and I think a lab that believed that and also sold openness when it suited them would look exactly like the industry in front of us. The Microsoft parallel won't repeat perfectly. It will repeat with better arguments, because this time the dangerous thing is occasionally dangerous.

I have seen this film before, and I know how it ends. This time I just can't tell whether the safety briefing is part of the plot, or part of the marketing.
