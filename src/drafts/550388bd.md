I tried to read the joint statement on open weights that most of the AI industry signed this summer. The list of signatories is long. One major lab is missing: Anthropic.

Open weights means you can download a model's trained parameters, the actual numbers that make it work, and run it on your own machines. If you are a junior engineer picking your stack right now, this is the fork in front of you: a model you can download and inspect, or a model that lives behind someone else's API.

The missing signature is a signal. I think we have seen this signal before.

## What happened in 1999

In the late nineties, Microsoft ran a campaign against open source. The arguments will sound familiar.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Microsoft spent years calling open source buggy, insecure, and dangerous. Internal memos leaked in 1998 showed the fear was strategic. The exact opposite turned out to be true.</p></div>

In August 1998, a Microsoft engineer named Vinod Valloppillil wrote an internal memo analyzing open source as a threat. Eric S. Raymond got hold of it and published it that autumn. It became known as the first Halloween document. The memo admitted, in writing, that open source software was technically competitive and that Microsoft needed a strategy against it. The fear was strategic rather than technical. The public message was different: open source was buggy, insecure, dangerous for enterprises. In 2001, Steve Ballmer called Linux a cancer.

Then reality voted. Today Linux and Apache run most of the internet. The most secure infrastructure on earth is open. And Microsoft, the company that called Linux a cancer, became one of the largest open source contributors on GitHub.

The mechanism matters more than the irony. When an incumbent warns you about a technology, ask who benefits if you believe them. In 1999 the answer was Microsoft. I think the safety argument and the business interest pointed in the same direction, and that alignment is the tell.

## The Anthropic position

Anthropic now sits in the same structural seat Microsoft sat in. Same arguments, different decade.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Anthropic argues open weights are a safety risk. Maybe they believe it. But the argument also protects their structural advantage, and that coincidence deserves scrutiny, whatever their intentions.</p></div>

I want to be fair here. Anthropic was founded as a safety company. The people there may genuinely believe that releasing weights is reckless. I don't know what is in their hearts, and I don't think it matters much for the analysis.

Look at the structure instead. Anthropic sells access to a closed model through an API. Every argument against open weights is also an argument for their business model. When safety rhetoric and revenue protection point the same way, you cannot tell them apart from the outside. I think that was true for Microsoft in 1999, and I think it is true for Anthropic now.

In Star Wars, Tarkin tells the Imperial council: the more you tighten your grip, the more star systems will slip through your fingers. He is talking about the Rebellion. He is also describing every incumbent that tried to close a technology that wanted to be open. The Empire built the Death Star to make resistance impossible. The plans leaked. They always leak.

## Why this time is harder

Here is where I have to break with my own analogy. Models can be genuinely dangerous in a way Linux never was.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>The safety argument against open weights is stronger than the one against open source ever was. That makes the analysis harder. Some of the risk is real, and the skill that matters is telling real risk apart from incumbent protection.</p></div>

Linux could crash your server. A frontier model could help someone design a pathogen. Those are different categories of risk, and pretending they are equal is lazy. In my experience, the people shouting loudest on both sides skip this part. The open weights camp says it is all FUD, all protectionism. The closed camp says it is all risk, all responsibility. Both are half right.

There is another difference. Weights are not source code. You can read Linux line by line and audit it. You cannot read a weight matrix and understand what the model will do. The many-eyes argument, the idea that open systems get debugged faster because everyone can look, works for code. For weights, the eyes see numbers rather than intentions. Distribution is also nearly free now, and the capability race has become a state concern, with export controls and chip restrictions that never applied to a web server.

So the 1999 playbook does not transfer cleanly. I think the pattern repeats, open wins most of the ground in the end, but the path is slower and the exceptions are real. Some models probably should stay closed for now. Which ones, and who decides, is the argument worth having.

## My take

The FUD campaign against open weights smells like 1999 because parts of it are 1999. Same incumbent economics, same safety vocabulary, same missing signature on the open letter.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>History says openness wins. This round says some of the caution is earned. Learn to audit arguments the way you audit dependencies: check who benefits when you believe them.</p></div>

If you are early in your career, you will build on whatever wins this fight. My take is that open weights take most of the ground, the way Linux did, and the closed labs keep the frontier for a while, the way Microsoft kept the desktop. The twist is that the safety question is real this time, so the victory will be negotiated instead of total.

When someone tells you a technology is too dangerous to be open, run the test the Halloween documents taught us. Check whether the warning and the business model point the same way. If they do, you are reading strategy dressed as concern. Sometimes concern is also true. Both things can hold at once.

The companies that called open software a cancer ended up running on it. Watch what Anthropic does in five years, not what it signs this summer.
