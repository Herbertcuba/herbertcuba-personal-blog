On July 24, 2026, [25 technology companies launched](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/24/nvidia-microsoft-meta-open-weight-ai-models.html) a letter called [Open Weights and American AI Leadership](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/corporate-responsibility/topics/open-weight/). Nvidia, Microsoft and Meta signed at launch. OpenAI joined three days later. Anthropic and Apple were still absent from Microsoft's August 3 list.

Anthropic built its identity around AI safety and sells access to Claude through a controlled service. I think its missing signature points to a familiar platform fight. Downloadable models let customers move work away from the Claude API, giving Anthropic a commercial incentive as well as a safety concern.

The warning may also be sincere. Imagine a security team choosing the model for an agent that reviews suspicious-login alerts. A hosted model can receive a safety update across future calls. A downloaded model can keep running inside the company's environment after its maker releases a safer version. That difference changes the argument.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Anthropic's absence resembles an old fight over control, but it cannot settle the safety case. Downloaded models create a recall problem that hosted services can partly avoid.</p></div>

## Microsoft left the memo behind

Microsoft documented the open-source threat in August 1998.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Microsoft privately described open source as technically credible and commercially dangerous. Its later reversal gives us a reason to inspect today's safety language beside the business model behind it.</p></div>

Microsoft program manager Vinod Valloppillil wrote [Halloween Document I](https://web.archive.org/web/20210125034937/http://www.catb.org/~esr/halloween/halloween1.html). The internal memo called open-source software a "direct, short-term revenue and platform threat" and said open projects could achieve or exceed commercial quality. It even concluded that "FUD tactics can not be used to combat it."

The public campaign hardened anyway. In June 2001, Steve Ballmer [called Linux "a cancer"](https://www.theregister.com/2001/06/02/ballmer_linux_is_a_cancer/) while arguing about the GNU General Public License.

Linux became infrastructure. [Every TOP500 supercomputer has run Linux since June 2018](https://www.top500.org/statistics/sublist/). Microsoft [bought GitHub](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2018/10/26/microsoft-completes-github-acquisition/) that year, maintains [Azure Linux](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/linux/) and now hosts the 2026 open-weights letter.

For the alert team, a local model makes Claude replaceable and weakens provider control over the model layer.

I think Microsoft saw the business threat early. Anthropic can sincerely fear misuse while benefiting from provider-controlled access.

## AI has no universal recall button

The analogy breaks when a released capability survives its maker's decision to change it.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Open weights transfer control to the operator and weaken the maker's ability to withdraw a capability. This makes Anthropic's safety argument stronger than Microsoft's case against Linux.</p></div>

Open weights are learned parameters that can be downloaded and run locally. Training code and full data may remain private, which falls short of the [Open Source AI Definition](https://opensource.org/ai/open-source-ai-definition).

With Claude, Anthropic can update future calls to the suspicious-login agent. With local weights, the security team chooses the update, and old copies may keep reviewing alerts.

In *Rogue One*, closing the archive after transmission cannot retrieve the Death Star plans already copied to the Rebel fleet. Model weights cross a similar boundary, except the copy can perform work.

[Dario Amodei's July 27 response](https://www.anthropic.com/news/position-open-weights-models) rejects a blanket ban and calls open models without dangerous capabilities a public good. He supports safety tests for every sufficiently capable model, whether open or closed. I think his concern about attacker advantage in biology is serious.

I don't know where the dangerous-capability threshold belongs. Heartbleed blocks easy faith in openness. Flawed code entered OpenSSL on [December 31, 2011](https://github.com/openssl/openssl/commit/4817504d069b4c5082161b02a22116ad75f822b1), shipped in 2012 and remained undiscovered until [April 2014](https://openssl-library.org/news/secadv/20140407.txt). Public inspection was possible, yet discovery was slow.

## Hold the alert workflow still

The suspicious-login agent gives both models one job and one test.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>Run Claude and a local model through the same saved alerts, tool limits and scoring method. A shared test turns vendor claims into evidence while exposing who owns updates and failures.</p></div>

Give both models the same masked alerts. Keep account locking behind code and human approval. Measure missed attacks, evidence leaks and cost per correct review. Record the model version, data path, update owner and stop condition.

The agent only recommends an investigation. Code and a human still control account suspension. A local model earns a production case when it clears the test and the team accepts patching and incident duty. Claude earns the same case if its safety or cost advantage justifies provider dependence.

The architecture should keep [the agent layer replaceable](/posts/who-owns-the-agent-layer/). It should also treat [reversibility as an engineering property](/posts/reversibility-is-the-new-correctness/). I think open weights will advance first in bounded workflows like suspicious-login review, where teams can measure failure and contain action.

## Safety claims need equal exposure

The signatories have commercial incentives too.

<div class="chapter-tldr"><span class="chapter-tldr__label">In short</span><p>A safety rule becomes credible when it follows capability and can constrain hosted models too. Category rules aimed mainly at downloadable rivals deserve harder scrutiny.</p></div>

OpenAI's signature complicates the simple incumbent story. Nvidia sells compute, and Microsoft sells cloud infrastructure. [Anthropic's safety posture](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropics-responsible-scaling-policy) also predates this dispute.

I think the Linux pattern will repeat with a twist. Open weights will become normal infrastructure for bounded work such as suspicious-login review, while models with demonstrated cyber or biological danger may remain controlled for longer. The release line should move with evidence.

The 1999 parallel tells us to inspect incentives, while the missing recall button explains why caution can be earned. Anthropic's position becomes credible when the same evidence can keep Claude behind the gate.
