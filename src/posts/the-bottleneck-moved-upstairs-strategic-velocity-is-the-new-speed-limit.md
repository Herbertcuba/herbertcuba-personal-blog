Earlier this year, I used agents to build a digital product in less than a day, work that would normally take weeks across several roles.

Since then, in my work with leadership teams, I see the slow part moving from building to deciding. When agents shrink execution from weeks to days, a quarterly strategy cycle becomes the constraint, and faster production without a new way of setting direction only moves a company faster in the wrong direction. The speed leaders should manage now is strategic velocity.

## The slow part moved

In the conversations around me, whether something can be built in time comes up less often than who decides whether it still fits the strategy.

A Harvard working paper on 718 firms shows the same shift one level down: coding agents raised coding activity without a significant rise in resolved work items, as code waited longer for review. That queue is code review, not strategy, but I expect the next one a floor up, where direction gets set.

## Where teams wait now

The pattern I see is a team that tests a hypothesis in a week, then waits weeks for a steering meeting to settle pricing or positioning. Meanwhile it builds more variants on old assumptions and asks managers for small go-aheads. The waiting becomes the main cost, because stale assumptions get built faster instead of tested sooner.

Leaders feel it too. The team's speed reaches their desk as a stream of small approvals that crowds out the few choices only they can make.

## Run strategy on two clocks

Deciding everything faster would trade this problem for pivot churn and rubber-stamped evidence. I would sort decisions by clock instead.

The slow clock holds intent: target segment, non-negotiables and the budget envelope. It changes rarely, and a monthly check asks only whether it still holds.

The fast clock runs inside that intent. Reversible bets are decided close to the evidence, within days, on a one-page brief with preset continue and kill triggers. Hard-to-undo choices, such as pricing, go to a short weekly forum instead of waiting for the quarter. "Reversible" has to cover contracts, customer trust and data, not only code.

None of this hurries the evidence; retention cohorts and market response take their time. What should shrink is the time a ready decision waits.

## Measure the loop

In February I wrote that velocity without direction creates chaos. Strategic velocity already appears in consulting writing, so mine is a working definition: the elapsed time from evidence to choice, coordinated action and reassessment, against an intent you hold steady.

Track that loop instead of production throughput, and repetitive approvals can become bounded autonomy. Your agents already work on a weekly clock. If strategy still runs quarterly, the slowest part of the company is the leadership calendar, and leaders can redesign that this month.
